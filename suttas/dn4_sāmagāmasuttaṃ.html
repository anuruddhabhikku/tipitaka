THUS HAVE I HEARD.

On one occasion the Blessed One was living in the Sakyan country at Samagama. Now on that occasion the Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta had just died in Pāvā. On his death the Nigaṇṭhas had split into two factions. They kept arguing, quarreling, & disputing, stabbing one another with weapons of the mouth: “You don’t understand this dhamma-vinaya. I understand this dhamma-vinaya. How could you understand this dhamma-vinaya? You’re practicing the wrong way. I’m practicing the right way. I’m being consistent. You’re inconsistent. What should have been said first you said last, what should have been said last you said first. What you took so long to think out has been confuted. Your position has been overthrown. You’re trapped, so go try to release your position or disentangle yourself if you can!”

It seemed as if there were nothing but slaughter among the Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta’s pupils. And his white-clothed lay disciples were disgusted, dismayed, and disappointed with the Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta’s pupils, as they were with his badly proclaimed and badly expounded dhamma and vinaya, which was unemancipating, unconducive to peace, expounded by one not fully enlightened, and was now with its shrine broken, left without a refuge.

42. Then Cunda the novice, who had spent the rains at Pāvā, went to where the venerable Ānanda was in Sāmagāma. Approaching him, he paid homage to the venerable Ānanda and sat down to one side.

Sitting to one side, Cunda the novice said this to the venerable Ānanda: “Bhante, Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta has recently died in Pāvā. As a result of his death, the Nigaṇṭhas have split into two factions… and now their stūpas are broken and they have no refuge.”

When this was said, the venerable Ānanda said this to Cunda the novice: “Friend Cunda, this is a matter worth reporting to the Blessed One. Let’s go, friend Cunda, to where the Blessed One is. Having gone there, we will report this matter to him.”

“Yes, Bhante,” Cunda the novice replied to the venerable Ānanda.

Then the venerable Ānanda and Cunda the novice went to where the Blessed One was. Having approached, they paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side.

Sitting to one side, the venerable Ānanda said this to the Blessed One: “Bhante, this novice Cunda says this: ‘Bhante, Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta has recently died in Pāvā. As a result of his death, the Nigaṇṭhas have split into two factions… and now their stūpas are broken and they have no refuge.’

Bhante, this is what I thought: ‘May no dispute arise in the saṃgha (community) after the Blessed One's passing. For such a dispute would be for the harm and unhappiness of many, for the detriment, harm, and suffering of gods and humans.’”

43. “What do you think, Ānanda? Those things that have been taught by me after directly knowing them, that is to say, the four foundations of mindfulness (cattāro satipaṭṭhānā), the four right exertions (cattāro sammappadhānā), the four bases of spiritual power (cattāro iddhipādā), the five faculties (pañcindriyāṇi), the five powers (pañca balāni), the seven factors of enlightenment (satta bojjhaṅgā), and the Noble Eightfold Path (ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo): do you see, Ānanda, even two bhikkhus who have differing theories about these things?”

“Venerable sir, concerning those things that have been taught by the Blessed One after directly knowing them, that is to say, the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of spiritual power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of enlightenment and the Noble Eightfold Path, I do not see even two bhikkhus who have differing theories about these things.

But, venerable sir, there are people who live deferential towards the Blessed One who might, when he has gone, create a dispute in the saṃgha about livelihood (ajjhājīve) or about the Pāṭimokkha (adhipātimokkhe).

Such a dispute would be for the harm and unhappiness of many, for the loss, harm, and suffering of gods and humans.”

“A dispute about livelihood (ajjhājīve) or about the Pāṭimokkha (adhipātimokkhe) would be trifling, Ānanda.

But should a dispute arise in the saṃgha about the path (magge) or the way (paṭipadāya), such a dispute would be for the harm and unhappiness of many, for the loss, harm, and suffering of gods and humans.

44. Ānanda, these are the six roots of disputes ( vivādamūlāni ).

What are the six? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu is angry (kodhano) and revengeful (upanāhī).

Ānanda, such a bhikkhu who is angry and revengeful, dwells disrespectful (agāravo) and undeferential (appatisso) towards the Teacher (satthari), towards the Dhamma, and towards the Sangha, and he does not fulfill the training (sikkhāya na paripūrakārī hoti).

Ānanda, such a bhikkhu who dwells disrespectful and undeferential towards the Teacher, towards the Dhamma… towards the Sangha, and who does not fulfil the training, creates a dispute (vivādaṃ janeti) in the Sangha (saṅghe), which would be for the harm (ahitāya) and unhappiness (asukhāya) of many (bahujana), for the loss (anatthāya), harm, and suffering (dukkhāya) of gods (deva) and humans (manussānaṃ).

Ānanda, now if you see any such root of dispute (vivādamūlaṃ) either in yourselves (ajjhattaṃ) or externally (bahiddhā), you should strive to abandon (pahānāya vāyameyyātha) that same evil (pāpakassa) root of dispute.

Ānanda, and if you do not see any such root of dispute either in yourselves or externally,

then you should practice (paṭipajjeyyātha) for the future non-arising (āyatiṃ anavassavāya) of that same evil root of dispute.

Thus there is the abandoning (pahānaṃ hoti) of that evil root of dispute; thus there is its future non-eruption (āyatiṃ anavassavo hoti).

Evametassa pāpakassa vivādamūlassa pahānaṃ hoti <pb ed="P" n="2.0247"/>, evametassa pāpakassa vivādamūlassa āyatiṃ anavassavo hoti. Imāni kho, ānanda, cha vivādamūlāni.

“Again, Ānanda, a bhikkhu is contemptuous and domineering… envious and avaricious… deceitful and fraudulent… has evil wishes and wrong view… adheres to his own views (sandiṭṭhiparāmāsī), holds on to them tenaciously (ādhānaggāhī), and relinquishes them with difficulty (duppaṭinissaggī).

Such a bhikkhu, Ānanda, adhering to his own views (sandiṭṭhiparāmāsī), holding on to them tenaciously (ādhānaggāhī), and relinquishing them with difficulty (duppaṭinissaggī), dwells without respect and without deference for the Teacher, for the Dhamma, and for the Saṅgha, and he does not fulfill the training.

Such a bhikkhu, Ānanda, dwelling without respect and without deference for the Teacher, for the Dhamma, and for the Saṅgha, and not fulfilling the training, creates a dispute in the Saṅgha, which would be for the harm and unhappiness of many, for the loss, harm, and suffering of gods and humans.

Ānanda, if you see any such root of dispute (vivādamūlaṃ) either in yourselves or externally, you should strive to abandon that same evil root of dispute (pāpakassa vivādamūlassa pahānāya).

Ānanda, if you do not see any such root of dispute (vivādamūlaṃ) either in yourselves or externally, you should practice in such a way that that same evil root of dispute does not erupt in the future.

Thus there is the abandoning (pahānaṃ) of that evil root of dispute (pāpakassa vivādamūlassa), thus there is the non-eruption (anavassavo) of that evil root of dispute (pāpakassa vivādamūlassa āyatiṃ) in the future.

These, Ānanda, are the six roots of dispute (cha vivādamūlāni).

<p n="46" rend="bodytext"><hi rend="paranum">46</hi><hi rend="dot">.</hi> ‘‘Cattārimāni <pb ed="M" n="3.0035"/>, ānanda, adhikaraṇāni. Katamāni cattāri? Vivādādhikaraṇaṃ, anuvādādhikaraṇaṃ, āpattādhikaraṇaṃ, kiccādhikaraṇaṃ – imāni kho, ānanda, cattāri adhikaraṇāni. “Ānanda, there are these four kinds of litigation (adhikaraṇāni). What are the four? Litigation because of a dispute (vivādādhikaraṇaṃ), litigation because of an accusation (anuvādādhikaraṇaṃ), litigation because of an offense (āpattādhikaraṇaṃ), and litigation concerning proceedings (kiccādhikaraṇaṃ) – these are the four kinds of litigation, Ānanda.

“Ānanda, there are these seven kinds of settlement of litigation (adhikaraṇasamathā). For the settlement and pacification of litigations whenever they arise: removal of litigation by confrontation (sammukhāvinayo) may be provided, removal of litigation on account of memory (sativinayo) may be provided, removal of litigation on account of past insanity (amūḷhavinayo) may be provided, the effecting of acknowledgement of an offense (paṭiññāya kāretabbaṃ), the opinion of the majority (yebhuyyasikā), the pronouncement of bad character against someone (tassapāpiyasikā), and covering over with grass (tiṇavatthārako).

<p n="47" rend="bodytext"><hi rend="paranum">47</hi><hi rend="dot">.</hi> ‘‘Kathañcānanda, sammukhāvinayo hoti? Idhānanda, bhikkhū vivadanti dhammoti vā adhammoti vā vinayoti vā avinayoti vā. Tehānanda, bhikkhūhi sabbeheva samaggehi sannipatitabbaṃ. Sannipatitvā dhammanetti samanumajjitabbā <pb ed="T" n="3.0054"/>. Dhammanettiṃ samanumajjitvā yathā tattha sameti <pb ed="V" n="3.0033"/> tathā taṃ adhikaraṇaṃ vūpasametabbaṃ. Evaṃ kho, ānanda, sammukhāvinayo hoti; evañca panidhekaccānaṃ adhikaraṇānaṃ vūpasamo hoti yadidaṃ – sammukhāvinayena.

How, Ānanda, does the sammukhāvinaya (removal of litigation by confrontation) come to be? Here, Ānanda, bhikkhus are disputing: ‘It is Dhamma (Doctrine),’ or ‘It is not Dhamma,’ or ‘It is Vinaya (Discipline),’ or ‘It is not Vinaya.’

Those bhikkhus should all meet together in concord.

Then, having met together, the guideline of the Dhamma should be examined.

Having examined the guideline of the Dhamma, that legal issue should be resolved in this way.

And so, Ānanda, there is the sammukhāvinaya; and so there comes to be the settlement of some litigations here by the sammukhāvinaya.

Tehānanda, bhikkhūhi yasmiṃ āvāse bahutarā bhikkhū so āvāso gantabbo. Tattha sabbeheva samaggehi sannipatitabbaṃ. Sannipatitvā dhammanetti samanumajjitabbā. Evaṃ kho, ānanda, yebhuyyasikā hoti, evañca panidhekaccānaṃ adhikaraṇānaṃ vūpasamo hoti yadidaṃ – yebhuyyasikāya.

How, Ānanda, does settlement by majority occur? If those monks are unable to settle that litigation in that monastery, Ānanda, those monks should go to a monastery where there are more monks. There, they should all meet together in concord. Having met together, the guideline of the dharma (dhamma) should be drawn out. Having drawn out the guideline of the dharma, that litigation should be settled in accordance with how it fits. Thus, Ānanda, does settlement by majority occur, and so there comes to be the settlement of some litigations here by the opinion of a majority.

Idhānanda, bhikkhū bhikkhuṃ evarūpāya garukāya āpattiyā codenti pārājikena vā pārājikasāmantena vā – ‘saratāyasmā evarūpiṃ <note>evarūpaṃ (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) evarūpāya-iti vuccamānavacanena sameti. vinayenapi saṃsandetabbaṃ</note> garukaṃ āpattiṃ āpajjitā pārājikaṃ vā pārājikasāmantaṃ vā’ti? 49. Now, Ānanda, how does removal of litigation on account of memory (smṛti-vinaya) come about? Here, Ānanda, one bhikṣu accuses another of a grave offense of this kind, involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājika-sāmanta), saying, 'Does the venerable one remember having committed such and such a grave offense, one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājika-sāmanta)?' If he says thus, 'No, friend (āvuso), I do not remember committing such a grave offense, one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājika-sāmanta)', then, Ānanda, removal of litigation on account of memory (smṛti-vinaya) should be granted to that bhikṣu. Such, Ānanda, is the removal of litigation on account of memory (smṛti-vinaya). And so there comes to be the settlement of some litigations here by removal of litigation on account of memory (smṛti-vinayena).

52. How, Ānanda, does the pronouncement of bad character against someone come about? Here, Ānanda, one bhikkhu reproves another bhikkhu for such and such a grave offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta): ‘Does the venerable one remember having committed such and such a grave offense, one involving defeat or bordering on defeat?’ He says: ‘I do not, friends (āvuso), remember having committed such and such a grave offense, one involving defeat or bordering on defeat.’

Despite the denial, the former presses the latter further: ‘Come on, Venerable, try harder to remember whether you’ve committed such-and-such a heavy offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta)?’

He says: ‘I do not, friends (āvuso), remember having committed such and such a grave offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta); but I do remember, friends (āvuso), having committed such and such a minor offence (appamattikaṃ āpatti)’

Despite the denial, the former presses the latter further: ‘Come on, Venerable, try harder to remember whether you’ve committed such-and-such a heavy offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta)?’ He says: ‘Indeed, friends (āvuso), if I had committed a minor offense (appamattikaṃ āpatti), even unasked I would acknowledge it.

So when asked, why shouldn’t I acknowledge having committed such and such a grave offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta)?’ He says: ‘Indeed, friend (āvuso), if you had committed a minor offense (appamattikaṃ āpatti), even unasked you would not acknowledge it, so when asked about a heavy offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta), why would you admit it?’ Come on, Venerable, try harder to remember whether you’ve committed such-and-such a heavy offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta)?’

He says: ‘I remember, friends (āvuso), having committed such and such a grave offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta).

I was hurried, I was confused, when I said that I did not remember having committed such and such a grave offense (garuka āpatti), one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmanta)’

And so, Ānanda, the pronouncement of bad character against someone comes about, and so there comes to be the settlement of some litigations here by the pronouncement of bad character against someone.

53. How, Ānanda, does the covering over with grass (tiṇavatthāraka) come about? Here, Ānanda, when bhikkhus have taken to quarreling and brawling and are deep in disputes, they may have said and done many things improper for a recluse. Those bhikkhus, Ānanda, should all meet together in concord. Then, having met together, a capable bhikkhu who is on one side (ekatopakkhikānaṃ) should rise from his seat, arrange his upper robe over one shoulder, raise his joined palms, and inform the Saṃgha:

‘Venerable sirs, may the Saṃgha hear me. When we took to quarreling and brawling and were deep in disputes, we said and did many things improper for a recluse. If it seems appropriate to the Saṃgha, then for the benefit of these venerables and myself, I’ll confess in the midst of the Saṃgha by covering over as if with grass, except for heavy offenses (thullavajja) and offenses connected with householders (gihipaṭisaṃyutta)’”.

Then, a capable bhikkhu who is on the other side of those bhikkhus should rise from his seat, arrange his upper robe over one shoulder, raise his joined palms, and inform the Saṃgha:

‘Venerable sirs, may the Saṃgha hear me. When we took to quarreling and brawling and were deep in disputes, we said and did many things improper for a recluse. If it seems appropriate to the Saṃgha, then for the benefit of these venerables and myself, I’ll confess in the midst of the Saṃgha by covering over as if with grass, except for heavy offenses and offenses connected with householders’”.

“Such is the covering over with grass, Ānanda, and so there comes to be a settling of some legal issues by covering over with grass (tiṇavatthāraka).”

54. “Ānanda, there are these six memorable qualities that create love and respect, and conduce to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

Which six? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu maintains bodily acts of loving-kindness (mettā kāyakamma) both in public and in private towards his companions in the holy life (sabrahmacārī).

This too is a quality that creates love and respect, and conduces to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

“Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu maintains verbal acts of loving-kindness (mettā vacīkamma) both in public and in private towards his companions in the holy life (sabrahmacārī).

This too is a quality that creates love and respect, and conduces to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

“Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu maintains mental acts of loving-kindness (mettā manokamma) both in public and in private towards his companions in the holy life (sabrahmacārī).

This too is a quality that creates love and respect, and conduces to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

“Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu shares without reservation with his virtuous companions in the holy life (sīlavantehi sabrahmacārīhi), any gain of a kind that accords with the Dharma and has been obtained in a way that accords with the Dharma (dhamma), including even what is in his alms bowl (pattapariyāpannamattampi).

This too is a quality that creates love and respect, and conduces to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

“Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu dwells both in public and in private possessing in common with his companions in the holy life (sabrahmacārīhi) those virtues (sīla) that are unbroken (akhaṇḍāni), untorn (acchiddāni), unblotched (asabalāni), unmottled (akammāsāni), liberating (bhujissāni), commended by the wise (viññuppasatthāni), not misapprehended (aparāmaṭṭhāni), and conducive to concentration (samādhisaṃvattanikāni).

This too is a quality that creates love and respect, and conduces to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

“Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu dwells both in public and in private possessing in common with his companions in the holy life (sabrahmacārīhi) that view (diṭṭhi) that is noble (ariyā) and emancipating (niyyānikā), and leads the one who practises in accordance with it to the complete destruction of suffering (sammā dukkhakkhayā).

This too is a quality that creates love and respect, and conduces to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

These are the six memorable qualities (sāraṇīyā dhammā) that create love and respect, and conduce to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

“If, Ānanda, you undertake and maintain these six memorable qualities (sāraṇīye dhamme), do you see any course of speech, trivial (aṇuṃ) or gross (thūlaṃ), that you could not endure (adhivāseyyātha)?”

“No, venerable sir.”

“Therefore, Ānanda, undertake and maintain these six memorable qualities (sāraṇīye dhamme).

That will lead to your welfare (hitāya) and happiness (sukhāya) for a long time (dīgharattaṃ.”

Thus spoke the Blessed One (bhagavā).

The venerable (āyasmā) Ānanda was satisfied and delighted in the Blessed One’s words.

The Sāmagāma Sutta, the fourth, is finished.
