Ekamantaṃ nisinno kho āyasmā sāriputto bhagavantaṃ etadavoca – ‘‘evaṃpasanno ahaṃ, bhante, bhagavati, na cāhu na ca bhavissati na cetarahi vijjati añño samaṇo vā brāhmaṇo vā bhagavatā bhiyyobhiññataro yadidaṃ sambodhiya’’nti.
141. Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One (bhagavā) was staying at Nāḷandā in Pāvārika's mango-grove.

Then the venerable (āyasmā) Śāriputta approached the Blessed One (bhagavā); having approached, he bowed down to the Blessed One (bhagavantaṃ) and sat down at one side.

Seated at one side, the venerable (āyasmā) Śāriputta said this to the Blessed One (bhagavantaṃ): "I have such faith, venerable sir (bhante), in the Blessed One (bhagavati) that there never has been, nor will there be, nor is there now any other ascetic (samaṇo) or Brahmin (brāhmaṇo) with greater direct knowledge than the Blessed One (bhagavatā) with regard to awakening (sambodhiya)."

141. "Thus I have heard" is the Sampasadanīya Sutta.

Here is a word-by-word explanation of it: nāḷandāya means in a city of that name, Nāḷanda, after making that city a pasture village. pāvārikambavane means in the mango grove of Dussapāvārika the millionaire. It seems that this was his park. Having heard the Blessed One's teaching, he was pleased with the Blessed One. In that park, he had a monastery built for the Blessed One, adorned with a brick hut and a pavilion. That monastery, like Jīvaka's mango grove, went by the name "Pāvārikambavana." The meaning is that he lived in that Pāvārikambavana. He said this to the Blessed One: "I am thus pleased, venerable sir, with the Blessed One." Why did he say this? To announce the pleasure that had arisen in himself.

Here is the connected story: It seems that early that day, after attending to his bodily needs, the elder, well-clothed and carrying his bowl and robe, entered for alms, inspiring faith in the pleasing (sight) of his going and so on for gods and humans, increasing the benefit and happiness of the residents of Nāḷanda. After the meal, returning from his alms round, he went to the monastery, showed the proper respect to the Teacher, and after the Teacher had entered the Perfumed Chamber, he paid homage to the Teacher and went to his day quarters. There, after showing the proper respect to his fellow residents and pupils and after they had departed, he swept the day quarters, spread out a leather mat, cooled his hands and feet with water from a water pot, folded his legs in the three-jointed lotus position, set a time limit, and attained the fruition attainment.

Having arisen from the attainment according to the time he had determined, he began to recollect his own qualities. Then, as he recollected his qualities, morality came to mind. Then, in sequence, concentration, wisdom, liberation, the knowledge and vision of liberation, the first jhāna, the second jhāna, the third jhāna, the fourth jhāna, the attainment of the base of the infinity of space, the attainment of the base of the infinity of consciousness, the attainment of the base of nothingness, the attainment of the base of neither perception nor non-perception, insight knowledge, mind-made power knowledge, the knowledge of the varieties of magical power, divine-ear knowledge, mind-reading knowledge, knowledge of the recollection of past lives, divine-eye knowledge...pe...the path of stream-entry, the fruit of stream-entry...pe...the path of arahantship, the fruit of arahantship, knowledge of the meaning, knowledge of the Dhamma, knowledge of language, knowledge of ready wit, the knowledge of a disciple's perfections. From here on, starting with the aspiration he made at the feet of the Buddha Anomadassī, more than a hundred thousand aeons ago, as he recollected his own qualities, the qualities up to the lotus posture in which he was sitting presented themselves.

In this way, as the elder recollected his own qualities, he was unable to see the measure or limit of his qualities. He thought: "Even for me, a disciple standing in the knowledge of a part (of the teaching), there is no measure or limit to my qualities. But what are the qualities of the Teacher for whom I went forth?" He began to recollect the qualities of the one with the ten powers. He began to recollect the qualities of the one with the ten powers, relying on the Blessed One's morality, relying on his concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, relying on the four establishments of mindfulness, relying on the four right exertions, the four bases of power, the four paths, the four fruits, the four kinds of special knowledge, the knowledge that distinguishes the four types of birth, and relying on the four noble lineages.

He said: "My teacher is unsurpassed in the exposition of skillful qualities, unsurpassed in the exposition of the bases, unsurpassed in entering the womb, unsurpassed in methods of instruction, unsurpassed in the attainment of vision, unsurpassed in the exposition of individuals, unsurpassed in exertion, unsurpassed in practice, unsurpassed in speaking and conduct, unsurpassed in the moral conduct of a man, unsurpassed in methods of advising, unsurpassed in the knowledge of the liberation of other individuals, unsurpassed in eternalist theories, unsurpassed in the knowledge of past lives, unsurpassed in divine-eye knowledge, unsurpassed in magical power, unsurpassed in this and in that." As he thus recollected the qualities of the one with the ten powers, he saw neither the end nor the measure of the Blessed One's qualities. If the elder did not see the end or measure of his own qualities, what would he see of the Blessed One's qualities? To the extent that someone's wisdom is great and their knowledge is clear, to that extent they have faith in the Buddha's qualities as being great. Ordinary worldly people, even after coughing or spitting, recollect the Buddhas' qualities by saying "Homage to the Buddhas," standing in their own respective grounds. A stream-enterer has more faith in the Buddhas' qualities as being great than all ordinary worldly people. 161. Ekissā lokadhātuyā means the ten-thousandfold world system.

There are three fields: the birth field ( jātikhettaṃ), the command field (āṇākhettaṃ), and the range field (visayakhettaṃ).

There, jātikhettaṃ is the name for the ten-thousandfold world system.

Indeed, it trembles when the Tathāgata enters his mother's womb, at his birth, at the time of his enlightenment, at the turning of the wheel of the Dharma, at the rejection of his life span, and at his complete passing away.

Moreover, āṇākhettaṃ is the name for a hundred thousand million world-spheres.

Here, the command (āṇā) of the Āṭānāṭiya, the Peacock Protection (moraparitta), the Banner Top Protection (dhajaggaparitta), the Jewel Protection (ratanaparitta) and others takes effect.

However, there is no limit to the visayakhettaṃ, for there is no object (avisayo) because of the Buddha's saying, "As far as there is knowledge (ñāṇaṃ), so far is there knowable (ñeyyaṃ); as far as there is knowable, so far is there knowledge; the knowable is limited by knowledge, [and] knowledge is limited by the knowable" (Mahāniddesa 55).

But in these three fields, setting aside this world-sphere, there is no sutra saying that Buddhas arise in another world-sphere, but there is [a sutra saying that] they do not arise.

The three baskets (piṭaka): the Basket of Discipline (vinayapiṭakaṃ), the Basket of Discourses (suttantapiṭakaṃ), and the Basket of Abhidhamma (abhidhammapiṭakaṃ).

The three recitations (saṅgīti): the recitation of the Elder Mahākassapa, the recitation of the Elder Yasa, and the recitation of the Elder Moggaliputtatissa.

In these three recitations, which are established in the three baskets, there is no sutra in the word of the Buddha saying, "Having released this world-sphere, Buddhas arise elsewhere," but there is [a sutra saying that] they do not arise.

Apubbaṃ acarima means that [Buddhas] do not arise together, before or after, but they arise before or after.

There, it should not be understood that [the time] from when [the Buddha] sat on the seat of enlightenment (bodhipallaṅke), [thinking,] "I will not rise until I have attained enlightenment (bodhiṃ)," until the acceptance of rebirth in his mother's womb, is before.

Indeed, at the acceptance of rebirth of the Bodhisatta, the extent of the field was made known only by the trembling of the ten-thousandfold world-sphere.

Even the arising of another Buddha is prevented.

And it should not be understood that [the time] from his complete passing away (parinibbāna) until even mustard-seed-sized relics (dhātuyo) remain is after.

Indeed, when the relics remain, even Buddhas remain.

Therefore, the arising of another Buddha is prevented in this interval.

However, the arising of another Buddha is not prevented when the relics completely pass away (dhātuparinibbāne).

Account of the Disappearance of the Three Baskets (tipiṭakaantaradhānakathā)

Tīṇi antaradhānāni are the disappearance of learning (pariyattiantaradhānaṃ), the disappearance of realization (paṭivedhaantaradhānaṃ), [and] the disappearance of practice (paṭipattiantaradhānaṃ).

There, pariyattī means the three baskets.

Paṭivedho means the realization of the truths (saccappaṭivedho).

Paṭipattī means the practice (paṭipadā).

There, realization and practice both exist and do not exist.

Indeed, at one time, there are many monks who realize [the truth], [so many that] it is necessary to extend a finger and point out, "This monk is an ordinary person (puthujjana)."

At one time on this island, there were no monks who were ordinary people.

Sometimes there are many who fulfill the practice, sometimes few.

Thus, realization and practice both exist and do not exist.

However, for the stability of the Dispensation, learning is the measure.

Indeed, the wise person, having heard the three baskets, fulfills both.

Tīṇi antaradhānāni, pariyattī, paṭivedho, paṭipattī

Just as our Bodhisatta, having produced the five superknowledges (pañcābhiññā) and the seven attainments (satta samāpattiyo) in the presence of Āḷāra, asked about the preliminary practice (parikammaṃ) of the attainment of neither perception nor non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatanasamāpattiyā), [and] he said, "I do not know."

Then, having gone to the presence of Udaka, having compared the distinction he had attained, he asked about the preliminary practice of the attainment of neither perception nor non-perception, [and] he told [him]. Immediately after his words, the great being accomplished that meditation (jhānaṃ).

And if two, four, eight, or sixteen were to arise together, they would not be extraordinary.

For in one monastery, even for two caityas, the gain and honor is not abundant.

Monks are also not born as extraordinary in great numbers, so too would Buddhas be; therefore they do not arise.

And because of the absence of distinction in the teaching.

For when one teaches the Dharma that is distinct in the smṛtyupasthānas, etc.

Even having arisen by another, he would have to be taught that way, therefore he would be unextraordinary.

But when one teaches the Dharma, the teaching is also extraordinary, and because of the presence of debate.

For when many Buddhas have arisen, the students of many teachers would dispute, saying, “Our Buddha is pleasing, our Buddha has a sweet voice, he is a gainer and virtuous.”

Therefore, they do not thus arise.

And this reason has also been extensively explained by the Elder Nāgasena, questioned by King Milinda.

For it was said there:

“Venerable Nāgasena, this was also spoken by the Blessed One: ‘This is impossible, monks, there is no opportunity, that in one world-element two arhats, completely awakened ones, should arise unarisen and not final; such a thing is not possible.’”

And when they are teaching, venerable Nāgasena, all tathāgatas teach the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, and when they are speaking, they speak the Four Noble Truths, and when they are training, they train in the three trainings, and when they are instructing, they instruct in the practice of diligence.

If, venerable Nāgasena, all the tathāgatas have one teaching, one talk, one training, one instruction, by what reason do two tathāgatas not arise at the same moment?

Even by the arising of just one Buddha, this world has become light-born; if there were a second Buddha, by the light of the two, this world would become even more light-born; and when two tathāgatas are exhorting, they could easily exhort, and when instructing, they could easily instruct, tell me the reason for this, so that I may be without doubt.”

This, great king, is a ten-thousandfold world system, the sustainer of one Buddha, it sustains the qualities of only one tathāgata, if a second Buddha were to arise, this ten-thousandfold world system would not sustain [it], it would shake, tremble, bend, incline, turn away, scatter, dissolve, shatter, it would not reach a place of rest.
As, great king, if there were a boat capable of carrying one man, and when one man had embarked, that boat were full, then a second man should come, like the first in age, complexion, stature, and size, with all his limbs and members, and should embark on that boat, would that boat, great king, bear the weight of both? No, revered sir. It would shake, tremble, bend, lean over, incline, scatter, break up, be shattered, and would not reach its place, but would sink in the water.

Even so, great king, this ten-thousandfold world-system is the sustainer of one Buddha, it sustains the special qualities of one tathāgata only. If a second Buddha were to arise, this ten-thousandfold world-system could not bear him...it would not reach its place.

Or again, great king, it is as if a man should eat his fill of food, filling himself right up to his throat, and being satisfied, replete, full, without interruption, made languid, no longer standing erect, should again eat just as much food, would that man, great king, be happy? No, revered sir, but having eaten once, he would die. Even so, great king, this ten-thousandfold world-system is the sustainer of one Buddha.

What now, revered Nāgasena, is it because of the excessive burden of the dharma that the earth trembles? Here, great king, if there were two carts filled with jewels right up to their brims, and taking the jewels from one cart, they were to heap them into the other, would that cart, great king, bear the jewels of both carts? No, revered sir, the nave of its wheel would break, the spokes of it would be shattered, the felly of it would give way, the axle of it would be broken.

What now, great king, is it because of the excessive burden of jewels that the cart breaks? Yes, revered sir.

Even so, great king, it is because of the excessive burden of the dharma that the earth trembles.

Moreover, great king, this reason has been brought forward to illustrate the power of a Buddha. Hear another, still more excellent, reason why two perfectly awakened Buddhas do not arise at the same time.

If, great king, two perfectly awakened Buddhas were to arise at the same moment, a dispute would arise in their assemblies, [some saying,] "Our Buddha!" [others,] "Our Buddha!" and partisanships would arise on both sides.

As, great king, if a dispute were to arise in the assemblies of two powerful ministers, [some saying,] "Your minister!" [others,] "Our minister!" and partisanships would arise on both sides; even so, great king, if two perfectly awakened Buddhas were to arise at the same moment, a dispute would arise in their assemblies, [some saying,] "Your Buddha!" [others,] "Our Buddha!" and partisanships would arise on both sides. This, great king, is the first reason why two perfectly awakened Buddhas do not arise at the same moment.

And again, great king, hear a further reason why two perfectly awakened Buddhas do not arise at the same moment.

If, great king, two perfectly awakened Buddhas were to arise at the same moment, that saying, "The foremost Buddha," would be false, [as would also the sayings,] "The eldest Buddha," "The best Buddha," "The distinguished Buddha," "The supreme Buddha," "The excellent Buddha," "The matchless Buddha," "The unequalled Buddha," "The peerless Buddha," "The unrivalled Buddha," "The individual without compare". That saying would be false.

Do you also, great king, rightly accept this reason as to why two perfectly awakened Buddhas do not arise at the same moment.

Moreover, great king, this is the natural order of Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, that one Buddha alone arises in the world.

For what reason? Because of the greatness of the qualities of the all-knowing Buddha. Whatever else, great king, is great, that is only one.

The earth, great king, is mighty, and it is only one.

The ocean is mighty, and it is only one.

Sineru, king of mountains, is mighty, and it is only one.

Space is mighty, and it is only one.

Sakka is mighty, and he is only one.

Māra is mighty, and he is only one.

Mahā-Brahmā is mighty, and he is only one.

The tathāgata, arhat, perfectly enlightened Buddha, is mighty, and he is alone in the world.

Where they arise, there is no opportunity for others.

Therefore, great king, the tathāgata, arhat, perfectly enlightened Buddha, one alone arises in the world.
