Durabhisambhavāni hi kho, brāhmaṇa, araññavanapatthāni pantāni <pb ed="T" n="1.0029"/> senāsanāni, dukkaraṃ pavivekaṃ, durabhiramaṃ ekatte, haranti maññe mano vanāni samādhiṃ alabhamānassa bhikkhuno’’ti.

Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was staying at Sāvatthī, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

Then the brahmin Jāṇussoṇi went to the Blessed One and exchanged greetings with him (saddhiṃ sammodi). When this courteous and amiable talk was finished, he sat down at one side. Seated at one side, the brahmin Jāṇussoṇi said to the Blessed One: “Master Gotama, when clansmen have gone forth from the home life into homelessness out of faith in Master Gotama, do they have Master Gotama for their leader (pubbaṅgamo), their helper (bahukāro), and their guide (samādapetā)? And do these people follow the example of Master Gotama (diṭṭhānugatiṃ āpajjati)?”

“That is so, brahmin, that is so, brahmin! When clansmen have gone forth from the home life into homelessness out of faith in me (mamaṃ uddissa saddhā agārasmā anagāriyaṃ pabbajitā), they have me for their leader (ahaṃ tesaṃ pubbaṅgamo), their helper (ahaṃ tesaṃ bahukāro), and their guide (ahaṃ tesaṃ samādapetā). And these people follow my example (mama ca pana sā janatā diṭṭhānugatiṃ āpajjati).”

“But, Master Gotama, remote jungle-thicket resting places in the forest (araññavanapatthāni pantāni senāsanāni) are hard to endure, seclusion (pavivekaṃ) is hard to practise, and it is hard to enjoy solitude (ekatte). One would think the jungles must rob a bhikkhu of his mind, if he has no samādhi.”

“That is so, brahmin, that is so, brahmin! Remote jungle-thicket resting places in the forest (araññavanapatthāni pantāni senāsanāni) are hard to endure, seclusion (pavivekaṃ) is hard to practise, and it is hard to enjoy solitude (ekatte). One would think the jungles must rob a bhikkhu of his mind, if he has no samādhi.”

Thus have I heard, the bhayabheravasūtra.

Here is an explanation of the unfamiliar words: athā is a particle ( nipāta) used to indicate connection.

Kho indicates certainty. bhagavato sāvatthiyaṃ vihāre avicchinneyevāti vuttaṃ hoti means it was said at the vihāra in Śrāvastī in the presence of the Blessed One without interruption.

Jāṇussoṇī is not a name given by his parents, but one obtained later as a result of a change in position.

The position of Jāṇussoṇī, it is said, was the position of the family priest (purohita), given to him by the king. Therefore, he is called "Jāṇussoṇī."

brahmaṃ aṇatīti brāhmaṇo, a brāhmaṇa is one who recites the mantras, this is the meaning.

This is the definitive etymology of the brāhmaṇas by birth.

The noble ones (ariyā) are called brāhmaṇas because they have cast off evil (bāhitapāpattā brāhmaṇā ti vuccanti).

Because the Blessed One approached him, yena, the instrumental case, has a locative sense. Therefore, "wherever the Blessed One was, there he approached," this is how the meaning should be understood.

For what reason did the Blessed One approach? Due to the intention of obtaining various kinds of excellent qualities (nānappakāraguṇavisesādhigamādhippāyena), due to the intention of enjoying delicious fruits (sāduphalūpabhogādhippāyena), like a great tree constantly bearing fruit with flocks of birds (dijagaṇehi niccaphalitamahārukkho viya).

Because the Blessed One approached, yena...

The meaning of upasaṅkamī is that he went.

Upasaṅkamitvā indicates the conclusion of approaching.

Or, having gone thus, it also means having gone to a place nearer to the Blessed One, called proximity.

bhagavatā saddhiṃ sammodī means that just as the Blessed One asked about what was tolerable, and so on, so he too was completely delighted with the Blessed One; like cold water mixed with warm water, he attained oneness.

And because he was pleased by the words, "Is it well with you, Gotama? Are you able to subsist? Are the disciples of the Blessed Gotama free from disease, free from affliction, quick to rise, strong, and living comfortably?" that is sammodanīyaṃ, fit to be rejoiced at, because it generates joy and delight (pītipāmojjasaṅkhātasammodajananato sammodituṃ yuttabhāvato ca sammodanīyaṃ), and sārāṇīyaṃ, fit to be remembered because of the sweetness of its meaning and expression, to continue it uninterruptedly for a long time, and because of its nature as something worth remembering (atthabyañjanamadhuratāya sucirampi kālaṃ sāretuṃ nirantaraṃ pavattetuṃ araharūpato saritabbabhāvato ca sārāṇīyaṃ).

It is sammodanīyaṃ because of the pleasure of being heard, and sāraṇīyaṃ because of the pleasure of being remembered.

Likewise, it is sammodanīyaṃ because of the purity of expression, and sāraṇīyaṃ because of the purity of meaning. Thus, in many ways it is sammodanīyaṃ. Having completely finished considering how it is sāraṇīyaṃ, having brought it to a conclusion, and having completed it, desiring to ask for what purpose he had come, he sat down on one side.

He approached, having approached, he rejoiced with the Blessed One; sammodanīyaṃ, sārāṇīyaṃ.

Ekamanta is a neuter designation of contemplation; like "the uneven moon and sun revolve" etc.

Therefore, just as one sits having sat down on one side, so he sat down. This is how the meaning here should be understood.

Or, this is an accusative expression in the locative sense.

Nisīdī means he sat down.

For wise men, having approached someone worthy of respect and being skilled in seating arrangements, sit down on one side; and this was one of them, therefore he sat down on one side.

He sat down on one side...

How does one sit, having sat down on one side?

Having avoided six faults of sitting.

For example, too far, too near, exposed to the wind, on a raised place, too directly in front, and too far behind.

For one sitting too far away, if he wants to speak, he must speak loudly.

One sitting too close causes crowding.
Sitting in a windy place, [he] obstructs [others] with the smell of his body.

Sitting in an elevated place, [he] shows disrespect.

Sitting too close, if [he] wants to look, [he] should look by striking eye with eye.

Sitting too far back, if [he] wants to look, [he] should look by stretching his neck.

Therefore, he also avoided these six faults of sitting and sat down; therefore, it was said, "Sit down to one side."

yeme means ye ime.

kulaputtā means young men of good families, there are two kinds of young men of good families: those by birth (jātikulaputtā) and those by conduct (ācārakulaputtā).

There, "Now at that time a clansman named Raṭṭhapāla, the son of the leading clan in that same Thullakoṭṭhika" (MN 2.294), thus, those who come from high-class families are called jātikulaputtā.

"Those clansmen who have gone forth from the household life into homelessness out of faith" (MN 3.78), thus, those who come from any family but are accomplished in conduct are called ācārakulaputtā.

Here, however, [they are] young men of good families by both reasons.

yeme kulaputtā jātikulaputtā ācārakulaputtā

saddhā means by faith.

agārasmā means from home.

anagāriya means going forth and the state of being a bhikkhu.

For going forth is anagāriyā, as there is no home here, meaning there is no occupation such as agriculture or cattle-raising that benefits the home.

A bhikkhu is also anagāro, as there is no home for him; the state of being anagāro is anagāriya.

pabbajitā means having approached; thus, in every way, it is said that [they] have approached going forth or the state of being a bhikkhu, which is called anagāriya.

pubbaṅgamo means going ahead, a leader.

bahukāro means very helpful in doing good deeds.

bhavaṃ tesaṃ gotamo samādapetā means the esteemed Gotama instructs and trains those young men of good families in higher morality and so on.

sā janatā means that group of people.

44. lābhasakkārasilokanti (regarding gain, honor, and fame): Here, lābho (gain) means that which is obtained; this is a designation for the four requisites.

sakkāroti (honor) means beautiful actions; the requisites themselves, when carefully and beautifully prepared, are called honor. And also the honoring action towards oneself by others, worship with flowers, etc., is pūjā (worship).

nikāmayamānāti (desiring) means patthayamānā (longing for).

[It is] just like the frightening and terrifying invitation and the fence of covetousness.

Regarding this, they narrate the story of Piyagāmika, which illuminates the meaning:

It seems that a certain monk named Piyagāmika, seeing the gain of monks who had undertaken the dhutaṅga practices, thinking, "I, too, will undertake the dhutaṅga practices and generate gain," undertook the sosānikaṅga (dwelling in a charnel ground) and lived in a charnel ground.

Then one day, an old ox, released from work, having wandered in the pasture during the day, spent the night in that charnel ground near a bush of flowers, chewing its cud.

Piyagāmika, having gone out for walking meditation at night, hearing the sound of its jaw, thought, "Surely, the Devarāja (divine king), knowing that I am greedy for gain and living in this charnel ground, has come to torment me." So, placing his palms together in reverence before the old ox, he pleaded, "O noble Devarāja, forgive me for this one night; from tomorrow on, I will not do this again," spending the entire night begging.

Then, when the sun rose, seeing it, he struck it with a bamboo stick and chased it away, [saying], "It frightened me the whole night!"

54. “When his mind is thus concentrated”: insight-producing fourth dhyāna-mind should be understood.

"For the knowledge of the destruction of the cankers": for the knowledge of the path of the arhat.

The path of arhat is called the destruction of the cankers because it destroys the cankers; here, this is knowledge, thus it is related to that.

"He directs [his] mind": he directs and inclines [his] mind to insight.

"He knew this suffering": in this way, he fully knew, understood, and penetrated the entire truth of suffering with its own characteristic mode, [thinking,] "This is the extent of suffering, there is no more than this."

And the craving that produces this suffering [is what he refers to when he says,] "This is the origin of suffering".

That state by attaining which both cease, that is nirvāṇa, the non-occurrence of those [formations,] [thus, he declares,] "This is the cessation of suffering."

He fully knew, understood, and penetrated the noble path that brings about its attainment, with its own characteristic mode, [declaring,] "This is the path leading to the cessation of suffering." This is how the meaning should be understood.

Thus, having shown the truths in their own form, now, showing [them] in terms of defilements, by way of a paraphrase, he says, "These are the cankers," and so forth.

"When I knew thus, when I saw thus": when I knew and saw thus.

He teaches the path that has reached its peak together with insight.

"Kāmāsava": because of the canker of sensual desire.

"Vimuccitthā": by this, he shows the characteristic of fruition, for the mind is freed at the moment of the path, [and] in the moment of fruition, it is liberated.

"Vimuttasmiṃ vimuttamiti ñāṇa": by this, he shows the knowledge of reviewing.

"Khīṇā jātī": By these, [he indicates] its ground; because the Blessed One reviews with that knowledge, [saying,] "khīṇā jātī," and so forth.

But which birth of the Blessed One is destroyed, and how did he understand it? It is not the past birth that is destroyed, because it was already destroyed before; nor the future, because there is no effort in the future; nor the present, because it exists.

But that birth which would arise from the non-cultivation of the path—the birth that is a division of the one, four, or five aggregates in the realms of existence of one, four, or five constituents—that, through the cultivation of the path, by attaining the state of non-arising, is destroyed. Having reviewed those abandoned defilements through the cultivation of the path, knowing that "even though karma exists in the absence of defilements, it does not lead to rebirth in the future," he understood.

These are the cankers. When I knew thus, when I saw thus, the canker of sensual desire, he was liberated. In the liberated, [there is] knowledge that is liberated. Destroyed is birth.

"Vusitaṃ": lived, fully lived, done, practiced, completed, is the meaning.

"Brahmacariyaṃ": the brahmacarya of the path; for along with the auspicious ordinary person, the seven learners live the monastic life, it is said, the one with destroyed cankers has lived the monastic life.

Therefore, the Blessed One, reviewing his own monastic life, understood, "The monastic life has been lived."

"Kataṃ karaṇīyaṃ": the task has been done. By means of the four paths in the four truths, the sixteenfold task has been completed by way of all-round knowing, abandoning, realizing, and cultivating, is the meaning.

Ordinary auspicious people and the like perform that task; the one with destroyed cankers has done what was to be done.

Therefore, the Blessed One, reviewing his own task, understood, "What was to be done has been done."

Lived. Monastic life. Task completed.

"Nāparaṃ itthattāyā": now, for the sake of this state, for the sake of this sixteenfold task, or for the destruction of defilements, there is no task of cultivating the path for me, he understood.

Or, "itthattāyā": from this state, from this kind, now, from the present continuum of aggregates, there is no other continuum of aggregates for me.

But these five aggregates, having been fully known, stand like trees with severed roots.

Through the cessation of the final consciousness, without fuel, like Jātavedas, they will be extinguished, he understood.

No more. For this state. For this state.

Now, showing the attainment of the knowledge of the destruction of the cankers, as it is taken up by the knowledge of reviewing in this way, to the brāhmaṇa, he says, "This, O brāhmaṇa," and so forth.

There, "vijjā": knowledge is the knowledge of the path of arhat.

"Avijjā": ignorance is the ignorance that conceals the four truths.

The rest is as aforesaid.
And thus, with the knowledge of former abodes, [he has] knowledge of the past; with divine vision, [he has] knowledge of the present and future; and with the destruction of defilements (āsava), all worldly and supramundane qualities (guṇa). Thus, with these three kinds of knowledge (vidjā), having gathered all the qualities of omniscience, he showed the brahmin his own state of non-delusion.

This, O brahmins, is my knowledge, ignorance. The description of the discourse on the knowledge of the destruction of defilements (āsava) is finished.

55. When this was said, the Brahmin thought: “The ascetic Gotama claims to be all-knowing, and even now he does not abandon living in the forest. Is there something else for him to do?”

Then the Blessed One, knowing his thought, and by means of tracing his train of thought, said, beginning with, "It may occur to you."

There, "It may occur to you, Brahmin, that it is like this," means, Brahmin, it might occur to you in this way.

"But it should not be regarded thus, Brahmin," means, Brahmin, you should not regard my resort to remote lodgings as being due to non-abandonment of lust, etc.

Thus, rejecting the non-reason for resorting to remote lodgings, and showing the reason, he said, beginning with, "There are two reasons."

There, the meaning itself is the reason.

Therefore, "There are two reasons, Brahmin," means, Brahmin, I see two meanings, two reasons.

"For pleasant abiding in the present life," here, "present life" means this immediate existence.

"Pleasant abiding" means the ease of all four modes of conduct; for in the forest, even attending to the needs of excretion, all modes of conduct are easy; therefore, this meaning should be understood: pleasant abiding in the present life.

"Having compassion for future generations," how is it that future generations are treated with compassion by living in the forest? For sons of good families who have gone forth in faith, seeing the Blessed One living in the forest, will think: "The Blessed One does not abandon remote forest dwellings, even though there is nothing for him to fully know, nothing to abandon, nothing to develop, nothing to realize. How much more so should we?" And they will think that they should live there.

Thus, they will quickly put an end to suffering.

In this way, future generations are treated with compassion.

Showing this meaning, he said, "Having compassion for future generations."

The explanation of the reason for living in the forest is finished.

56. Having heard that, the delighted brāhmaṇa spoke, beginning with, “having a compassionate form” (anukampitarūpā).

Therein, “having a compassionate form” (anukampitarūpā) [means:] having a compassionate nature (anukampitajātikā), having a compassionate disposition (anukampitasabhāvā).

“Populace” (janatā) [means:] a group of people (janasamūho).

“Just as that Arhat, the Perfectly Awakened One…” (yathā taṃ arahatā sammāsambuddhenā) [means:] Just as an Arhat, a Perfectly Awakened Buddha, might have compassion (anukampeyya), just so is [the meaning of] “having a compassionate form” (anukampitarūpāti).

Having said this, and again appreciating that Dharma teaching of the Blessed One, he said this to the Blessed One: “Excellent, dear Gotama! Excellent, dear Gotama!” (abhikkantaṃ, bho gotama, abhikkantaṃ, bho gotamā).

Here, this word “excellent” (abhikkantasaddo) is seen in the senses of exhaustion, beautiful, handsome, appreciation.

In passages such as, “The night is far spent, venerable sir (bhante); the first watch is over; the community of monks has been sitting for a long time,” (abhikkantā, bhante, ratti, nikkhanto paṭhamo yāmo, ciranisinno bhikkhusaṅgho) etc. (Cūḷava. 383; A. Ni. 8.20), it is seen in [the sense of] exhaustion (khaye).

In passages such as, “This one is more excellent and more refined than these four persons” (ayaṃ imesaṃ catunnaṃ puggalānaṃ abhikkantataro ca paṇītataro cā) etc. (A. Ni. 4.100), [it is used in the sense of] handsome (sundare).

Excellent, dear Gotama! Excellent, dear Gotama! (abhikkantaṃ, bho gotama, abhikkantaṃ, bho gotamā)

“Who <pb ed="m" n="1. 0134"/> bows to my feet with the power, glory, and the water;

<pb ed="m" n="1. 0134"/> with excellent color (abhikkantena vaṇṇena) illuminating all directions?”

In passages such as these (Vi. Va. 857), [it means] beautiful (abhirūpe).

In passages such as, “Excellent, venerable sir (bhante)” (abhikkantaṃ, bhante) etc. (Dī. Ni. 1.250; Pārā. 15), [it indicates] appreciation (abbhanumodane) <pb ed="v" n="1. 0138"/>.

Here too, it is just appreciation (abbhanumodaneyeva).

Since it is appreciation (abbhanumodane), therefore, the statement, “Good, good, dear Gotama” (sādhu sādhu bho, gotamāti vuttaṃ hotīti veditabbaṃ) should be understood.

<pb ed="v" n="1. 0138"/> “In fear, anger, praise, haste, excitement;

In laughter, sorrow, serenity (prasāde), the wise one does it repeatedly.”

By this characteristic, here, under the influence of serenity (pasādavasena) and through praise (pasaṃsāvasena), this is said twice; thus it should be understood.

Or <pb ed="p" n="1. 0130"/> “excellent” (abhikkanta) means especially lovely (abhikantaṃ).

[That is,] extremely desirable (atiiṭṭhaṃ), extremely pleasing (atimanāpaṃ), extremely good (atisundaranti vuttaṃ hoti).

<pb ed="p" n="1. 0130"/> Excellent (abhikkanta)

There, with one utterance of “excellent” (abhikkantasaddena), he praises the teaching (desanaṃ thometi), with another, his own serenity (attano prasādaṃ).

This is the significance here: “Excellent, dear Gotama, is this Dharma teaching of yours (yadidaṃ bhoto gotamassa dhammadesanā); excellent, having come to this Dharma teaching of yours (bhoto gotamassa dhammadesanaṃ āgamma) is my serenity (mama pasādoti).”

Or rather, the Blessed One praises the utterance in two ways, thinking, “The utterance of dear Gotama is excellent (abhikkantaṃ) in that it dispels faults (dosanāsanato), excellent in that it leads to the attainment of good qualities (guṇādhigamanato), and likewise in that it generates faith (saddhājananato), generates wisdom (paññājananato), is meaningful (sātthato), is well-expressed (sabyañjanato), has clear wording (uttānapadato), has profound meaning (gambhīratthato), is pleasing to the ear (kaṇṇasukhato), reaches the heart (hadayaṅgamato), does not elevate oneself (anattukkaṃsanato), does not disparage others (aparavambhanato), is cool with compassion (karuṇāsītalato), is pure with wisdom (paññāvadātato), is delightful to behold (āpātharamaṇīyato), is capable of being examined (vimaddakkhamato), is pleasant to hear (suyyamānasukhato), is beneficial when investigated (vīmaṃsīyamānahitatoti),” thus it should be connected with these etc. (evamādīhi yojetabbaṃ).
Furthermore, he praises the teaching itself with four similes.

There, nikkujjita means placed face down, or born with the face down. Ukkujjeyya means he would make the face upward. Paṭicchannanti means covered with grass, leaves, etc. Vivareyya means he would uncover it. Mūḷhassa means to one lost in the directions. Maggaṃ ācikkheyyā means after having seized the men by his hand one should say “This is the path”. Andhakāre means in a fourfold darkness with dense thickets of forests, cloud banks, on the fourteenth night of the dark fortnight, this, for the moment, is the meaning of the words without elaboration.

This, however, is the interpretation of the significance:–According as some one would upturn what had been turned with its face downwares, in this way, by making me who had fallen into heresy which constitutes the turning away from the excellent truth, rise up from the heresy ; according as he would open out what had been covered and concealed.

in this way, by the opening of the sāsana which had been covered and concealed by the jungle of wrong views beginning from the disappearance of dispensation of the Exalted Buddha Kassapa; Just as if one were to point out the right path to one who was lost, so when I was travelling along a false path, a wrong path, the Exalted One disclosed to me the path to heaven and to liberation. And just as if one were to bring a lamp into a dark place, so when I was submerged in the darkness of delusion, not seeing the forms of the Buddha and other jewels, because the Dhamma has been revealed in many ways by the Exalted Gotama to me through the lamp of teaching that destroys the darkness of delusion that covers them.

The description of rejoicing at the teaching is finished.

Description of the aspect of serene faith

The going for refuge (saraṇagamana) is an act of consciousness (cittuppāda) that, through confidence (pasāda) in and reverence (taggarutā) for the Triple Gem (ratanattaya), is devoid of defilements (vihatakilesa) and occurs in the mode (ākāra) of regarding (pavatta) the Triple Gem as the supreme resort (tapparāyaṇatā).
A being with complete parts (taṃsamaṅgisatta) goes for refuge; by the act of consciousness of the kind that has been described (vuttappakāra), [he thinks:] "These three refuges are my refuge, these are my supreme resort" – thus he approaches it, this is the meaning.
Thus, first, saraṇa (refuge), saraṇagamana (going for refuge), and whoever goes for refuge – this triad should be understood.

Saraṇa (refuge), saraṇagamana (going for refuge), and whoever goes for refuge.
In the division of going for refuge, however, there are two kinds of going for refuge: the supramundane (lokuttara) and the mundane (lokiya).
Therein, the supramundane going for refuge is achieved at the moment of the path (maggakkhaṇa) by those who have seen the truths (diṭṭhasaccānaṃ), through the eradication of the defilements of the going for refuge (saraṇagamanupakkilesasamuccheda); by way of its object (ārammaṇato), it takes nirvāṇa (nibbāna) as its object (ārammaṇaṃ), and by way of its task (kiccato), it succeeds (ijjhāti) in regard to the entire Triple Gem (sakalepi ratanattaye).
The mundane refuge (lokiya) of ordinary people (puthujjana) is achieved through the temporary repression (vikkhambhana) of the blemishes attaching to their going for refuge (saraṇagamanupakkilesa); by way of its object (ārammaṇato), its object is the qualities of the Buddha and so forth (buddhādiguṇārammaṇaṃ); that is, in reality (atthato), it consists in the acquisition of faith (saddhāpaṭilābha) in the objects of the Buddha and so forth (buddhādīsu vatthūsu); and Right View (sammādiṭṭhi) that is rooted in faith (saddhāmūlikā ca) is called straightening of views (diṭṭhijukammanti) among the ten bases of meritorious action (dasasu puññakiriyavatthūsu).

In the division of going for refuge <pb ed="v" n="1.
0141"/> this triad occurs in four ways (catudhā pavattati): by self-surrender (attasanniyyātanena), by regarding [the Triple Gem] as the supreme resort (tapparāyaṇatāya), by accepting discipleship (sissabhāvūpagamanena), and by reverence (paṇipātenāti).
Therein, self-surrender (attasanniyyātanena) means: "From today onward, I give myself to the Buddha, to the Dharma, to the Saṅgha" – thus, [there is] the relinquishing of self to the Buddha and so forth (buddhādīnaṃ attapariccajanaṃ).
Regarding [the Triple Gem] as the supreme resort (tapparāyaṇatā) means: "From today onward, I am one who regards the Buddha as the supreme resort, the Dharma as the supreme resort, the Saṅgha as the supreme resort; thus, may you hold me" – thus [there is] the state of regarding [the Triple Gem] as the supreme resort (tapparāyaṇabhāvo).
Accepting discipleship (sissabhāvūpagamanaṃ) means: "From today onward, I am a follower of the Buddha, of the Dharma, of the Saṅgha; thus, may you hold me" – thus, [there is] the acceptance of discipleship (sissabhāvūpagamo).
Reverence (paṇipāto) means: "From today onward, I will perform respectful salutation (abhivādana), rising in welcome (paccuṭṭhāna), [making] añjali (añjali), and proper service (sāmīcikamma) to the Buddha and so forth, to those very three objects; thus, may you hold me" – thus, [there is] the utmost reverence (paramanipaccakāro) towards the Buddha and so forth.

Now, this [taking refuge] is fourfold, based on kinship, fear, respect for the teacher, or spiritual worthiness. Of these, going for refuge occurs through homage by prostration based on spiritual worthiness, not through the others. For refuge is taken only in relation to what is superior, and it is broken in relation to what is superior. Therefore, if a Śākya or a Koliya venerates [the Buddha], thinking, "The Buddha is our relative," the refuge is not taken. Or if one venerates [the Buddha] out of fear, thinking, "The ascetic Gotama is honored by kings and is of great power; if I do not venerate him, he might do me harm," the refuge is not taken. Or if, remembering something learned from the Blessed One during the time of [his] Bodhisattva-career or during the time [after he became a Buddha]—

"With one part one should enjoy possessions, with two parts one should apply oneself to work, and the fourth part one should set aside as a reserve for times of distress" (Digha Nikaya 3.265)—

having learned such an instruction, one venerates [the teacher], thinking, "He is my teacher," the refuge is not taken. But if one venerates [the Buddha], thinking, "This one is the foremost worthy of offerings in the world," the refuge is taken.

Thus, if a male or female lay follower who has taken refuge venerates even a renunciant belonging to another sect, thinking, "This is my relative," the going for refuge is not broken; how much less when the person is not a renunciant! Likewise, when one venerates a king out of fear, [thinking,] "Since he is honored by the country, if he is not venerated, he might do harm," [the refuge is not broken]. Likewise, the going for refuge is not broken even if one venerates a sectarian teacher from whom one has learned some craft, thinking, "This is my teacher." Thus, the distinction in going for refuge should be understood.

Here, for the supramundane going for refuge, the four fruits of asceticism are the result-fruit, and the destruction of all suffering is the benefit-fruit. For it was said:

"Whoever has gone for refuge to the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, sees with right wisdom the four noble truths: suffering, the arising of suffering, and the transcending of suffering, and the noble eightfold path leading to the stilling of suffering. In that case, the mundane going for refuge is defiled by ignorance (aññāṇa), doubt (saṃsaya), misunderstanding (micchāñāṇa), etc., concerning the three objects of faith; thus it does not become very luminous (mahājutikaṃ) and pervasive (mahāvipphāraṃ). But there is no defilement of the supramundane going for refuge.

The breach of the mundane going for refuge is of two kinds, blameworthy (sāvajjo) and blameless (anavajjo). The blameworthy comes about when one surrenders oneself, etc., to another teacher, etc.; this has undesirable fruits (aniṭṭhaphalo). The blameless occurs when one dies; because this does not produce karmic results it is without fruit (aphala). But there is no breach of the supramundane going for refuge. Even in another existence a holy disciple (ariyasāvako) will not turn to another teacher. Thus, the defilement (saṃkileso) and breach (bhedo) of going for refuge should be understood.

Exposition on the story of the rules for a lay follower.

In this context, what is a lay follower (upāsaka)? Why is he called a lay follower (upāsaka)? What is his virtue (śīla)? What is his livelihood (ājīva)? What is his failure (vipatti)? What is his success (sampatti)? This assorted material should be understood.
Therein, who is a lay follower? Anyone who is a householder who has gone for the three refuges.

Thus it is said: “Mahánáma, when a lay follower has gone for refuge to the Buddha, has gone for refuge to the Dhamma, and has gone for refuge to the Saògha, to this extent, Mahanama, one is a lay follower” (Saṃyutta Nikāya 5.1033).

Who is a lay follower? Why is he called a lay follower? Because of drawing near to the Triple Gem (ratanattayassa upāsanato). For he draws near to the Buddha, thus he is a lay follower; he draws near to the Dhamma and the Saògha, thus he is a lay follower.

Why is he called a lay follower?

What is his morality (śīla)? The five abstinences (pañca veramaṇiyo). As it is said: “Mahánáma, when a lay follower has refrained from killing living beings (pāṇātipātā paṭivirato hoti), has refrained from taking what is not given (adinnādānā), has refrained from sexual misconduct (kāmesu micchācārā), has refrained from false speech (musāvādā), [and] has refrained from liquor, intoxicants, [and] the basis for carelessness (surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhānā paṭivirato hoti), to this extent, Mahanama, a lay follower is virtuous (sīlavā hotī)” (Saṃyutta Nikāya 5.1033).

What is his livelihood (ājīvo)? Having abandoned the five wrong trades (pañca micchāvaṇijjā pahāya), he earns his living righteously, in accordance with the Dhamma (dhammena samena jīvitakappanaṃ).

For this is said: “Bhikkhus, these five trades should not be engaged in by a lay follower (upāsakena akaraṇīyā). What are the five? Trading in weapons (satthavaṇijjā), trading in beings (sattavaṇijjā), trading in meat (maṃsavaṇijjā), trading in intoxicants (majjavaṇijjā), and trading in poisons (visavaṇijjā). These are the five trades which should not be engaged in by a lay follower” (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177).

What is his livelihood?

What is his failure (vipattī)? The failure of that very morality (sīlassa) and livelihood (ājīvassa) is his failure. Further, that which makes him an outcast (caṇḍālo), stained (malañca), and repulsive (patikuṭṭho), that too is to be understood as his failure. These are in essence the five qualities, such as lack of faith (assaddhiyādayo pañca dhammā). As it is said: “Bhikkhus, possessed of five qualities, a lay follower is an outcast lay follower (upāsakacaṇḍālo), a stained lay follower (upāsakamalañca), and a repulsive lay follower (upāsakapatikuṭṭho ca). What are the five? He lacks faith (assaddho hoti); he is immoral (dussīlo hoti); he believes in superstitious omens (kotūhalamaṅgaliko hoti); he relies on omens (maṅgalaṃ pacceti), not on kamma (no kammaṃ); he seeks spiritually worthy persons (dakkhiṇeyyaṃ) outside of here (ito ca bahiddhā) and he shows honor there first (tattha ca pubbakāraṃ karoti)” (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.175).

What is his failure?

What is his success (sampattī)? Success in his moral discipline (sīlasampadā) and success in his livelihood (ājīvasampadā) is his success. So too are the five qualities, such as faith (saddhādayo pañca dhammā), etc., which make him a gem. As it is said: “Bhikkhus, possessed of five qualities, a lay follower is a gem-like lay follower (upāsakaratanañca), a red lotus-like lay follower (upāsakapadumañca), and a white lotus-like lay follower (upāsakapuṇḍarīkañca). What are the five? He has faith (saddho hoti); he is morally disciplined (sīlavā hoti); he does not believe in superstitious omens (na kotūhalamaṅgaliko hoti); he relies on kamma (kammaṃ pacceti), not on omens (no maṅgalaṃ); he does not seek spiritually worthy persons (dakkhiṇeyyaṃ) outside of here (na ito bahiddhā) and he shows honor here first (idha ca pubbakāraṃ karoti)” (Aṅguttara Nikāya).
