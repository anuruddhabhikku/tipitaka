Thus have I heard:

On one occasion the Blessed One (bhagavā) was staying at Sāvatthi in the Jetavana, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery. There the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying, "Monks!" "Venerable sir," they replied. The Blessed One said this:

"These three are the characteristics, signs and distinguishing features of a fool (bāla). What three? Here, a fool thinks bad thoughts ( duccintitacintī), speaks bad words (dubbhāsitabhāsī), and does bad deeds (dukkaṭakammakārī). If, monks, a fool did not think bad thoughts, speak bad words, and do bad deeds, then why would the wise recognize him as: ‘This person is a fool, an untrue man’? But because, monks, a fool thinks bad thoughts, speaks bad words and does bad deeds, therefore the wise know him as: ‘This person is a fool, an untrue man.’

"That fool, monks, experiences pain (dukkha) and grief (domanassa) in three ways, here and now.

If, monks, a fool is sitting in an assembly (sabhāyaṃ) or along a street (rathikāya) or in a square (siṅghāṭake), and people there are discussing certain pertinent and relevant matters, he thinks:

If, monks, a fool is one who kills living beings (pāṇātipātī), takes what is not given (adinnādāyī), misconducts himself in sensual pleasures (kāmesumicchācārī), speaks falsehood (musāvādī), and indulges in wine (surā), liquor (meraya), and intoxicants (majja), which are the basis of negligence (pamādaṭṭhāyī), he thinks: ‘These people are discussing certain pertinent and relevant matters; these things are found in me, and I am seen engaging in those things.’

This, monks, is the first kind of pain and grief that a fool experiences here and now."

Duccintitacintīti cintayanto abhijjhābyāpādamicchādassanavasena duccintitameva cinteti. Dubbhāsitabhāsīti bhāsamānopi musāvādādibhedaṃ dubbhāsitameva bhāsati. Dukkaṭakammakārīti karontopi pāṇātipātādivasena dukkaṭakammameva karoti. Tatra ceti yattha nisinno, tassaṃ parisati. Tajjaṃ tassāruppanti tajjātikaṃ tadanucchavikaṃ, pañcannaṃ verānaṃ diṭṭhadhammakasamparāyikaādīnavappaṭisaṃyuttanti adhippāyo. Tatrāti tāya kathāya kacchamānāya. 246. "Thus have I heard," concerning the Bālapaṇḍitasutta.

There, bālalakkhaṇāni means the characteristics of a fool, by which a fool is known or understood.

Bālanimittāni means the signs of a fool, those very things that are the cause of his understanding.

Bālāpadānāni means the deeds of a fool.

Duccintitacintī means thinking only evil thoughts in terms of covetousness, ill-will, and wrong views.

Dubbhāsitabhāsī means speaking only evil speech, such as lies and so forth.

Dukkaṭakammakārī means doing only evil deeds in terms of killing living beings and so forth.

Tatra ce means in that assembly where he is sitting.

Tajjaṃ tassāruppa means befitting him, suitable for him, connected with the dangers of the five kinds of hatred, visible here and now, and related to future lives; this is the meaning.

Tatrā means in that story that is being discussed.

Bāla etc., are words used in the sense of ownership.

Again, when a fool, O monks, sees kings arresting a robber who is a culprit and inflicting various kinds of punishments on him—flogging him with whips (kasāhipi tāḷente), beating him with canes (vettehipi tāḷente), beating him with half-clubs (addhadaṇḍakehipi tāḷente), cutting off his hands (hatthampi chindante), cutting off his feet (pādampi chindante), cutting off his hands and feet (hatthapādampi chindante), cutting off his ears (kaṇṇampi chindante), cutting off his nose (nāsampi chindante), cutting off his ears and nose (kaṇṇanāsampi chindante), inflicting the "porridge pot" punishment (bilaṅgathālikampi karonte), inflicting the "polished-shell shave" punishment (saṅkhamuṇḍikampi karonte), inflicting the "Rāhu's mouth" punishment (rāhumukhampi karonte), inflicting the "fiery wreath" punishment (jotimālikampi karonte), inflicting the "flaming hand" punishment (hatthapajjotikampi karonte), inflicting the "blades of grass" punishment (erakavattikampi karonte), inflicting the "bark dress" punishment (cīrakavāsikampi karonte), inflicting the "antelope" punishment (eṇeyyakampi karonte), inflicting the "hooked-flesh" punishment (baḷisamaṃsikampi karonte), inflicting the "penny" punishment (kahāpaṇikampi karonte), inflicting the khārāpatacchika punishment, inflicting the palighaparivattika punishment, inflicting the palālapīṭhaka punishment, drenching him with hot oil, setting dogs on him to eat him, impaling him alive, and cutting off his head with a sword (asināpi sīsaṃ chindante)—then, O monks, the fool thinks thus: ‘Because of such evil actions (pāpakānaṃ kammānaṃ hetu) as these, kings arrest a robber who is a culprit and inflict various kinds of punishments on him—flogging him with whips…cutting off his head with a sword (asināpi sīsaṃ chindanti); such things exist in me, and I am to be found among such things. If the kings should recognize me (maṃ cepi rājāno jāneyyuṃ), the kings would arrest me and inflict various kinds of punishments on me—they would flog me with whips…impale me alive, and cut off my head with a sword (asināpi sīsaṃ chindeyyu’nti).’ This, O monks, is the second kind of pain and grief that a fool experiences here and now (dutiyaṃ diṭṭheva dhamme dukkhaṃ domanassaṃ paṭisaṃvedeti).

248. ‘‘Puna caparaṃ, bhikkhave, bālaṃ pīṭhasamārūḷhaṃ vā mañcasamārūḷhaṃ vā chamāyaṃ chamāya (sī. pī.) vā semānaṃ, yānissa pubbe pāpakāni kammāni katāni kāyena duccaritāni vācāya duccaritāni manasā duccaritāni tānissa tamhi samaye olambanti ajjholambanti abhippalambanti. Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, mahataṃ pabbatakūṭānaṃ chāyā sāyanhasamayaṃ pathaviyā olambanti ajjholambanti abhippalambanti; evameva kho, bhikkhave, bālaṃ pīṭhasamārūḷhaṃ vā mañcasamārūḷhaṃ vā chamāyaṃ vā semānaṃ, yānissa pubbe pāpakāni kammāni katāni kāyena duccaritāni vācāya duccaritāni manasā duccaritāni tānissa tamhi samaye olambanti ajjholambanti abhippalambanti. Tatra, bhikkhave, bālassa evaṃ hoti – ‘akataṃ vata me kalyāṇaṃ, akataṃ kusalaṃ, akataṃ bhīruttāṇaṃ; kataṃ pāpaṃ, kataṃ luddaṃ, kataṃ kibbisaṃ. Yāvatā, bho, akatakalyāṇānaṃ akatakusalānaṃ akatabhīruttāṇānaṃ katapāpānaṃ kataluddānaṃ katakibbisānaṃ gati taṃ gatiṃ pecca gacchāmī’ti. So socati kilamati paridevati urattāḷiṃ kandati sammohaṃ āpajjati. Idampi, bhikkhave, bālo tatiyaṃ diṭṭheva dhamme dukkhaṃ domanassaṃ paṭisaṃvedeti. ‘‘Sa kho so, bhikkhave, bālo kāyena duccaritaṃ caritvā vācāya duccaritaṃ caritvā manasā duccaritaṃ caritvā kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā apāyaṃ duggatiṃ vinipātaṃ nirayaṃ upapajjati. Yaṃ kho taṃ, bhikkhave, sammā vadamāno vadeyya – ‘ekantaṃ aniṭṭhaṃ ekantaṃ akantaṃ ekantaṃ amanāpa’nti, nirayameva taṃ sammā vadamāno vadeyya – ‘ekantaṃ aniṭṭhaṃ ekantaṃ akantaṃ ekantaṃ amanāpa’nti. Yāvañcidaṃ, bhikkhave, upamāpi upamāhipi (sī.) na sukarā yāva dukkhā nirayā’’ti. upamāhipi (sī.)
“Again, monks, when a fool is sitting on a chair ( pīṭhasamārūḷhaṃ ) or lying on a couch ( mañcasamārūḷhaṃ ) or resting on the ground, the evil actions ( pāpakāni kammāni ) done by him in the past, the misconduct ( duccaritāni ) of body ( kāyena ), speech ( vācāya ), and mind ( manasā ), cover him, overspread him, and envelop him.

Just as the shadow of a great mountain peak in the evening covers, overspreads, and envelops the earth, so too, monks, when a fool is sitting on a chair or lying on a couch or resting on the ground, the evil actions done by him in the past, the misconduct of body, speech, and mind, cover him, overspread him, and envelop him.

There, monks, the fool thinks, ‘Indeed, I have not done good ( kalyāṇaṃ ), I have not done wholesome deeds ( kusalaṃ ), I have not made myself a shelter from anguish ( bhīruttāṇaṃ ); I have done evil ( pāpaṃ ), I have done what is cruel ( luddaṃ ), I have done what is wicked ( kibbisaṃ ).’

‘When I pass away, I shall go to the destination ( gatiṃ ) of those who have not done what is good, have not done what is wholesome, have not made themselves a shelter from anguish, have done evil, have done what is cruel, have done what is wicked.’

He sorrows, grieves, and laments, he weeps beating his breast and becomes distraught.

This too, monks, is the third kind of pain ( dukkhaṃ ) and grief ( domanassaṃ ) that a fool feels here and now.

“Furthermore, monks, that fool, having practiced misconduct of body ( kāyena duccaritaṃ caritvā ), having practiced misconduct of speech ( vācāya duccaritaṃ caritvā ), having practiced misconduct of mind ( manasā duccaritaṃ caritvā ), upon the dissolution of the body ( kāyassa bhedā ), after death ( maraṇā ), is reborn in a state of deprivation ( apāyaṃ ), in an unhappy destination ( duggatiṃ ), in perdition ( vinipātaṃ ), even in hell ( nirayaṃ upapajjati ).

“Monks, were one rightly speaking to say of anything: ‘That is utterly unwished for ( aniṭṭhaṃ ), utterly undesired ( akantaṃ ), utterly disagreeable ( amanāpaṃ ),’ one would be rightly speaking of hell ( nirayameva ) when one says: ‘That is utterly unwished for, utterly undesired, utterly disagreeable.’

“Monks, it is so hard to find a simile ( upamāpi na sukarā ) for the suffering ( dukkhā ) in hell ( nirayā ).”

249. Having spoken thus, a certain bhikkhu said this to the Blessed One: “Is it possible, venerable sir, to make a simile?” “It is possible, bhikkhu,” the Blessed One said.

“Suppose, bhikkhu, men were to arrest a robber culprit and present him to the king, saying: ‘Sire, here is a robber culprit. Order what punishment you wish for him.’

Then the king would say: ‘Go, men, strike this man in the morning with a hundred spears.’

And they would strike him in the morning with a hundred spears.

Then the king would say at noon: ‘Sirs, how is that man?’ “ ‘Sire, he is still alive.’

Then the king would say: ‘Go, men, strike that man at noon with a hundred spears.’

And they would strike him at noon with a hundred spears.

Then the king would say in the evening: ‘Sirs, how is that man?’ ‘Sire, he is still alive.’

Then the king would say: ‘Go, men, strike that man in the evening with a hundred spears.’

And they would strike him in the evening with a hundred spears.

What do you think, bhikkhus: if that man were struck with three hundred spears, would he experience pain and grief because of that?” “Venerable sir, that man would experience pain and grief because of being struck with even one spear, let alone three hundred.”

256. "sīsaṃ nhātassā" means of one who has bathed his head with fragrant water.
"uposathikassā" means of one who has undertaken the uposatha vows.
"uparipāsādavaragatassā" means of one who, having gone to the upper terrace of the palace, having eaten a fine meal, having entered the auspicious chamber on the great terrace above the palace, reflects on moral conduct.

Then, it is said that in the morning the king, having disbursed a hundred thousand, having given a great gift, and again having bathed his head with sixteen water-jars filled with scented water, having completed the morning meal, having arranged the pure upper robe over one shoulder, having ascended to the auspicious bedchamber of the upper palace, sits having crossed his legs, reflecting on the accumulation of merit arising from his generosity. This is the custom of all wheel-turning monarchs.

Then, because of the aforementioned accumulation of merit from good deeds, for those reflecting on this, the divine wheel-jewel (cakkaratana) appears, as if cleaving the surface of the eastern ocean, resembling a sapphire saṅghāṭa, as if adorning the sky.
Because it is endowed with divine power, it is called "divine".
"sahassāraṃ" [means] it has a thousand spokes.
"sanemikaṃ sanābhikaṃ" means with the felly (nemi) and with the nave (nābhi).
"sabbākāraparipūraṃ" means complete in all aspects.

The divine thousand-spoked, with felly and nave, complete in every way:
There, because the wheel (cakka) is a jewel (ratana) by its nature of producing delight (ratijanana), it is called the "wheel-jewel" (cakkaratana).
That nave (nābhi) which is called "with nave" (sanābhika) is made of indanīla jewel.
In its center is a channel (panāḷi) made of silver (rajatamayā), which shines as if smiling with a row of pure, smooth teeth.
In the middle, with a circle like a moon disc, on both the inner and outer sides, there is a pattern (parikkhepo) made with a silver band (rajatapaṭṭena).
In those channels of the nave, the demarcation lines (paricchedalekhā) at the joined places of the surrounding bands are very clearly visible.
This is the completeness of the nave in every aspect.

That wheel-jewel (cakkaratana):
Those spokes (ara) which are called "thousand-spoked" (sahassāra) are made of seven jewels (sattaratanamayā), and are radiant with splendor like rays of the sun.
The ghaṭamaṇika demarcation lines etc. on them are also very clearly visible.
This is the completeness of the spokes in every aspect.

That rim (nemi) which is called "with a rim" (sanemika) is made of very red, pure, smooth coral (pavāḷamaya), as if mocking the splendor of a cluster of young sun rays (bālasūriyarasmikalāpasiriṃ).
In its joints (sandhīsu), there are very clearly visible bands (paṭṭā) of twilight hue (sañjhārāga), possessing splendor (sassirika), and round delineation lines (vaṭṭaparicchedalekhā).
This is the completeness of the rim in every aspect.

On the surface of the wheel-band (nemimaṇḍalapiṭṭhiyaṃ), between each group of ten spokes (arānaṃ antare), there is a coral rod (pavāḷadaṇḍo) with a hollow interior (antosusiro), marked with perforated circles (chiddamaṇḍalacitto), like a windpipe (dhamanavaṃso), which is receptive to the wind (vātagāhī), and when struck by the wind (vātena paharitassa), the sound (saddo) is melodious (vaggu), delightful (rajanīyo), and pleasing (kamanīyo), like a well-tuned five-piece orchestra (pañcaṅgikatūriyassa viya).
Moreover, above that coral rod (pavāḷadaṇḍassa upari), there is a white umbrella (setacchattaṃ), and on both sides (ubhosu passesu) are rows of gathered flower garlands (samosaritakusumadāmapantiyo). Thus, that circumference of the rim (nemiparikkhepassa), adorned with hundreds of coral rods (pavāḷadaṇḍasatena) bearing hundreds of white umbrellas (setacchattasatadhārinā), surrounded by pairs of rows of gathered flower garlands (samosaritakusumadāmapantisatadvayaparivārena), has within the two channels of the nave (dvinnampi nābhipanāḷīnaṃ anto) two lion faces (dve sīhamukhāni), from which hang two pearl clusters (muttakalāpā olambanti), equal in measure to a palm tree trunk (tālakkhandhappamāṇā), possessing the splendor (sassirikā) of a collection of full-moon rays (puṇṇacandakiraṇakalāpasassirikā), with ends like red woolen balls equal to a young sun (taruṇaravisamānarattakambalageṇḍukapariyantā), as if surpassing the beauty of the course of the ākāśa gaṅgā (sky river), and when the three wheels are turned together in the sky, they appear as if turning together (samparivattamānehi tīṇi cakkāni ekato parivattantāni viya khāyanti).
This is its completeness in every way (sabbaso sabbākāraparipūratā).

Then, this being complete in all aspects (evaṃ sabbākāraparipūraṃ), in accordance with its nature (pakatiyā), having eaten the evening meal (sāyamāsabhattaṃ bhuñjitvā), having sat down on the appointed seats (paññattāsanesu) at their own house doors (attano attano gharadvāre nisīditvā), while people were engaged in continuous conversation (pravattakathāsallāpesu manussesu), and children were playing in the crossroads of the streets (vīthicatukkādīsu kīḷamāne dārakajane), it comes toward the capital (rājadhāniabhimukhaṃ āgacchati), as if adorning the sky region (ākāsappadesena upasobhayamānaṃ viya) with the tops of the tree branches (rukkhasākhaggāni) not too high and not too low (nātiuccena nātinīcena), close to the tops of the forest groves (vanasaṇḍamatthakāsannena), making the ears of beings to be filled with a sweet sound (madhurassarena sattānaṃ sotāni odhāpayamānaṃ) that is heard from twelve leagues away (dvādasayojanato paṭṭhāya suyyamānena), attracting the eyes with a color (vaṇṇena nayanāni samākaḍḍhantaṃ) that is brilliant with the rising of various splendors (nānappabhāsamudayasamujjalena) from a league away (yojanato paṭṭhāya), proclaiming the power of merit (puññānubhāvaṃ ugghosayantaṃ viya) of the wheel-turning king (rañño cakkavattissa).

Then, merely at the sound of that wheel-jewel (atha tassa cakkaratanassa saddassavaneneva), those people (tesaṃ manussānaṃ) whose hearts were turned (āvajjitahadayānaṃ), looking in the eastern direction (puratthimadisaṃ olokayamānānaṃ), thinking, "From where now, whose now, is this sound?" (kuto nu kho, kassa nu kho ayaṃ saddo'ti) one of them said this to another (aññataro aññataraṃ evamāha) – "Look, sir, a marvel! (passa bho acchariyaṃ) This full moon (ayaṃ puṇṇacando) formerly rose alone (pubbe eko uggacchati), but today (ajja pana) another one has risen with it (attadutiyo uggato). Indeed, this pair of full moons (etañhi puṇṇacandamithunaṃ), like a pair of royal swans (rājahaṃsamithunaṃ viya), flies through the surface of the sky in sequence (pubbāpariyena gaganatalaṃ abhilaṅghatī)."
Another one said, "What are you talking about, friend? Where have you ever seen two full moons rising together? Surely this is the sun, with its stream of golden rays and variegated colors!"

Another one, smiling, said thus: "Are you crazy? Just now the sun has set. How can it rise again, following this full moon? Surely this is a celestial mansion (vimāna) of a meritorious being, shining with the light of many jewels."

All of them, rejecting [the previous opinions], said thus: "Why do you babble so much? This is neither the full moon, nor the sun, nor a divine celestial mansion.

[Since] they do not have such splendor and wealth, it must be the wheel treasure (cakraratana)!"

Thus, while that crowd was engaged in conversation, the moon-like circle disappeared, and that wheel treasure (cakraratana) appeared before that group of people.

Then, when they said, "To whom does this belong?", [some] speakers said, "It belongs to no one else. Surely, our king is fulfilling the duties of a wheel-turning monarch (cakravartin); it belongs to him!"

Then, that great crowd, and whoever else saw [it], all followed the wheel treasure (cakkaratana).

That wheel treasure (cakraratana) too, as if wishing to make known its arrival for the sake of the king, having circled the city seven times on the ramparts themselves, having circumambulated the king's inner palace (antepura) clockwise, stood in a place near the northern lion-window (uttarasīhapañjara) of the inner palace (antepura), where it was easy to honor it with fragrant flowers and other offerings. Woman-treasure:

Dassanīyā [lovely] means that when seen, she pleases the eyes; therefore, she should be looked at even after abandoning other distracting tasks.

Pāsādikā [delightful] means that when seen, she gladdens the mind by way of joy.

Paramāya [supreme] means superior because she is the bringer of delight; excellent.

Vaṇṇapokkharatāyā [endowed with the beauty of a lotus] means beautiful in color.

Samannāgatā [possessed of] means endowed.

Abhirūpā [beautiful] because she is neither too tall nor too short; dassanīyā [lovely] because she is neither too thin nor too stout; pāsādikā [delightful] because she is neither too swarthy nor exceedingly fair.

Abhirūpā [beautiful], dassanīyā [lovely], pāsādikā [delightful].

"Like a tuft of cotton or a tuft of wool": the touch of her body is like that of cotton that has been placed in curdled ghee and then struck a hundred times, or like that of wool that has been struck a hundred times.

Pariṇāyakaratanavaṇṇanā (Explanation of the advisor-jewel)

Thus, for the king, the wheel-turner, who has the householder-jewel that has appeared, an advisor-jewel (pariṇāyaka-ratanaṃ) appears, who is capable of managing all affairs. He is like the eldest son of the king. By nature, he is wise, clever, and intelligent, and relying on the king's meritorious power, knowledge of others' minds arises in him due to his own karmic power. By this, knowing the conduct of the minds of the royal assembly within twelve yojanas, he is capable of determining what is harmful and beneficial to the king. He, too, seeing that power of his, with a delighted heart, satisfies the king by instructing him in all matters.

Therefore, it was said, "Again...pe...the advisor-jewel appears."

259. "Which are the four kinds of success? Here, monks, a Wheel-turning Monarch is handsome (abhirūpa), comely (dassanīya), and graceful (pāsādika), possessing the supreme beauty of complexion (paramāya vaṇṇapokkharatāya), and he surpasses other human beings (manussehi) in that respect. This is the first kind of success (paṭhamāya iddhiyā) that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“Again (puna caparaṃ), monks, a Wheel-turning Monarch is long-lived (dīghāyuko) and enduring (ciraṭṭhitiko), and he surpasses other human beings in that respect. This is the second kind of success (dutiyāya iddhiyā) that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“Again (puna caparaṃ), monks, a Wheel-turning Monarch is free from illness (appābādho) and affliction (appātaṅko), possessing a good digestion (samavepākiniyā gahaṇiyā) that is neither too cool (nātisītāya) nor too warm (nāccuṇhāya), and he surpasses other human beings in that respect. This is the third kind of success (tatiyāya iddhiyā) that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“Again (puna caparaṃ), monks, a Wheel-turning Monarch is dear (piyo) and agreeable (manāpo) to brāhmaṇas and householders (gahapatis). Just as (seyyathāpi), monks, a father is dear (piyo) and agreeable (manāpo) to his children (puttā), so too (evameva kho), monks, a Wheel-turning Monarch is dear (piyo) and agreeable (manāpo) to brāhmaṇas and householders (gahapatis). Brāhmaṇas and householders (gahapatis) too, are dear (piyā) and agreeable (manāpā) to a Wheel-turning Monarch (cakkavattissa). Just as (seyyathāpi), monks, children (puttā) are dear (piyā) and agreeable (manāpā) to a father (pitu), so too (evameva kho), monks, brāhmaṇas and householders (gahapatis) are dear (piyā) and agreeable (manāpā) to a Wheel-turning Monarch (cakkavattissa).

“Once (bhūtapubbaṃ), monks, a Wheel-turning Monarch was driving in a pleasure park (uyyānabhūmiṃ) with his four-constituent army (caturaṅginiyā senāya). Then (atha kho), monks, brāhmaṇas and householders (gahapatis) went to the Wheel-turning Monarch (rājānaṃ cakkavattiṃ), and spoke thus (evamāhaṃsu): ‘Sire (deva), drive slowly (ataramāno), that we (mayaṃ) may see (passeyyāma) you (taṃ) longer (cirataraṃ).’ And so the Wheel-turning Monarch (rājāpi cakkavattī), monks, told his charioteer (sārathiṃ āmanteti): ‘Charioteer (sārathi), drive slowly (ataramāno), that the brāhmaṇas and householders (gahapatis) may see (passeyyuṃ) me (maṃ) longer (cirataraṃ).’ This is the fourth kind of success (catutthāya iddhiyā) that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses. A Wheel-turning Monarch (rājā cakkavattī), monks, possesses these four kinds of success (imāhi catūhi iddhīhi).

“What do you think (taṃ kiṃ maññatha), monks? Would a Wheel-turning Monarch (rājā cakkavattī), because of possessing these seven treasures (imehi sattahi ratanehi) and these four kinds of success (imāhi catūhi ca iddhīhi), experience pleasure (sukhaṃ) and joy (somanassaṃ)?” “Venerable sir (bhante), a Wheel-turning Monarch (rājā cakkavattī) would experience pleasure (sukhaṃ) and joy (somanassaṃ) because of possessing even one treasure (ekamekenapi ratanena), let alone seven treasures (sattahi ratanehi) and four kinds of success (catūhi ca iddhīhi)!”

260. Then, taking a small stone the size of his hand, the Blessed One addressed the bhikkhus thus: “What do you think, bhikkhus? Which is the greater, this small stone that I have taken, the size of my hand, or Himavat, the king of mountains?”

“Venerable sir, the small stone that the Blessed One has taken, the size of his hand, does not count beside Himavat, the king of mountains; it is not even a fraction, there is no comparison.”

“So too, bhikkhus, the pleasure and joy that a Wheel-turning Monarch (cakravartin) would experience because of possessing the seven treasures (saptaratna) and the four kinds of success does not count beside the happiness of heaven; it is not even a fraction, there is no comparison.

“If, some time or other, at the end of a long period, the wise man (paṇḍita) comes back to the human state, it is into a high family that he is reborn - into a family of well-to-do nobles (khattiyamahāsālakula), or well-to-do brāhmaṇas (brāhmaṇamahāsālakula), or well-to-do householders (gahapatimahāsālakula) - one that is rich,-of great wealth, of great possessions, with abundant gold (jātarūpa) and silver (rajata), with abundant assets and means, and with abundant money and grain.

He is handsome, comely and graceful, possessing the supreme beauty of complexion. He obtains food and drink, clothes, vehicles, garlands, scents and unguents, bed, lodging, and light.

He conducts himself well in body (kāyena), speech (vācāya), and mind (manasā).

Having conducted himself well in body, speech, and mind, on the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (maraṇā), he reappears in a happy destination (sugati), even in heaven (svarga), the world (loka).

“Bhikkhus, suppose a gambler at the very first lucky throw won a great fortune, yet a lucky throw such as that is negligible."

it is a far more lucky throw when a wise man (paṇḍita) who conducts himself well in body (kāyena), speech (vācāya), and mind (manasā), on the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (maraṇā), reappears in a happy destination (sugati), even in heaven (svarga), the world (loka).

This is the complete perfection of the wise man’s grade.”
