Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was staying at Savatthi in the Jetavana at Anathapindika’s monastery.

There the Fortunate One addressed the monks, saying: "Bhikkhus."

"Venerable sir," those bhikkhus replied to the Fortunate One.

The Blessed One said this:

“Be my heirs in the Dhamma, bhikkhus, not my heirs in material things ( āmiṣadāyādā ). Out of compassion for you I have thought: ‘How shall my disciples be my heirs in the Dhamma, not my heirs in material things?’

If you are my heirs in material things, bhikkhus, not my heirs in the Dhamma, you will be reproached thus: ‘The Teacher’s disciples live as his heirs in material things, not as heirs in the Dhamma’; and I will be reproached thus: ‘The Teacher’s disciples live as his heirs in material things, not as his heirs in the Dhamma.’

If you are my heirs in the Dhamma, bhikkhus, not my heirs in material things, you will not be reproached as it will be said: ‘The Teacher’s disciples live as his heirs in the Dhamma, not as his heirs in material things’; and I will not be reproached as it will be said: ‘The Teacher’s disciples live as his heirs in the Dhamma, not as his heirs in material things.’

Therefore, bhikkhus, be my heirs in the dharma, not my heirs in material things. Out of compassion for you I have thought: ‘How shall my disciples be my heirs in the Dhamma, not my heirs in material things?’

Thus have I heard: the dhammadāyādasuttaṃ.

Because in this case there is an occasion for [telling] the story of the origin [of this sutta], therefore, having shown that, we will make a [systematic] explanation of the series of words.

In which case did this origin occur?

In [connection with] gains and honors.

It seems that the Blessed One had a great accumulation of gains and honors.

Just as those four incalculable ones [accumulated merit] by fulfilling the perfection of giving.

Having risen up in all directions, like a great and paired cloud, and thinking "We will give a result in one lifetime to all perfections," and as if they were massed together, they produced a great flood of gains and honors.

Then, from here and there, nobles, Brahmins, and others, with their hands full of food, drink, vehicles, clothing, garlands, perfumes, ointments, etc., came and searched for the Blessed One, [saying] – “Where is the Buddha? Where is the Blessed One? Where is the god of gods, the best of men, the lion among men?”

Having brought hundreds of cartloads of requisites, and not getting space, they stood all around, even for the distance of a gāvuta, jostling cart-pole against cart-pole, and kept following along.

Like the Andhakavinda Brahmins and others.

Everything should be understood according to the method that has come down in the khandhakas and in those suttas.

Just as [it was] for the Blessed One, so also for the Saṅgha of monks.

Furthermore, this was said: "At that time, the Blessed One was respected, honored, esteemed, venerated, and revered; he obtained robes, alms-food, lodging, and medicine as the requisites for the sick. The Saṅgha of monks was also respected…pe…requisites" (Udā. 14).

Similarly – "To the extent that now, Cunda, a Saṅgha or group has arisen in the world, I do not see any other Saṅgha, Cunda, that has attained such a peak of gains, status, and fame as this Saṅgha of monks" (Dī. Ni. 3.176).

This accumulation of gains and honors that arose for the Blessed One and for the Saṅgha of monks, having become one, was immeasurable, like the water of two great rivers.

Gradually, the monks became attached to requisites and indulged in them.

Even after the meal, when oil, honey, molasses, etc., were brought, they would strike the gong and shout, "Give [it] to our teacher! Give [it] to our preceptor!"

And that behavior of theirs was evident even to the Blessed One.

Then the Blessed One, having aroused spiritual agitation [by saying] "This is not fitting," thought –

"It is not possible to establish a training rule [saying] that requisites are impermissible.

For the monastic life of those of good family is bound up with requisites.

Come now, let me teach the practice of being heirs to the Dharma.

That will be like the establishment of a training rule for those of good family who desire training, and like a mirror for viewing the whole body placed at the city gate. Just as, when a mirror for viewing the whole body is placed at the city gate, people of the four castes see their reflection, abandon faults, and become blameless, just so, those of good family who desire training, wishing to adorn themselves with the adornment of effort, having turned their attention to this teaching that is like a mirror for viewing the whole body, having avoided the practice of being heirs to material things, and fulfilling the practice of being heirs to the Dharma, will quickly make an end to birth, aging, and death."

This sutta was spoken on this occasion.

Therefore, “Be heirs to my Dharma, O monks; do not be heirs to material things”: Be heirs to my Dharma, O monks; do not be [heirs] to material things.

Whoever is an acceptor of my Dharma, be that; and whatever, indeed, is my material [support], do not be acceptors of that.

Therein, the Dharma is of two kinds – Dharma that is not circuitous, and Dharma that is circuitous.

Material things are also of two kinds – material things that are not circuitous, and material things that are circuitous.
How so? Indeed, although the supramundane dharma, distinguished by the path, the fruit, and nirvāṇa, is ninefold, it is a nippariyāyadhamma, a dharma that is brought about, not a dharma [arrived at] by any means, reason, or pretext.

But whatever merit is dependent on turning away [from the cycle of rebirth], for example, here someone, desiring to turn away, gives alms, undertakes precepts, performs the uposatha practice, makes offerings of objects with garlands of incense, etc., listens to and teaches the dharma, brings about meditative attainments; doing so, gradually he obtains the nippariyāyadhamma, the deathless, nirvāṇa: this is pariyāyadhamma.

Likewise, the four requisites, such as robes, are truly only nippariyāyāmisa, not material [goods] by any other means, reason, or pretext.

But whatever merit involves the round-trip, for example, here someone, desiring the round, wishing for fortunate existence, gives alms…brings about meditative attainments; doing so, gradually he obtains the good fortune of gods and humans: this is called pariyāyāmisa.

"Be my heirs in the dharma, monks, not heirs in material things!"

There, the nippariyāyadhamma also belongs to the Blessed One alone. For, having been spoken by the Blessed One, monks attain the path, the fruit, and nirvāṇa. And it has been said, "For the Blessed One, brahmin, is the producer of the unarisen path, the engenderer of the ungenerated path, the declarer of the undeclared path; he is the knower of the path, the finder of the path, the one skilled in the path. And now his disciples abide following the path and afterwards become endowed with it" (M.iii.79), and – "For knowing, friend, the Blessed One knows; seeing, he sees; he is vision, he is knowledge, he is the dharma, he is the holy one; he is the speaker, the proclaimer, the elucidator of meaning, the giver of the deathless, the lord of the dharma, the Tathāgata" (M.i.203).

Previously, monks did not obtain excellent almsfood. They were those who ate only morsels of food collected on alms round. Because the Blessed One, dwelling in Rajagaha, had permitted – "I allow you, monks, communal meals, designated meals, invitational meals, ticket meals, fortnightly meals, uposatha meals, and first-day meals" (Cūḷava.325) – they obtained excellent food.

Likewise, dwellings. Previously, monks were those who had only dwellings at the foot of trees, in unmade caves, etc. Because the Blessed One had permitted – "I allow you, monks, five kinds of lodgings" (Cūḷava.294) – they obtained these dwellings: a vihāra, an aḍḍhayoga, a pāsāda, a hammiya, and a cave.

Previously, they used only stale vegetables as medicine. Because the Blessed One himself had permitted – "I allow you, monks, five kinds of medicines, namely, ghee, fresh butter, oil, honey, and molasses" (Mahāva.260) – in this way, they obtained various medicines.
Moreover, the Blessed One owns even the circumstantial material things.
For they know precisely because it was spoken by the Blessed One: ‘One desiring prosperity in future lives, having given a gift, [kept] morality,… having developed attainments, gradually obtains circumstantial material things, divine prosperity, [and] human prosperity’.
Therefore, since both the non-circumstantial Dharma and the circumstantial Dharma, and the non-circumstantial material things and the circumstantial material things belong to the Blessed One alone, therefore, showing his ownership there, he said: “Become my heirs in the Dharma, monks, not heirs in material things.”

Become heirs of that Dharma of both kinds which belongs to me.
Do not become heirs of that material thing which belongs only to me.
Become owners of the Dharma portion, not of the material portion.

Do not be like that.

There is in me for you… not heirs in material things:
The meaning of that is this: There is in me compassion, sympathy, a desire for welfare for you. By what cause, by what means should the disciples be heirs in the Dharma, owners of the Dharma portion, not heirs in material things?

Having thus conveyed this instruction, he (the Buddha) now, in order to praise the one who perfectly fulfills the practice of the Dharma-inheritance, said "Here, monks (bhikkhaves)," etc. For even upon hearing the Buddha's praise, there are those who undertake the practice for that purpose.

There, idha (here) is a particle. Bhuttāvī means having eaten, having finished the meal duties, it is said. Pavārito means having been invited by the yāvadattha-pavāraṇā, having eaten as much as needed, having refused food, being satisfied, it is said.

Indeed, there are four kinds of pavāraṇā: the vassaṃvuṭṭha-pavāraṇā (invitation at the end of the rains), the paccaya-pavāraṇā (invitation regarding requisites), the an-atirikta-pavāraṇā (invitation without excess), and the yāvadattha-pavāraṇā (invitation as much as needed).

There, "I allow, monks, that monks who have spent the rains invite with respect to three things" (Mahāva. 209), this is the vassaṃvuṭṭha-pavāraṇā.

"Venerable Sir, I wish to invite the Saṅgha to ask for tonics for four months" (Pāci. 303) and "except for another invitation, except for a constant invitation" (Pāci. 307), this is the paccaya-pavāraṇā.

"Being pavārita means eating is seen, cooked food is seen, it is brought forward within arm's reach, a refusal is seen, this is called being pavārita" (Pāci. 239), this is the an-atirikta-pavāraṇā.

"She served the Buddha and his monks with choice hard and soft food till they were satisfied" (Dī. Ni. 1.297, 358), this is the yāvadattha-pavāraṇā. This is what is intended here. Therefore, it was said, " Pavārito means having been invited by the yāvadattha-pavāraṇā."

Paripuṇṇo means full with food. Pariyosito means having finished eating, the elision of the later word should be noted. As much as should be eaten, that much has been eaten, the meaning is, "my eating activity is finished." Suhito means satisfied, it is said that he is happy due to the absence of the suffering of hunger. Yāvadattho means as much as I need with food, all of that has been obtained.

And here, the later three are proof of the former three. For whoever is pariyosito has eaten (bhuttāvī). And whoever is suhito is pavārito by the yāvadattha-pavāraṇā. Whoever is yāvadattho is paripuṇṇo.

Or the former are [proof] of the later. Since he has eaten (bhuttāvī), therefore he is pariyosito. Since he is pavārito, therefore he is suhito. Since he is paripuṇṇo, therefore he is yāvadattho. All of this should be understood as having been said conceptually.
Siyā means "may be" in the sense of one possibility, but also in the sense of an alternative. And, "siyā (syāt) some bhikkhu might commit an offense or a transgression" (siyā aññatarassa bhikkhuno āpatti vītikkamo). Here, both apply.

Atirekadhammo is simply an additional quality. Likewise, chaḍḍanīya dhammo is a quality that should be rejected. That is, something additional should be discarded and nothing else done.

Athā means "at that time."

Jighacchādubbalyaparetā means overcome by hunger and weakness, touched and followed by hunger and weakness for eight or ten days. There, some, though hungry, are not weak; they are able to endure hunger. But to show that these are not like that, it says both.

Tyāha means "I say".

Sace ākaṅkhathā means "if you wish."

Appaharite means "where greenery is not overgrown," meaning in a place where there are no grasses that are liable to be destroyed by scattering alms food. Therefore, it means a place that is both devoid of grass and has thick grass, where the grasses will not be destroyed even if alms food is scattered by a cart. This is said for the sake of not disturbing beings.

Appāṇake means "without living beings," or in a great mass of water devoid of living beings liable to be killed by scattering alms food. For small creatures die when disturbed by scattering food in a small amount of water, but not in large lakes, etc. This is said for the sake of protecting living beings.

Opilāpessāmī means "I will cause to sink."

Tatrekassā means "of those two, to one."

"Vuttaṃ kho paneta ("but this was said") refers to one who has heard this teaching well and repeatedly reflects on it. The word vutta ("said") is also seen in the sense of hair removal, such as in "kāpaṭiko māṇavo daharo vuttasiro" ("a deceitful young man with a shaved head"). It is also seen in the sense of planting, such as in "yathā sāradikaṃ bījaṃ, khette vuttaṃ virūhatī" ("just as a seed sown in a field in autumn sprouts").
Even in statements like, “This was said by the Blessed One, this was said by the Worthy One,” the “said” (kathite) is to be understood here. However, “kathitaṃ kho panetaṃ” means “it was indeed said.”

Āmisaññatara means one of the four material requisites, that is, one. Yadidaṃ is an indeclinable particle that should be changed according to the meaning in each case, in all genders, cases, and numbers. But here, it means “yo eso” (that which). That is, what is called alms food. “Idaṃ āmisaññataraṃ” means this. Yaṃnūnāhaṃ means "sādhu vatāhaṃ" (it would be good if I...). Evaṃ means, just as I spend this moment now, so too the day and night. Vītināmeyya means “should spend, should pass."

So taṃ piṇḍapātaṃ means, not having eaten that alms food, which should be received with respect on the head by the world with its gods, and which is even more than the Sugata, desiring to be an heir to the Dharma, having reflected on the simile of the burning head, he should spend that day and night in this way with weakness from hunger.

Atha dutiyassā means, in this second instance, this is the summary: if that monk, thinking "it would be good if I…pe…should spend it," should think thus: for one gone forth, it is difficult to seek alms food in a village crowded with the five strands of sensual desire, like medicine in a forest crowded with wild animals. But this alms food is free from the dangers of seeking and is even more than the Sugata, like a well-born kṣatriya prince from both sides. And by these five reasons, alms food should not be consumed: that is, it should not be consumed criticizing the person, saying "it belongs to a shameless person." It should not be consumed because of impure origin, saying "it arose from the non-existent expectation of a nun cooking." It should not be consumed out of compassion for the owner, saying "the monk who owns the alms food is hungry." That donor should not be consumed out of compassion for his pupils, etc., saying, "the pupils or others related to him are hungry." Even those donors are content. Moreover, it should not be consumed out of lack of faith, saying "the monk who owns the alms food is faithless." And by these reasons, this is free. The Blessed One is the foremost of the modest, the alms food is of pure origin, the Blessed One and the donor are content, there is no other person who is hopeful, the Blessed One is the foremost of those in the world who have faith, thinking thus, he should eat that alms food…pe…he should spend it.

To this extent, even one who practices the ascetic life without eating, has not eaten alms food that should be eaten. Even one who practices the ascetic life after eating, has eaten what should be eaten. There is no difference in alms food. But there is a difference in persons. Therefore, showing that, he says, kiñcāpi so etc.

There, kiñcāpi is an indeclinable particle in the sense of permission and praise. What does he permit? That faultless use of that monk. What does he praise? Doing the ascetic life after eating. This is said: even if that monk, having eaten what should be eaten in this way, should do what should be done.

Atha kho asuyeva me purimo bhikkhū means: that former monk, even rejecting that alms food, practices the ascetic life, he alone is more heroic than the heroes in the two heroes, like a more wise person among the two wise people, more worthy of offerings and more praiseworthy, more worthy of honor and praise than the second monk, it is said.
Now, explaining that meaning with a reason, he says, beginning with, "What is the reason for that?"

The meaning of that is: How might it be for you there that this bhikkhu is more worthy of respect and praise than the Blessed One? Because that rejection of almsfood by that bhikkhu will lead to wanting little, and so forth, for a long time...to arousing energy.

How so? For if, at another time, excessive wanting, evil wanting, or great wanting should arise in him regarding the requisites, then, reflecting thus, he will ward it off with this goad of rejecting almsfood: "Hey, you! Having rejected even an abundance of almsfood from the Well-gone One, did you produce such a desire?"

This is the method for preventing discontent and lack of austerity that has arisen. In this way, it will lead to his wanting little, contentment, and austerity.

What is the reason for that? Because of its being easy to support. Here is the explanation: A certain person is difficult to support and hard to nourish, both for himself and for his attendants.

A certain person is easy to support and easy to nourish, both for himself and for his attendants.

How so? For one who, having received sour things and so forth, seeks non-sour things, discarding what was received in one house in another house, and wandering through the whole village, enters the monastery with an empty bowl and lies down—this one is difficult to support for himself.

But one who, even when his bowl is filled with choice rice, meat, and so forth, shows a face of gloomy sullenness and displeasure, and disparagingly gives that almsfood, saying in their presence, "What is this that you have given?", to novices or lay householders—this one is difficult to support for his attendants.

Seeing this, people keep far away, thinking, "A bhikkhu who is hard to support cannot be sustained."

But one who, having received any kind of almsfood, coarse or fine, eats it with a gladdened mind, goes to the monastery, and does his own work—this one is easy to support for himself.

And one who, even for others, having given a gift, small or large, coarse or fine, without disdain, being pleased in mind, with a serene face, eats it in their presence and then departs—this one is easy to support for his attendants.

Seeing this, people become exceedingly trusting, [thinking,] "Our venerable one is easy to support; he is content even with a little. We ourselves will sustain him," and having made a promise, they sustain him.

There, if at another time, a mind with a tendency toward being difficult to support should arise in him, either for himself or for his attendants, then, reflecting thus, he will ward it off with this goad of rejecting almsfood: "Hey, you! Having rejected even an abundance of almsfood from the Well-gone One, did you produce such a mind?" In this way, it will lead to his being easy to support.

If, moreover, laziness should arise in him, he will ward off even that with this goad: "Hey, you! Having at that time rejected even an abundance of almsfood from the Well-gone One, and having practiced the śrāmaṇa dharma even while overcome with hunger and weakness, are you today engaging in laziness?" Thus reflecting, it will lead to his arousing energy.

In this way, that rejection of almsfood by him will lead for a long time to wanting little...to arousing energy.

In this way, these five qualities of his being complete, they will completely fulfill the ten grounds for talk.

How so? Here, in the Pāli [canon] itself, three have come by way of wanting little, contentment, and arousing energy; the remaining ones are included by austerity.

For this is just the name of all the grounds for talk, namely, austerity.

As it was said: "And such talk, Ānanda, as deals with effacement, as favors the mind's release, and which leads to complete disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, peace, direct knowledge, enlightenment, and Nirvāṇa, that is, talk on wanting little," and so forth (Majjhima Nikāya 3.189, 192)—[this is] the detailed explanation.

In this way, these five qualities being complete, they will completely fulfill the ten grounds for talk.

The ten grounds for talk being complete, the three trainings will completely fulfill [them].

How so? Here, these four talks—talk on wanting little, talk on contentment, talk on aloofness from society, [and] talk on virtue—are included within the training in higher virtue.

These three talks—talk on seclusion, talk on arousing energy, [and] talk on meditative concentration—are included within the training in higher mind.

These three talks—talk on wisdom, talk on deliverance, [and] talk on the knowledge and vision of deliverance—are included within the training in higher wisdom.

In this way, the ten grounds for talk being complete, the three trainings will completely fulfill [them].
The three trainings, being complete, will completely fulfill the five aggregates of one beyond training ( asekṣa-dharmāskandha).

How so? Because the complete higher training in morality (adhiśīla-śikṣā) is the aggregate of morality of one beyond training; the higher training in mind (adhicitta-śikṣā) is the aggregate of meditation of one beyond training; the higher training in wisdom (adhiprajñā-śikṣā) is the aggregate of wisdom, liberation, the knowledge and vision of liberation of one beyond training. Thus, the three trainings, being complete, will completely fulfill the five aggregates of one beyond training.

The five aggregates of the Dharma, being complete, will completely fulfill the deathless nirvāṇa.

Just as on the top of a mountain, a rain cloud, having rained, completely fills the mountain caves, ponds, streams, and branches;

Those being complete, [fill] small pools, the small pools fill large pools, the large pools fill small rivers, the small rivers fill large rivers, the large rivers fill the great ocean; just so, the five qualities of that bhikṣu, being complete, from the ten topics of discussion onward, up to the deathless nirvāṇa, will completely fulfill [them].

This bhikṣu, being one who has undertaken the practice that is the inheritance of the Dharma (dhammadāyāda-paṭipadā), obtains the supreme inheritance of the Dharma (paramadhammadāyāda). Seeing this meaning, the Blessed One said, "What is the cause? Because, bhikṣus, that is his," and so on.

Thus, having established the state of being worthy of offerings and praise of that bhikṣu by means of a cause, now, joining those bhikṣus to that very state, [he] said, "Therefore, bhikṣus..." and so on.

What was said? Because whoever, having eaten that alms food (piṇḍapāta), would do the practice of a śramaṇa, that one is excluded from these five root qualities (mūlaguṇa).

But whoever, without eating, would do [it], that one is a partaker of these. "Therefore, this to me, bhikṣus...not inheritors of material things (āmiṣadāyāda)."

"Therefore, this to me, bhikṣus"—the Blessed One said this passage of the sūtra, from the beginning to the end, up to "not inheritors of material things," the Blessed One said.

"Idaṃ vatvāna sugato [Having said this, the Sugata]," because having said this passage of the sūtra, having gone to the beautiful practice, the Blessed One attained the designation "Sugata."

"Uṭṭhāyāsanā vihāraṃ pāvisī [Having risen from the seat, he entered the vihāra]," having risen from the seat of the Buddha that had been prepared, he entered the vihāra, his own great fragrance chamber (mahāgandhakuṭi), without a divided assembly (parisā).

Why? For the sake of Dharma-praise.

"Idam avoca bhagavā [Thus spoke the Blessed One]," "Idaṃ vatvāna sugato [Having said this, the Sugata]," "Sugato [The Sugata]," "Uṭṭhāyāsanā vihāraṃ pāvisī [Having risen from the seat, he entered the vihāra]."

It seems that those who enter the vihāra after an unfinished teaching (deśanā) enter for two reasons: either for the sake of praising the person (pudgala) or for the sake of praising the Dharma.

Entering for the sake of praising the pudgala, he thought thus: "This outline (uddeśa) has been shown by me in brief, not divided in detail. The bhikṣus who receive the Dharma, having grasped [it], will approach Ānanda or Kātyāyana and ask. Having compared [it] with my knowledge (jñāna), they will speak. Then the bhikṣus who receive the Dharma will again ask me. To them I will say, 'Well spoken by Ānanda, bhikṣus, well spoken by Kātyāyana! Even if you were to ask me this meaning, I too would explain it just so.' Thus I will praise those pudgalas. Then, having generated respect in them, the bhikṣus will approach. Those bhikṣus too will apply [themselves] to meaning and Dharma, and those trained by them, fulfilling the three trainings, will make an end of suffering."

Entering for the sake of Dharma-praise, he thought thus, just as he thought here: "When I have entered the vihāra, Sāriputta, seated in this very assembly (parisad), while disparaging that very inheritor of material things (āmiṣadāyāda) and praising the inheritor of Dharma (dhammadāyāda), will teach the Dharma. Thus, this teaching (deśanā) spoken by the intention (mati) of both of us, being united in one place (ekajjhāsayāya), will be chief and weighty, like a stone umbrella.

Like a boat placed at a tīrtha for the sake of crossing over the four floods (caturogha), it will be like a chariot yoked with four well-trained horses for the sake of going on the path (mārga).

And just as when the king, having placed a command (āṇa) in the assembly, saying, 'For one doing thus, this is the punishment (daṇḍa)', and having risen from the seat, has ascended to the palace (pāsāda), the general (senāpati), seated right there, puts into effect that command placed by the king; just so, Sāriputta, seated in this very assembly, having praised the teaching placed by me, will teach. Thus, this teaching spoken by the intention (mati) of both of us will be stronger and will blaze like the midday sun."

Thus, here, for the sake of Dharma-praise, having risen from the seat, he entered the vihāra.

And in such places, the Blessed One, while seated, disappears from the seat itself and enters the vihāra by means of mental velocity (cittagati), it should be understood.

For if he were to go by means of bodily velocity (kāyagati), the entire assembly, having surrounded the Blessed One, would go, and that, having been divided once, would again be a bad gathering (dussannipāta). Therefore, the Blessed One entered by means of mental velocity.

This is the way in the cases of the middle and new [monks].

Here, in this first basis (ṭhānena) the elders (therā) are blameworthy (bhikkhū gārayhā). Blameworthy (gārayhā). Elders (therā). In the second basis (dutiyena ṭhānenā). In the third basis (tatiyena ṭhānenā).

Having abandoned greed and the like, they fulfill mental seclusion. This great elder, though surrounded by his pupils and resident disciples, even when it is appropriate to sit down, having performed his meal duties, enters in the evening and departs in the morning, or enters in the morning and departs in the evening, even in this state of old age. He performs kasiṇa preparations, brings about attainments, and attains the fruits of the path. In every way, he fulfills mental seclusion. In this second instance, the elder monks are praiseworthy; they obtain praise.

At a time when soft cloths of silk and the like, pleasant to the touch, and light robes and the like are fitting for the elder, at that very time, this great elder wears robes made of rags, takes up the unrelaxed teaching, having dispelled the hindrances, having attained the fruition attainment, he dwells fulfilling detachment from the bases of existence. "What was he like in his youth and middle age?" In this third instance, the elders are praiseworthy; they obtain praise.

33. "Friends (āvuso), what is the connection (anusandhi)?" Thus, after disparaging the practice based on material inheritance in nine ways, praising the practice based on the inheritance of the Dharma in nine ways, and completing the teaching in eighteen ways, those phenomena that were mentioned as "those phenomena whose abandonment the Teacher spoke of, and they do not abandon those phenomena," thus, phenomena that should be abandoned were spoken of.

To show those in their essence as "these are they," he said this beginning with "Friends (tatrāvuso), greed (lobho) is evil (pāpako)," this is the connection.

Moreover, below, the Dharma was spoken of only by way of implication.

But material things were spoken of both by way of implication and without implication.

Now, to speak of the supramundane path, which is the Dharma without implication, he said this.

This is the connection here.

There, tatra means an indication of the past teaching, it means "in the teaching spoken of in the manner beginning with 'When the Teacher dwells in seclusion, the disciples do not train in seclusion.'"

"Greed (lobho) is evil (pāpako), hate (doso) is evil (pāpako)," these two things are evil and base, he shows that these should be abandoned.

There, greed (lobho) is characterized by coveting (lubbhanalakkhaṇo).

Hate (doso) is characterized by ill-will (dussanalakkhaṇo).

Among these, greed (lobho) exists in the gain of the requisites of one who is an heir to material things, hate (doso) exists in the lack of gain (alābhe).

Through greed (lobhena), one desires what has not been obtained (aladdhaṃ pattheti), through hate (dosena), one becomes agitated when not obtaining (alabhanto vighātavā hoti).

Greed (lobho) also exists in things that should be given (deyyadhamme hoti), hate (doso) exists in one who does not give (adāyake vā) or one who gives what is displeasing (amanuññadāyake vā).

Through greed (lobhena), one fills up the phenomena rooted in the nine cravings (navataṇhāmūlake dhamme paripūreti), through hate (dosena), the five instances of stinginess (pañca macchariyāni).

Now, showing the means for their abandonment, he said this beginning with "for the abandonment of greed (lobhassa ca pahānāyā)."

Its meaning is: for the abandonment of that evil greed (lobhassa ca) and hate (dosassa ca).

"There is a middle way (atthi majjhimā paṭipadā)," this was said referring to the path (maggaṃ sandhāya idaṃ vuttaṃ).

For the path does not go to those two extremes, greed (lobho) is one extreme (eko anto), hate (doso) is one extreme (eko anto), it is liberated from these extremes, therefore it is called "the middle way (majjhimā paṭipadā)."

Because it exists in the middle of these (etesaṃ majjhe bhavattā) it is "middle (majjhimā)," and because it should be practiced (paṭipajjitabbato ca) it is "way (paṭipadā)."

Likewise, indulgence in sensual pleasures is one extreme (eko anto), indulgence in self-mortification is one extreme (eko anto), eternalism is one extreme (eko anto), annihilationism is one extreme (eko anto), it should be expanded upon in the same manner as before.

But with "conducive to vision (cakkhukaraṇī)," etc., he praises that same path (paṭipadaṃ).

For it leads to the sight of the truths (saccānaṃ dassanāya saṃvattati) in the sense of being conducive to vision (dassanapariṇāyakaṭṭhenāti), therefore "conducive to vision (cakkhukaraṇī)."

It leads to the knowledge of the truths (saccānaṃ ñāṇāya saṃvattati) in the sense of being conducive to understanding (viditakaraṇaṭṭhenāti), therefore "conducive to knowledge (ñāṇakaraṇī)."

And from the calming of greed etc. (rāgādīnañca vūpasamanato), "it leads to calm (upasamāya saṃvattati)."

Because it shows the knowable nature of all four truths (catunnampi saccānaṃ abhiññeyyabhāvadassanato), "it leads to direct knowledge (abhiññāya saṃvattati)."

"Enlightenment (sambodho)" means the path (maggo), because it leads to its attainment (tassatthāya saṃvattanato), "it leads to enlightenment (sambodhāya saṃvattati)."

For the path itself leads to the path's attainment (maggatthāya saṃvattati) by accomplishing the task that should be done by the path (maggena kātabbakiccakaraṇato).

"Nirvāṇa (nibbānaṃ)" is without condition (appaccayaṃ), but because it leads to the realization (sacchikiriyāya) and direct experience (paccakkhakammāya) of that, it is called "it leads to nirvāṇa (nibbānāya saṃvattati)."

This is the essence here.

Explanation otherwise is proliferation.

Now, wishing to show that middle way (majjhimaṃ paṭipadaṃ) in its essence (sarūpato), after asking "And what is that (katamā ca sā)?" he explains it in the manner beginning with "This itself (ayamevā)."
_Ayamevā_ ("this very one") is a word of emphasis, for the purpose of refuting other paths and for showing the commonality of Buddhas, Pratyekabuddhas, and disciples of the Buddha.

And it was said, "This is the path; there is no other for the purification of vision" (Dhp. 274).

Moreover, this one is ariya ("noble") because of keeping away from defilements. It is ariya because it leads to the abandonment of enemies (aripahānāya), because it is taught by the noble ones (ariyena), and because it leads to the attainment of the state of the noble ones (ariyabhāvappaṭilābhāya). It is aṭṭhaṅgiko ("eightfold") because it is endowed with eight factors (aṅgehi), and it is not devoid of factors, like a five-piece orchestra, etc. It is maggo ("path") because it goes, killing the defilements; or it seeks (maggati) nirvāṇa; or it is sought (maggīyati) by those who desire nirvāṇa; or it is approached (gammati) and practiced by them.

" _Seyyathidaṃ_" is a particle (nipāto); its meaning is, "Which is that?" (tassa katamo so iti ceti), or "Which are those eight factors?" (katamāni vā tāni aṭṭhaṅgānīti). Indeed, each factor is itself the path. As it was said, "Right view is the path and also the cause" (Dhs. 1039). The ancients also said, "Right view is the path of vision; right thought is the path of application... right concentration is the path of non-distraction."

_Ayamevā ariyā aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo_.

Among these, _sammādiṭṭhi_ ("right view") has the characteristic of seeing correctly. _Sammāsaṅkappo_ ("right thought") has the characteristic of applying [the mind] correctly. _Sammāvācā_ ("right speech") has the characteristic of grasping correctly. _Sammākammanto_ ("right action") has the characteristic of undertaking correctly. _Sammāājīvo_ ("right livelihood") has the characteristic of purifying correctly. _Sammāvāyāmo_ ("right effort") has the characteristic of striving correctly. _Sammāsati_ ("right mindfulness") has the characteristic of attending correctly. _Sammāsamādhi_ ("right concentration") has the characteristic of concentrating correctly.

The etymology of these should be understood in the same way, that _sammādiṭṭhī_ ("right view") means "one sees correctly" (sammā passatīti).

_Sammādiṭṭhi, sammāsaṅkappo, sammāvācā, sammākammanto, sammāājīvo, sammāvāyāmo, sammāsati, sammāsamādhi, sammādiṭṭhī_.

Therein, right view, when arising, abandons wrong view and the defilements associated with it, as well as ignorance. It makes nirvāṇa its object and sees the associated states. And these are without confusion, not from the object; therefore, it is called "right view."

Right thought abandons wrong thought and the defilements associated with it. It makes nirvāṇa its object and applies the associated states correctly; therefore, it is called "right thought."

Right speech abandons wrong speech and the defilements associated with it. It makes nirvāṇa its object and grasps the associated states correctly; therefore, it is called "right speech."

Right action abandons wrong action and the defilements associated with it. It makes nirvāṇa its object and undertakes the associated states correctly; therefore, it is called "right action."

Right livelihood abandons wrong livelihood and the defilements associated with it. It makes nirvāṇa its object and purifies the associated states correctly; therefore, it is called "right livelihood."
Right effort abandons wrong effort and related defilements and sloth, and makes nirvāṇa its object. It correctly grasps associated dharmas, therefore it is called "right effort."

Right mindfulness abandons wrong mindfulness and related defilements, and makes nirvāṇa its object. It correctly establishes associated dharmas, therefore it is called "right mindfulness."

Right concentration abandons wrong concentration and related defilements and distraction, and makes nirvāṇa its object. It correctly concentrates associated dharmas, therefore it is called "right concentration."

Now, he said, concluding that very practice, "This, O friend, is it."

Its meaning is: That which was spoken of, having gathered the four supramundane paths into one, "the eightfold path," this, O friend…etc… leads to nirvāṇa.

This, O friend, is it. Having shown the greed and hatred among the dharmas to be abandoned, and the means to abandon them, now, showing the other dharmas to be abandoned and the means to abandon them, he said, "There, friend, is anger, and…”

There, anger has the characteristic of being easily angered, or of being fierce, its function is the making of injury, its manifestation is aversion.

Resentment has the characteristic of binding, its function is not relinquishing enmity, its manifestation is the state of adherence to anger.

And it was said, "In the former time there is anger, in the latter time there is resentment," etc. (Vibha. 891).

There, friend, is anger, and anger, resentment, belittling others’ good qualities is the characteristic of depreciation, its function is the ruin of those qualities, its manifestation is the covering over of them.

Grasping at the yoke is the characteristic of obstinacy, equating one's own qualities with others’ qualities is its function, its manifestation is attending by measuring the qualities of others.

Depreciation, obstinacy, envy is the characteristic of being displeased at others' success, or its characteristic is being unable to bear it, there, its taste is lack of delight, from that, its manifestation is turning away.

Concealing one's own success is the characteristic of stinginess, its function is unease at sharing one's own success with others, its manifestation is contraction.

Envy, stinginess, concealing committed evils is the characteristic of deceit, its taste is concealing, its manifestation is covering that over.

Making known one's own nonexistent good qualities is the characteristic of craftiness, its taste is citing those, its manifestation is making those prominent even by means of bodily gestures.

Deceit, craftiness, rigidity is the characteristic of the mind's state of being puffed up, its taste is lack of reverence, its manifestation is intractability.

Excelling in deeds is the characteristic of impetuosity, its taste is opposing, its manifestation is disrespect.

Rigidity, impetuosity, inflation is the characteristic of pride, its taste is ego-making, its manifestation is the state of being puffed up.

Extreme inflation is the characteristic of conceit, it is exceedingly ego-making.

Its manifestation is exceeding the state of being puffed up.

Pride, conceit, the state of intoxication is the characteristic of vanity, its taste is grasping intoxication, its manifestation is madness.

Abandoning the mind in the five sense pleasures is the characteristic of negligence, its taste is not giving rise to abandonment, its manifestation is loss of mindfulness. Thus, the characteristics, etc., of these dharmas should be understood.

This is the summary here, but the detailed explanation should be understood in the same manner as has been told in the Vibhaṅga (Vibha. 891), beginning with “What is anger there?”

Vanity, negligence, in the Vibhaṅga, specifically, there, an heir to material things, not obtaining [something] for himself, is angry at another who has obtained [it]; his anger, once arisen, is simply anger, beyond that, [there is] resentment.

He, thus angry, resenting, and while there are good qualities in another who has obtained [something], depreciates them, and grasps at the yoke, [thinking,] “I, too, am like that.” This is his depreciation and obstinacy. Thus, the one who is given to depreciation and obstinacy, because of another's gains, honor, etc., is envious and malicious, [thinking,] “What is this to him, by means of this?" This is his envy.

If, however, he has some success, he does not bear sharing that with him. This is his stinginess.

For the sake of gain, however, he conceals even faults that are present in himself. This is his deceit.

Even nonexistent good qualities he makes known.

This is his craftiness.

He who has thus undertaken [this behavior], if he obtains gain according to his inclination, is rigid and ungentle-minded; he cannot be persuaded, [thinking,] “This should not be done in this way.” This is his rigidity.

If, however, someone says something to him, "This should not be done in this way," he becomes of hardened mind and contorts his face, [thinking,] “Who is he to me?"
"Having understood and spoken thus," this is his sārambho (beginning, undertaking).

Then, with thambha (obstinacy), thinking "I am better," he becomes conceited (mānī).

With sārambha, disdaining others, thinking "Who are these?", he becomes overly conceited (atimānī); this is his māno (conceit) and atimāno (over-conceit).

He generates mada (intoxication) of various kinds, such as intoxication with birth, etc., through these conceits and over-conceits.

Being intoxicated, he becomes negligent with respect to things such as the divisions of sense pleasures; this is his mada (intoxication) and pamādo (negligence).

Kodho (anger), upanāho (grudge), issā (envy), maccheraṃ (stinginess), māyā (deceit), sāṭheyyaṃ (craftiness), thambho (obstinacy), sārambho (beginning, undertaking), māno (conceit), atimāno (over-conceit), mado (intoxication), pamādo (negligence): thus, a recipient of material things is not freed from these evil qualities and other such qualities.

Thus, these qualities to be abandoned should be understood.

The means of abandonment, in terms of both the text (pāṭhato) and the meaning (atthato), are entirely without distinction everywhere.

Here, this distinction, the order (kamo), and the method of development (bhāvanānayo) should be understood in terms of the proficiency of familiarizing oneself with knowledge.

There, first of all, the bhedo (distinction): this middle path is sometimes the Noble Eightfold Path (ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo), sometimes the sevenfold path.

This eightfold path arises by way of the supramundane first jhāna (absorption); by way of the remaining jhānas, it is sevenfold.

However, here, it is called eightfold from the standpoint of the highest designation.

For beyond this, there is no path factor.

Thus, the bhedo (distinction) should be understood here.

Bhedo (distinction): But since right view (sammādiṭṭhi) is the best of all wholesome qualities (sabbakusalānaṃ), as he said, "Wisdom is the best, the skillful say" (Jā. 2.17.81).

And it is foremost in the wholesome division, as he said, "And how, monks, does right view go before? One understands right view as right view, wrong view as wrong view" (M. Ni. 3.136) - in detail.

As he also said, "Knowledge, monks, goes before the attainment of wholesome qualities."

The remaining factors originate and are produced from that, as he said, "For one with right view, right thought arises...pe...for one with right mindfulness, right concentration arises" (M. Ni. 3.141).

Therefore, these factors are spoken of in this order; thus, the kamo (order) should be understood here.

Kamo (order): As for the bhāvanānayo (method of development), some develop insight (vipassana) preceded by serenity (samatha), some develop serenity preceded by insight.

How? Here, a certain one first produces access concentration (upacārasamādhi) or attainment concentration (appanāsamādhi); this is serenity. He then sees that, and the qualities associated with it, as impermanent, etc.; this is insight.

Thus, first serenity, then insight.

Therefore, it is said, "He develops insight preceded by serenity."

As he is developing insight preceded by serenity, the path is generated, and he cultivates, develops, and increases that path. As he cultivates, develops, and increases that path, the fetters are abandoned, and the underlying tendencies are uprooted; thus, he develops insight preceded by serenity.
Here, someone, without first producing tranquility [ samatha ] of the type described, sees the five aggregates of clinging as impermanent, etc.; this is insight [vipaśyanā].

For him, through the fulfillment of insight, from attending to abandonment, the one-pointedness of mind arises in regard to the things born there; this is tranquility. Thus, first insight, then tranquility. Therefore, it is said, "He develops tranquility preceded by insight."

As he is developing serenity preceded by insight, the path is generated. He frequents that path…pe… cultivates it; as he frequents that path…pe… the latent tendencies are eliminated (A. Ni. 4.170; Paṭi. Ma. 2.1); thus, he develops tranquility preceded by insight.

However, for one developing insight preceded by tranquility, at the moment of the supramundane path, tranquility and insight are yoked together. Thus, the method of cultivation (bhāvanānayo) should be understood in this way here.
