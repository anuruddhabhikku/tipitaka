21. Thus have I heard:

Once the Blessed One was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapindika’s Park.

There the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying: “Bhikkhus!”

“Venerable sir,” those monks replied.

The Blessed One said this: “Bhikkhus, there are some ascetics and Brahmins who speculate about the future, who hold views about the future, who assert various doctrinal propositions concerning the future. They declare, ‘The self is percipient (saññī attā) and unimpaired (arogo) after death (paraṃ maraṇā),’ or ‘The self is non-percipient (asaññī attā) and unimpaired after death,’ or ‘The self is neither percipient nor non-percipient (nevasaññīnāsaññī attā) and unimpaired after death.’ Or they describe the annihilation (ucchedaṃ), destruction (vināsaṃ), and extermination (vibhavaṃ) of an existing being at death, or some assert nirvāṇa (nibbānaṃ) here and now (dṛṣṭadharm निर्वाण).

Thus, they either describe an existing self that is unimpaired after death, or they describe the annihilation, destruction, and extermination of an existing being at death, or they assert nirvāṇa here and now.

Thus, these views being five become three, and being three become five. This is the summary of the ‘five and three.’

22. “Therein, bhikkhus, those recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self (ātman) as percipient (saññin) and unimpaired (aroga) after death (maraṇā), describe such a self, percipient and unimpaired after death, to be either: material (rūpin); or immaterial (arūpin); or both material and immaterial (rūpiñca arūpiñca); or neither material nor immaterial (nevarūpiṃ nārūpiṃ); or percipient of unity (ekattasaññin); or percipient of diversity (nānattasaññin); or percipient of the limited (parittasaññin); or percipient of the immeasurable (appamāṇasaññin). Or else, among those few who go beyond this, some assert the consciousness-kasina, immeasurable (appamāṇaṃ) and imperturbable (āneñjaṃ).

“The Tathāgata, bhikkhus, understands this thus: Those good recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self (ātman) as percipient (saññin) and unimpaired (aroga) after death (maraṇā), describe such a self, percipient and unimpaired after death, to be either material (rūpin); or immaterial (arūpin); or both material and immaterial (rūpiñca arūpiñca); or neither material nor immaterial (nevarūpiṃ nārūpiṃ); or percipient of unity (ekattasaññin); or percipient of diversity (nānattasaññin); or percipient of the limited (parittasaññin); or percipient of the immeasurable (appamāṇasaññin), since the perceptions are declared to be the purest (parisuddhā), supreme (paramā), best (aggā), and unsurpassed (anuttariyā) – whether perceptions of form (rūpasaññānaṃ) or of the formless (arūpasaññānaṃ), of unity (ekattasaññānaṃ) or of diversity (nānattasaññānaṃ).

some assert the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana), immeasurable (appamāṇaṃ) and imperturbable (āneñjaṃ), to be the self.

That is conditioned (saṅkhataṃ) and gross (oḷārikaṃ), but there is cessation (nirodha) of formations (saṅkhārānaṃ). Having known ‘There is this’ seeing the escape (nissaraṇadassāvī) from that, the Tathāgata has gone beyond that.

23. “Therein, bhikkhus, those recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self (ātmā) as non-percipient (asaññiṃ) and unimpaired (arogaṃ) after death (paraṃ maraṇā) describe such a self, non-percipient and unimpaired after death, to be either: material (rūpiṃ); or immaterial (arūpiṃ); or both material and immaterial; or neither material nor immaterial.

Therein, bhikkhus, these criticize those recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as percipient (saññiṃ) and unimpaired after death,

What is the reason for this? Because they say: ‘Perception (saññā) is a disease, perception is a tumor (gaṇḍa), perception is a dart (sallaṃ); this is peaceful (santaṃ), this is sublime (paṇītaṃ), that is, non-perception (asaññaṃ)’.

The Tathāgata, bhikkhus, understands this thus: Those good recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as non-percipient and unimpaired after death describe such a self, non-percipient and unimpaired after death, to be either material or immaterial; or both material and immaterial; or neither material nor immaterial.

That any recluse or brāhmaṇa could say: “Apart from material form (rūpā), apart from feeling (vedanā), apart from perception (saññā), apart from formations (saṅkhārehi), I shall describe the coming (āgatiṃ) and going (gatiṃ) of consciousness (vijñāṇassa), its passing away (cutiṃ) and re-appearance (upapattiṃ), its growth (vuddhiṃ), increase (virūḷhiṃ), and maturation (vepullaṃ)” - that is impossible.

‘That is conditioned (saṅkhataṃ) and gross (oḷārikaṃ), but there is cessation (nirodho) of formations.’ Having known ‘There is this,’ seeing the escape (nissaraṇa) from that, the Tathāgata has gone beyond that (tadupātivatto).

24. “Therein, monks, those ascetics and Brahmins who describe the self as neither percipient nor non-percipient ( nevasaññīnāsaññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti) and unimpaired after death describe such a self, neither percipient nor non-percipient and unimpaired after death, to be either: material; or immaterial; or both material and immaterial; or neither material nor immaterial.

Therein, monks, those ascetics and Brahmins who describe the self as percipient ( saññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti) and unimpaired after death criticise these, and those good ascetics and Brahmins who describe the self as non-percipient ( asaññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti) and unimpaired after death criticise these.

Why is that? Because perception ( saññā) is a disease, perception ( saññā) is a tumor, perception ( saññā) is a dart, and non-perception (asaññā) is stupefaction; this is peaceful, this is sublime, that is, ‘neither-perception-nor-non-perception’ (nevasaññānāsañña).

Those good ascetics and Brahmins who describe the self as neither percipient nor non-percipient and unimpaired after death describe such a self, neither percipient nor non-percipient and unimpaired after death, to be either material…or neither material nor immaterial.

Monks, those ascetics or Brahmins who describe the attainment of this base by means of formations regarding what is seen, heard, sensed, and cognized, this is declared to be a disaster for the attainment of this base.

<p n="25" rend="bodytext"><hi rend="paranum">25</hi><hi rend="dot">.</hi> ‘‘Tatra, bhikkhave, ye te samaṇabrāhmaṇā sato sattassa ucchedaṃ vināsaṃ vibhavaṃ paññapenti <pb ed="V" n="3.0020"/>, tatra, bhikkhave, ye te samaṇabrāhmaṇā saññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti arogaṃ paraṃ maraṇā tesamete paṭikkosanti, yepi te bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā asaññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti arogaṃ paraṃ maraṇā tesamete paṭikkosanti, yepi te bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā nevasaññīnāsaññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti arogaṃ paraṃ maraṇā tesamete paṭikkosanti. Taṃ kissa hetu? Sabbepime bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā uddhaṃ saraṃ <note>uddhaṃsarā (sī. pī.), uddhaṃ parāmasanti (syā. kaṃ.)</note> āsattiṃyeva abhivadanti – ‘iti pecca <pb ed="T" n="3.0033"/> bhavissāma, iti pecca bhavissāmā’ti. Seyyathāpi nāma vāṇijassa vāṇijjāya gacchato evaṃ hoti – ‘ito me idaṃ bhavissati, iminā idaṃ lacchāmī’ti, evamevime bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā vāṇijūpamā maññe paṭibhanti – ‘iti pecca bhavissāma, iti pecca bhavissāmā’ti. Tayidaṃ, bhikkhave, tathāgato abhijānāti. Ye kho te bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā sato sattassa ucchedaṃ vināsaṃ vibhavaṃ paññapenti te sakkāyabhayā sakkāyaparijegucchā sakkāyaññeva anuparidhāvanti anuparivattanti. Seyyathāpi nāma sā gaddulabaddho daḷhe thambhe vā khile <note>khīle (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.)</note> vā upanibaddho <pb ed="P" n="2.0233"/>, tameva thambhaṃ vā khilaṃ vā anuparidhāvati anuparivattati <pb ed="M" n="3.0022"/>; evamevime bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā sakkāyabhayā sakkāyaparijegucchā sakkāyaññeva anuparidhāvanti anuparivattanti. ‘Tayidaṃ saṅkhataṃ oḷārikaṃ atthi kho pana saṅkhārānaṃ nirodho attheta’nti – iti viditvā tassa nissaraṇadassāvī tathāgato tadupātivatto.

“Therein, bhikkhus, those recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the annihilation, destruction, and extermination of an existing being at death, therein, bhikkhus, those recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as percipient (saññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti) and unimpaired (arogaṃ paraṃ maraṇā) after death, these criticize those good recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as non-percipient (asaññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti) and unimpaired (arogaṃ paraṃ maraṇā) after death, and they criticize those good recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as neither percipient nor non-percipient (nevasaññīnāsaññiṃ attānaṃ paññapenti) and unimpaired (arogaṃ paraṃ maraṇā) after death.

Why is that? All these good recluses and brāhmaṇas, rushing onwards, assert their attachment thus: ‘We shall be thus after death, we shall be thus after death.’

Just as a merchant going to market thinks: ‘Through this, that will be mine; with this, I will get that’; so too, these good recluses and brāhmaṇas seem like merchants when they declare: ‘We shall be thus after death, we shall be thus after death.’

The Tathāgata, bhikkhus, understands this.

Those good recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the annihilation, destruction, and extermination of an existing being at death, through fear of personality (sakkāyabhayā) and disgust with personality (sakkāyaparijegucchā), keep running and circling around that same personality (sakkāyaññeva anuparidhāvanti anuparivattanti).

Just as a dog bound by a leash tied to a firm post or pillar keeps on running and circling around that same post or pillar; so too, these good recluses and brāhmaṇas, through fear of personality (sakkāyabhayā) and disgust with personality (sakkāyaparijegucchā), keep running and circling around that same personality (sakkāyaññeva anuparidhāvanti anuparivattanti).

<pb ed="P" n="2.0238"/> <p rend="centre">Pañcattayasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ dutiyaṃ.

33. Here, bhikkhus, some recluse or brāhmin, with the relinquishing of views about the past and the future, through complete lack of resolve upon the fetters of sensual pleasure, and with the surmounting of the rapture of seclusion, unworldly pleasure, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, regards himself thus: ‘I am at peace, I have attained Nibbāna, I am without clinging.’

This, bhikkhus, the Tathāgata understands.

This good recluse or brāhmin, with the relinquishing of views about the past and the future… regards himself thus: “I am at peace, I have attained Nibbāna, I am without clinging.” Certainly this venerable one asserts the way directed to Nibbāna.

Yet this good recluse or brāhmin still clings, clinging either to a view about the past or to a view about the future or to a fetter of sensual pleasure or to the rapture of seclusion or to unworldly pleasure or to neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling.

And when this venerable one regards himself thus: “I am at peace, I have attained Nibbāna, I am without clinging,” that too is declared to be clinging on the part of this good recluse or brāhmin.

That is conditioned and gross, but there is cessation of formations. Having known ‘There is this’ seeing the escape from that, the Tathāgata has gone beyond that.

“Bhikkhus, this supreme state of sublime peace has been discovered by the Tathāgata, that is, liberation through not clinging, by understanding as they actually are the origination, the disappearance, the gratification, the danger, and the escape in the case of the six bases of contact.

This the Blessed One said. Elated, those bhikkhus delighted in the Blessed One’s statement.

The Pañcattaya Sutta, the second, is finished.
