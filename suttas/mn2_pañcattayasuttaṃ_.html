21. Thus have I heard:

Once the Blessed One was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

There the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying: “Monks!”

“Venerable sir,” those monks replied.

The Blessed One said this: “Monks, there are some ascetics and Brahmins who speculate about the future, who hold views about the future, who assert various doctrinal propositions concerning the future. They assert that ‘the self is percipient and unimpaired after death.’ Some assert that ‘the self is non-percipient and unimpaired after death.’ Some assert that “the self is neither percipient nor non-percipient and unimpaired after death.’ Or they describe the annihilation, destruction, and extermination of an existing being at death, or some assert dṛṣṭadharmanirvāṇa (Nibbana here and now).

“Thus they either describe an existing self that is unimpaired after death; or they describe the annihilation, destruction, and extermination of an existing being at death; or they assert dṛṣṭadharmanirvāṇa (Nibbana here and now).

Thus these five become three, and three become five. This is the summary of the ‘five and three.’

21. It is like this:

Thus I have heard—the Pañcattayasutta.

There, "some" means a certain.

"Ascetics and brāhmaṇas" means ascetics by way of approaching wandering, brāhmaṇas by birth, thus agreed upon by the world as both ascetics and brāhmaṇas.

Aparantakappikā means those who imagine and variously imagine the further end.

Or, aparantakappikā is because they have a thought construction concerning the further end.

Here, "end" means the section intended like in, "Indeed, friend, the existing aggregate is one end," etc. (A.N. 6.61).

Kappa means craving and views.

And it was said, "A thought construction: From the summary, there are two thought constructions, the thought construction of craving and the thought construction of views."

Therefore, the meaning here should be understood thus: Because they persistently think and speculate about the future portion of the aggregates by way of craving and views, they are "speculators about the past."

Because for them, thus imagining and persisting in the further end, a view that repeatedly follows just the further end by way of arising, they are "those who see what follows the further end."

Those who have such a view, starting from and reaching and encountering that further end, expressing many kinds of statements and terms for views, cause even another person to have a direction for views.

"Many kinds" means many types.

"Adhivuttipadānī" means terms of designation.

Or alternatively, because of speaking by surpassing the real meaning, not grasping it as it is in its own nature, "views" are said to be adhivuttiyo, the terms of views are "adhivuttipadānī," statements that illuminate views is the meaning.

Saññī means possessing perception.

Arogo means constant.

Itthake means thus some, thus one is the meaning.

By this, sixteen perception-vādā are told, asaññī by this, eight non-perception-vādā, nevasaññīnāsaññī by this, eight neither-perception-nor-non-perception-vādā, sato vā pana sattassā by this, seven annihilationist-vādā.

There, sato means existing.

Uccheda means interruption.

Vināsa means non-seeing.

Vibhava means cessation of existence.

All these are indeed synonyms for one another.

Diṭṭhadhammanibbānaṃ or, by this, five diṭṭhadhammanibbāna-vādā are told.

There, diṭṭhadhammo means the Dhamma that is directly perceived is said, that is a designation for the self-attainment attained there and there.

Nirvāṇa in this very life, diṭṭhadhammanibbānaṃ, is the subsiding of suffering in this very individuality is the meaning.

Santaṃ vā means existing by way of having perception and so forth with three aspects.

Tīṇi hontī means having perception and so forth, one by way of a peaceful self, the others two, thus three.

Saññī, arogo, ittheke, asaññī, nevasaññīnāsaññī, sato vā pana sattassā, sato, uccheda, vināsa, vibhava, diṭṭhadhammanibbānaṃ, vā, diṭṭhadhammo, santaṃ vā, tīṇi hontī

22. “Therein, bhikkhus, those recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as percipient (saññiṃ attānaṃ) and unimpaired after death, describe such a self to be either material (rūpiṃ), or immaterial (arūpiṃ), or both material and immaterial, or neither material nor immaterial, or percipient of unity (ekattasaññiṃ), or percipient of diversity (nānattasaññiṃ), or percipient of the limited (parittasaññiṃ), or percipient of the immeasurable (appamāṇasaññiṃ). Some assert that this, in one way or another, goes beyond the consciousness-sphere, while others describe the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana) as immeasurable (appamāṇaṃ) and imperturbable (āneñjaṃ).

The Tathāgata, bhikkhus, understands this thus.

Those good recluses and brāhmaṇas who describe the self as percipient and unimpaired after death describe such a self to be either material, or immaterial, or both material and immaterial, or neither material nor immaterial, or percipient of unity, or percipient of diversity, or percipient of the limited, or percipient of the immeasurable, or whether the perceptions of those that are supremely pure, highest, unsurpassed, are of form (rūpa), or of the formless (arūpa), or of unity (ekatta), or of diversity (nānatta). Some assert the base of nothingness, immeasurable and imperturbable, to be the self.

That is conditioned (saṅkhataṃ) and gross (oḷārikaṃ), but there is cessation of formations (saṅkhārānaṃ nirodho). Having known ‘There is this’ seeing the escape from that, the Tathāgata has gone beyond that.

Rūpiṃ or having material form, is [explained as follows:] either by means of fingernail-form or by means of kasiṇa-form.
There, the one who attains takes the kasiṇa-form as self, the logician indeed takes both forms.
Arūpiṃ or formless, is either the sign of the formless attainment or, having set aside the aggregate of perception, those who teach the remaining formless phenomena as self, both the one who attains and the logician teach in this way.
The third view arises by way of a confused grasping, the fourth is simply by way of logical grasping.
In the second tetrad, the first view is described by way of the one who has attained, the second view is by way of the one who has not attained, the third view is by means of slight intoxication or by means of intoxication with liquor or by way of preliminary work on the kasiṇa, the fourth view should be understood as described by way of the extensive kasiṇa.

Etaṃ vā panekesaṃ upātivattata means, in brief, that which is spoken of with the word saññī, the meaning is those who have transcended the aggregate of perception.
Others say there are eight.
That duality will become manifest hereafter.
Here, in this regard, is the meaning in brief—some can transcend these seven or eight perceptions, but some cannot.
There, those who can, they alone are grasped.
For those who are able to transcend, it is just as if, among men who have crossed the Ganges, one might go to a long pond and stand, and another might go beyond that to a great village; just so, some, saying that they have made the dimension of infinite consciousness unmoving, stand, and some [stand in] the dimension of nothingness.
There, to show first the dimension of infinite consciousness, it is said, "Viññāṇakasiṇameke," the consciousness-kasiṇa is one.
Hereafter he will say, "The dimension of nothingness is one."

Tathāgato abhijānāti means, The Tathāgata knows.

27. Thus, having shown the forty-four views relating to the future ( aparantakappike ), now, in order to show the eighteen views relating to the past ( pubbantakappike ), he says, “There are, monks…” and so forth.

Therein, those who conceive and discriminate the past, which is reckoned in terms of past divisions, are called those who relate to the past ( pubbantakappikā ).

Or, “they have the past,” thus they are those who relate to the past.

In this way, the remainder should also be understood according to the method that has been stated previously.

“The self is eternal, and the world is eternal”: having taken one or another among forms etc., as "the self" and "the world," they declare, “It is eternal, immortal, constant, permanent.”

As it is said: "‘Form is the self and the world; it is eternal,’ thus they designate the self and the world," this is the detailed explanation.

The same method applies in the case of non-eternal etc.

Here, by the first assertion, four eternalist views are stated, and by the second assertion, seven annihilationist views.


“But surely these have come below; why are they taken up again here?”

They have come to show that there, in that very place, he dies and is annihilated.

Here, however, one who gains the ability to recall past lives, a theorist, sees the past but not the future; it occurs to him thus: “The self, having come from the past, is annihilated right here; it does not go beyond this.” They are taken up in order to show the meaning of this.

By the third assertion, four partly eternalist views are stated, and by the fourth assertion, four equivocating views are stated.

“Having an end” ( antavā ) means having a limit, bounded, circumscribed.

With regard to one whose kasiṇa has not been developed, having taken that kasiṇa as "the self" and "the world," it occurs to him thus.

The second assertion is stated in dependence on the developed kasiṇa; the third assertion is regarding one whose kasiṇa is developed horizontally but not developed upwards or downwards; the fourth assertion is stated in dependence on reasoning.

The immediately following tetrad is just according to the method stated below.


“Wholly happy" means continuously happy.

This view arises in dependence on those who have gained the ability to recall past lives and on reasoning.

For this view arises in one who has gained the ability to recall past lives, and who, by means of the knowledge of past lives, recollects his birth in a kṣatriya family etc. as being wholly happy.

Similarly, [it arises in] one who has the ability to recall past lives, and who, experiencing present happiness, recollects a similar state of self in seven past lives.

However, in the case of one who reasons, it arises simply through reasoning, in one who is presently endowed with happiness, [thinking], “In the past, I was just like this.”


“Wholly happy” [and] “wholly miserable”: this view does not arise in one who has gained [the ability to recall past lives].

For he is happy only through the bliss of absorption here.

However, in one who is touched by suffering here, and who has the ability to recall past lives, it arises simply through reasoning.

The third view arises in all those whose happiness and suffering are mixed here, and so does the fourth view.

For now, in one who has gained [the ability to recall past lives] and is without suffering or happiness by means of the fourth dhyāna, [and] who recollects only the brahmaloka in which he previously existed as one possessing the fourth dhyāna.

Even in one who has the ability to recall past lives, it is this view which arises in one who is presently in a state of equanimity, in one who recollects the state of equanimity itself, in one who reasons, it is this view which arises in one who is presently in a state of equanimity, [thinking], “In the past, it will be like this.”

To this extent, the four eternalist views, the four partly eternalist views, the four views about the world being finite and infinite, the four equivocating views, and the two views that arise without a cause—these eighteen views relating to the past have been stated.

"Here, O bhikkhus, those ascetics and Brahmins who maintain such a doctrine and view, saying, 'The self and the world are eternal; only this is true, anything else is wrong,' that, apart from faith ( saddhā), apart from approval (ruci), apart from oral tradition (anussava), apart from reasoned cogitation (ākāraparivitakka), apart from reflective acceptance of a view (diṭṭhinijjhānakkhanti), they will have any pure and clear personal knowledge (paccattaṃ ñāṇaṃ) of this – that is impossible.

Since they have no pure and clear personal knowledge (paccattaṃ ñāṇaṃ), even the mere fragmentary knowledge that those good ascetics and Brahmins clarify about their view is declared to be clinging (upādāna).

'That is conditioned (saṅkhataṃ) and gross (oḷārikaṃ), but there is cessation (nirodha) of formations (saṅkhārānaṃ.' Having known ‘There is this’ seeing the escape (nissaraṇa) from that, the Tathāgata has gone beyond that (tadupātivatto)."

28. Now, singling out the view, he said, “Here, bhikkhus,” etc.

There, “personally known knowledge” means direct knowledge (paccakkhañāṇaṃ).

“Perfectly pure” means without defilements (nirupakkilesaṃ).

“Spotless” means radiant (pabhassaraṃ).

Vipassanā-knowledge (vipassanāñāṇaṃ) is spoken of by all the terms.

For the five qualities, beginning with faith (saddhā), exist even in outside (bāhirasamaya) systems, but vipassanā-knowledge exists only in the Teaching (sāsana).

“Merely clarifying the knowledge-portion therein” means just introducing the knowledge section there with "we know this".

"It is called grasping" means that is not knowledge; this is called wrong view (micchādassanaṃ). Therefore, that too is called grasping of views for those venerable ones; this is the meaning.

Even though that which has the mere characteristic of knowing is only a knowledge-portion, even so, because of the continued tendency of that view and because of the condition of grasping, it is indeed grasping (upādāna).

“Having transcended that” means having overcome that view (diṭṭhiṃ atikkanto).

Thus far, the four eternalist views (sassatavādā), the four partial-eternalist views (ekaccasassatikā), the four views concerning the finite and infinite (antānantikā), the four evasive wrigglers (amarāvikkhepikā), the two causally adventitious views (adhiccasamuppannikā), the sixteen perception-theories (saññīvādā), the eight non-perception theories (asaññīvādā), the eight neither-perception-nor-non-perception theories (nevasaññīnāsaññīvādā), the seven annihilationist views (ucchedavādā), the five here-and-now-Nibbāna views (diṭṭhadhammanibbānavādā)—these sixty-two views that appear in the Brahma-net (brahmajāla) Sūtra have been discussed.

However, when the Brahma-net (brahmajāla) Sūtra has been spoken of, this sutta is as good as unspoken.

Why? Because here, self-view (sakkāyadiṭṭhi) comes in excess of that.

However, when this has been spoken of, the Brahma-net (brahmajāla) has been spoken of.

“Here, bhikkhus, some recluse or brāhmin, with the relinquishing of views about the past (pūrvānta) and the future (aparantā), through complete lack of resolve upon the fetters of sensual pleasure (kāmasaṃyojana), and with the surmounting of the rapture (prīti) of seclusion (praviveka), unworldly pleasure, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling (adukkhamasukhā vedanā), regards himself thus: ‘I am at peace (śānto’hamasmi), I have attained Nirvāṇa (nirvṛto’hamasmi), I am without clinging (anupādāno’hamasmi)’".

“The Tathāgata, bhikkhus, understands this.”

“This good recluse or brāhmin, with the relinquishing of views about the past and the future… regards himself thus: “I am at peace, I have attained Nirvāṇa, I am without clinging.” Certainly this venerable one asserts the way directed to Nirvāṇa.

Yet this good recluse or brāhmin still clings, clinging either to a view about the past or to a view about the future or to a fetter of sensual pleasure or to the rapture of seclusion or to unworldly pleasure or to neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling.

And when this venerable one regards himself thus: “I am at peace, I have attained Nirvāṇa, I am without clinging,” that too is declared to be clinging on the part of this good recluse or brāhmin.

That is conditioned (saṃkhata) and gross (oḷārika), but there is cessation (nirodha) of formations (saṅkhārāna). Having known ‘There is this’ seeing the escape (nissaraṇa) from that, the Tathāgata has gone beyond that.

“Bhikkhus, this unsurpassed state of sublime peace (śāntivarapada) has been fully awakened (abhisambuddha) by the Tathāgata, that is, liberation (vimokkha) through not clinging (anupādā), by understanding as they actually are (yathābhūtaṃ) the origination (samudaya), the disappearance (atthaṅgama), the gratification (assāda), the danger (ādīnava), and the escape (nissaraṇa) in the case of the six bases of contact (ṣaṇṇāṃ sparśāyatanānāṃ).

The Blessed One said this. Elated, those bhikkhus delighted in the Blessed One’s statement.

The Pañcattaya Sutta, the second, is finished.

33. "`I am without grasping (anupādānohamasmī)`" means that I am the one being disciplined (niggahaṇo ahamasmi).

"`Conducive to nirvāṇa (nibbānasappāyaṃ)`" means helpful (upakārabhūtaṃ) and suitable (sappāyaṃ) for nirvāṇa.

Now, the vision of the path (maggadassanaṃ) arises everywhere upon the drying up of craving (nikantiyā sukkhāpitāya), so how can this be called a practice conducive to nirvāṇa (nibbānassa upakārapaṭipadā)? It is called a practice conducive to help because it arises everywhere by way of non-grasping (anupādiyanavasena), by way of non-seizing (aggaṇhanavasena).

"`Disparages (abhivadatī)`" means to disparage (upavadati) with pride (abhimānena).

"`Views about the past (pubbantānudiṭṭhiṃ)`" means the eighteen kinds of views about the past (aṭṭhārasavidhampi pubbantānudiṭṭhiṃ).

"`Views about the future (aparantānudiṭṭhiṃ)`" means the forty-four kinds of views about the future (catucattārīsavidhampi aparantānudiṭṭhiṃ).

"`Signifies clinging (upādānamakkhāyati)`" means it signifies clinging to views (diṭṭhupādānaṃ akkhāyati), since the grasping of "`I am (ahamasmīti)`" is included in the category of self-view (sakkāyadiṭṭhipariyāpannattā).

"`The unsurpassed state of peace (santivarapadaṃ)`" means the peaceful (santaṃ) unsurpassed state (uttamapadaṃ), because of the subsiding of defilements (vūpasantakilesattā).

"`The six sense-bases (channaṃ phassāyatanānaṃ)`" means that the Blessed One (bhagavatā) showed nirvāṇa (nibbānaṃ dassitaṃ) by rejecting (paṭikkhepena) two bases (dvinnāṃ āyatanānaṃ) in this statement (ettha): "`Where (yattha) the eye (cakkhu) ceases (nirujjhati) and the perception of forms (rūpasaññā) ceases (nirujjhati), that is the base to be known (so āyatano veditabbo).`"

The unsurpassed state of peace (santivarapadaṃ), the six sense-bases (channaṃ phassāyatanānaṃ).

"`Where water (āpo) and earth (pathavī), fire (tejo) and wind (vāyo) do not find a footing (na gādhati);

from there the streams turn back (ato sarā nivattanti), here the cycle (vaṭṭaṃ) does not turn (na vattati);

here name (nāma) and form (rūpa), cease without remainder (asesaṃ uparujjhati).`"

(Saṃyutta Nikāya 1.27)

Here (ettha), nirvāṇa (nibbānaṃ dassitaṃ) is shown by rejecting (paṭikkhepena) formations (saṅkhāra).

"`Where (kattha) water (āpo) and earth (pathavī), fire (tejo) and wind (vāyo) do not find a footing (na gādhati);

where (kattha) long and short (dīghañca rassañca), subtle and gross (aṇuṃ thūlaṃ), beautiful and ugly (subhāsubhaṃ);

where (kattha) name (nāma) and form (rūpa), cease without remainder (asesaṃ uparujjhati).`"

(Dīgha Nikāya 1.498)

There (tatra) is the explanation (veyyākaraṇaṃ bhavati):

"`Consciousness (viññāṇaṃ) is invisible (anidassanaṃ), endless (anantaṃ), radiant in every way (sabbato pabha).`"

Here (ettha), nirvāṇa (nibbānaṃ dassitaṃ) is shown by rejecting (paṭikkhepena) formations (saṅkhāra). In this sutta (imasmiṃ pana sutte), it is shown by rejecting the six bases (chaāyatanapaṭikkhepena dassitaṃ). And elsewhere (aññattha ca), only nirvāṇa (nibbānameva dassitaṃ) is shown as "`release through non-grasping (anupādāvimokkho),"` but here (idha pana) it is the attainment of the fruit of arahantship (arahattaphalasamāpatti). The rest (sesaṃ) is clear (uttānameva) throughout (sabbattha).

Release through non-grasping (anupādāvimokkho).
