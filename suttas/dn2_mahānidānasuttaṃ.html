Thus have I heard.

On one occasion the Blessed One was living among the Kurus, where there was a town of the Kurus named Kammāsadhamma.

Then the Venerable Ānanda approached the Blessed One and after paying homage to him sat down at one side.

Seated at one side, the Venerable Ānanda said to the Blessed One: “It’s incredible, venerable sir, it’s amazing, venerable sir, how deep is this dependent arising (*paṭiccasamuppāda*), and how deep its appearance. Yet it seems to me as plain as can be!”

The Buddha: “Don’t say that, Ānanda. Don’t say that.

Deep is this dependent co-arising (*paṭiccasamuppāda*), and deep its appearance.

It’s because of not understanding and not penetrating this *dhamma* that this generation is like a tangled skein, a knotted ball of thread, like matted rushes and reeds, and does not go beyond *saṃsāra* with its plane of misery, unfortunate destinations, and lower realms.

95. Thus.

Having heard thus, I recall the *Mahānidānasutta* in Kurus.

Herein is an elucidation of the terms that are not clear.

"Living in the Kurus" means that the Kuru princes, whose residence, even though a single district, is called "Kurus" by common parlance.

In that Kuru district.

The teachers of the Aṭṭhakathā say: At the time of Mandhātu, people in the three continents, having heard that "Jambudīpa is the birthplace of Buddhas, Paccekabuddhas, great disciples of the Buddhas, Universal Monarchs and other excellent beings, an excellent and exceedingly pleasant continent," came to Jambudīpa with King Mandhātu Cakkavatti, who was touring the four continents preceded by the Wheel Jewel.

Then the king asked the leader of the army: "Is there a place more delightful than the human world?"

Why do you speak thus, O god? Do you not see the power of the moon and sun? Is their place not more delightful than this?" The king, with the Wheel Jewel in front, went there.

The four great kings, having heard, "King Mandhātu has arrived," and that "the king is mighty and powerful, and cannot be resisted by war," surrendered their kingdoms.

He took it and asked again, "Is there a place more delightful than this?"

Then they told him of the Tāvatiṃsa heaven.

"The Tāvatiṃsa heaven, O god, is more delightful than this.

There, these four great kings are the attendants of Sakka, the king of the gods, standing guard at the gate. Sakka, the king of the gods, is mighty and powerful. These are his places of enjoyment: the Vejayanta palace, a thousand leagues high; the Sudhammā assembly hall of the gods, five hundred leagues high; the Vejayanta chariot, two and a half hundred leagues, and the elephant Erāvaṇa, Nandanavana adorned with thousands of divine trees, Cittalatāvana, Phārusakavana, Missakavana, Pāricchattaka koviḷāra a hundred leagues high, and below it, the Paṇḍukambalasilā, sixty leagues long and fifty leagues wide, with a color like the jayakusuma flower, and because of its softness, half of Sakka's body sinks into it when he sits down."

Having heard that, the king, wishing to go there, set the Wheel Jewel in motion.

It stood in the sky with its fourfold army.

Then, from the midst of the two heavens, the Wheel Jewel descended and stood on the earth with the fourfold army headed by the leader of the army.

The king went alone to the Tāvatiṃsa heaven.

Sakka, having heard, "Mandhātu has arrived," went to meet him and said, "Welcome, great king, it is your own, great king, instruct, great king," and having said this, he divided the kingdom into two parts, along with the dancers, and gave one part.

As soon as the king was established in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven, his human nature disappeared, and his divine nature appeared.

It is said that when he was sitting with Sakka on the Paṇḍukambalasilā, a difference was seen with the blink of an eye.

Not noticing that, the gods are confused by the difference between Sakka and him.

Enjoying divine prosperity there, until thirty-six Sakkas had arisen and passed away, having ruled the kingdom, not satisfied with desires, having departed from there, he died in his own park, his body touched by wind and sun.

When the Wheel Jewel was again established on the earth, the leader of the army had Mandhātu's sandals inscribed on a golden plate, saying, "This is Mandhātu's kingdom," and he ruled the kingdom.

Those people who had come from the three continents, unable to return, approached the leader of the army and begged, "O god, we have come by the power of the king, now we cannot return, give us a place to live."

He gave each of them a district.

There, the district inhabited by people who came from Pubbavideha obtained the name "Videharaṭṭha" by that former designation, the district inhabited by people who came from Aparagoyāna obtained the name "Aparantajanapada," the district inhabited by people who came from Uttarakuru obtained the name "Kururaṭṭha", but many, taking villages and towns, are referred to in the plural.

Therefore it is said, "Living in the Kurus."

Kammāsadhamma is the name of the Kuru's town, and some interpret the meaning of Kammāsadhamma with the letter dha changed to da.

Kammāsa is tamed here, therefore Kammāsadammo.

Kammāsa means Kammāsapāda is called porisāda.
It is said that in his foot, at the place pierced by a thorn, a wound grew and healed, becoming like variegated wood. Therefore, it became known as *kammāsapāda*.

And he, being restrained from the state of being a man-eater in that place, was forbidden. By whom? By the Great Being, the *Bodhisatta*.

In which *jātaka* is it? Some say in the *Mahāsutasomajātaka*. But the elders say it is in the *Jayaddisajātake*. Then the *kammāsapāda* was subdued by the Great Being.

As it is said: *kammāsadhammaṃ* is the name of a market town of the Kurus; *kammāsadhamma, kammāsadammo.*

When I was the son of Jayaddisa, the Atraja of the lord of the Pañcāla kingdom, abandoning life, I freed my father; and I pleased the *kammāsapāda*.”

Some, however, explain the meaning with the letter “dha.” It is said that the virtuous practice of the Kurus, the *kuruvattadhamma*, became stained *kammāso jāto* in the Kurūraṭṭha. Therefore, that place is called *kammāsadhamma*, because the *dhamma* arose there as a stain *kammāso ettha dhammo jāto*.

The market town established there also had that very name. Why was it not said with a locative? Because of the lack of opportunity.

It is said that in that market town, there was no monastery where the Blessed One could stay. But going away from the market town, in a certain delightful place full of water, there was a large forest grove. There the Blessed One stayed, making that market town a pasture village.

Therefore, the meaning here should be understood thus: “He dwells in the Kurus; *kammāsadhammaṃ* is the name of a market town of the Kurus, making that a pasture village.”

Some say *āyasmā* is a beloved word, a respectful word. *Ānanda* is the name of that elder. *Ekamanta* is a designation of the neuter gender in thought: “The uneven moon and sun revolve,” and so forth.

Therefore, just as one sitting is sitting to one side, so one should sit down; thus the meaning here should be seen. Or, in the sense of place, this is a word of use: *nisīdī* he approached.

The wise, having approached a revered person, sit down to one side with skill in seating. And he was one of them, therefore he sat down to one side.

How, indeed, is one sitting, sitting to one side? By avoiding six faults in sitting. Namely: too far, too near, upwind, elevated ground, too directly facing, too far behind.

One sitting too far away, if he wants to speak, must speak loudly. One sitting too near causes crowding. One sitting upwind bothers with body odor. One sitting in an elevated place…
He makes a show of disrespect.
If someone is sitting too close and wishes to see, one should strike eye with eye and see.
If someone is sitting too far back and wishes to see, one should turn the neck and see.
Therefore, he too, having circumambulated the Blessed One three times, respectfully venerated him, and avoiding these six faults of sitting, Ánanda, the treasurer of the Dharma, sat down in a place facing the right knee-circle, having entered into the midst of the six-colored rays of the Buddha, as if plunging into a pool of bright lac-dye, as if covering himself with a golden cloth, as if entering the midst of a canopy of red lotus garlands.
Thus it was said: “He sat down at one side."

At what time, and for what reason, did the venerable Āyasmā approach the Blessed One? It was in the evening, for the purpose of asking a question about the principle of conditionality.
It is said that on that day the Venerable Ánanda, in order to benefit families, walked for alms in the village of Kammásadhamma, as if depositing a bundle of a thousand coins at the door of each house. When he returned from his almsround, he did his duties to the Teacher. When the Teacher entered the Fragrant Cottage, he venerated him, went to his own day-quarters, and did his duties to his own pupils. After their departure, he swept the day-quarters, spread out his leather mat, took water from the water-pot, cooled his hands and feet with the water, and, sitting down cross-legged, attained the fruition-attainment of stream-entry.
Then, at the predetermined time, emerging from the attainment, he immersed his mind in the principle of conditionality.
He explored the twelve-factored principle of conditionality three times, first starting from the beginning thus: “With ignorance as condition, volitional formations come to be,” and working down to the end, then working from the end back to the beginning, and then working from both ends to the middle and from the middle to both ends.
As he was thus exploring, the principle of conditionality became manifest to him, and stood before him as clear as clear can be.

Then he thought: “This principle of conditionality is said by all the Buddhas to be ‘deep and to appear deep.’ Yet to me, a disciple established in partial knowledge, it stands revealed, manifest, and plain. Is it only to me that it stands as clear as clear can be, or to others as well?” Then it occurred to him: “Let me take this question and ask the Blessed One. Surely the Blessed One, after clarifying this point, will deliver a discourse like one who lifts Mount Sineru with a clod of earth, and make it clear to me."
For there are four areas where the thundering of the Buddhas becomes great, their knowledge enters its proper field, the greatness of the Buddha-knowledge is discerned, and their teaching becomes deep, stamped with the three characteristics, and connected with emptiness, namely, the promulgation of the Vinaya, the classification of the diversity of planes, the exposition of the principle of conditionality, and the classification of the diversity of tenets.”

Though he ordinarily approached the Exalted One a hundred or a thousand times a day, he never approached without a cause or reason. So that day he rose up from his day-quarters, shook out his leather mat and, taking it along, in the evening he approached the Buddha with his question in mind, thinking: “I will confront the elephant-like Buddha and hear his trumpeting cry of knowledge. I will confront the lion-like Buddha and hear his lion’s roar of knowledge. I will confront the Buddha-thoroughbred and see his pacing gait of knowledge.”
Thus it was said above: “He approached in the evening, for the purpose of asking a question about the principle of conditionality.”

As for *yāva gambhīro*, here the word *yāva* indicates exceeding measure; *gambhīro* exceeds measure; the meaning is exceedingly deep.
As for *gambhīrāvabhāso*, being deep, it shines forth; the meaning is, it appears.
For some water might be shallow yet appear deep, like stagnant water having a dark color because of rotting leaves, etc.
This might be only knee-deep, yet appear to be a hundred fathoms.
Some water might be deep yet appear shallow, like the clear water of the Maṇigaṅga.
This might be a hundred fathoms deep yet seem to be knee-deep.
Other water might be shallow and appear shallow, like the water in a pot, etc.
And still other water might be deep and appear deep, like the water in the ocean at the foot of Mount Sineru.
Thus water can be described in four ways.
But this is not so in regard to dependent arising.
This can be described in only one way: “it is deep and appears deep.”
But though this is so, the Venerable Ánanda says: “To myself it seems as clear as clear can be. How wonderful and marvellous, venerable sir!”
Thus revealing his own astonishment, he asked a question, sat down, and became silent.

The Exalted One, hearing his statement, thought to himself: “Ánanda says that a matter which belongs to the domain of the Buddhas is clear to himself. This is like stretching out one’s hand to take hold of the highest plane of existence, like trying to cut through Mount Sineru and remove its core, like wanting to cross the ocean without a ship, or like turning over the earth and trying to take its nutritive essence.
Let me point out its depth to him.” Then he said: “Do not say so, Ánanda!”
Do not say so! There, "Do not say so!" means *ha*-kāra is merely a particle.

The meaning is: Do not say thus.

And the Blessed One says "Do not say so, Ānanda!" both to extol the Venerable Ānanda and to restrain him.

Extolling.

There, extolling – Ānanda, you have great wisdom (*mahāpañño*) and clear knowledge (*visadañāṇo*). Therefore, dependent arising (*paṭiccasamuppāda*), though deep (*gambhīro*), seems clear (*uttānaka*) to you.

But for others it cannot be characterized as clear (*uttānaka*); rather, it is deep (*gambhīra*) and it appears deep.

Four similes are given to illustrate this.

A well-trained champion wrestler, it is said, who had been nourished with nutritious food for six months and had exercised with a wrestler’s stone, was going to the fighting arena during a carnival when they showed him a wrestler’s stone along the way. He asked: “What is that?"

“A wrestler’s stone.”

Bring it here.

“We cannot lift it,” they said. Then he went over to it himself. Saying “What is so heavy with this?" he lifted two such stones with two hands, threw them away like balls, and continued on his way.

Though the stone was light for the wrestler, it could not be called light for the others.

As the wrestler had been nourished with nutritious food for six months, so the Venerable Ānanda had been endowed with the aspiration for 100,000 aeons. As for the wrestler with his great strength the stone was light, but not for others, so for the Elder with his great wisdom dependent arising (*paṭiccasamuppāda*) was clear (*uttāno*), but it could not be called clear (*uttānoti*) for others.

In the great ocean there is a fish called Timi, which is two *yojana*s and one hundred *yojana*s long; Timiṅgala is three *yojana*s and one hundred *yojana*s long; Timipiṅgala is four *yojana*s and one hundred *yojana*s long; and Timirapiṅgala is five *yojana*s and one hundred *yojana*s long. Ānanda, Timinanda, Ajjhāroha, and Mahātimi are four thousand *yojana*s in length.

There, they are indicated by just Timirapiṅgala.

When it shakes its right fin, it is said, it stirs up the water in an area of 500 *yojana*s.

Similarly for its left fin.

Its tail, and its head.

But when it plays about in the water—shaking both fins, striking the water with its tail, ducking its head in and out—it churns up the water in an area of 700 or 800 *yojana*s until it becomes just like water boiling in a pot on the stove. Even water in a region 300 *yojana*s deep cannot cover its back.

This fish might say: “They are always saying that the ocean is deep. How can it be deep? I can’t even find enough water to cover my back.”

For the Timirapiṅgala, with its vast body, the ocean might be shallow (*uttānoti*), but it cannot be called shallow (*uttānoti*) for the smaller fish. Similarly, for the Elder with his vast knowledge dependent arising (*paṭiccasamuppāda*) might be clear (*uttānoti*), but it cannot be called clear (*uttānoti*) for others.

The royal Supaóóa bird is 250 *yojana*s long, his right wing fifty *yojana*s, as is his left wing, his tail sixty *yojana*s, his neck thirty *yojana*s, his beak nine *yojana*s, and his feet twelve *yojana*s.

When he starts to stir up a wind even an area 700 or 800 *yojana*s is not enough.

He might say: “They are always saying that space (*ākāso*) is infinite (*ananto*). How can it be infinite (*anantatā*)? I can’t even find enough room to stretch out my wings and stir up a wind.”

For the royal Supaóóa bird, with his vast body, space (*ākāso*) might be limited (*parittoti*), but it cannot be called limited (*parittoti*) for smaller birds. Similarly for the Elder with his vast knowledge dependent arising (*paṭiccasamuppāda*) might be clear (*uttānoti*), but it cannot be called clear (*uttānoti*) for others.

Rāhu, the lord of the titans, is 4,800 *yojana*s tall from his feet to the end of his hair.

His armspan is 1,200 *yojana*s.

His thickness is 600 *yojana*s.

His palms and soles are 300 *yojana*s, so too his mouth.

Each segment of his fingers is fifty *yojana*s, as is the distance between his eyebrows.

and his head 900 *yojanas*.
When he enters the ocean, the deepest water comes only up to his knees.
He might say: “They are always saying that the ocean is deep. How can it be deep? I can’t even find water deep enough to cover my knees.”
For Rāhu, with his vast body, the ocean might be shallow, but it cannot be called shallow for others. Similarly, for the Elder with his vast knowledge, dependent origination might be obvious, but it cannot be called obvious for others.
Referring to this matter, the Blessed One said, "Do not speak like that, Ānanda; do not speak like that, Ānanda."
For the Elder, dependent origination, although profound, becomes clear through four causes.
What are the four? Through the fulfillment of previous support (*pubbūpanissayasampatti*), dwelling in a place of pilgrimage (*titthavāsena*), being a stream-enterer (*sotāpannatāya*), and great learning (*bahussutabhāvenāti*).

The Story of the Fulfillment of Previous Support

It is said that one hundred thousand eons ago, the teacher Padumuttara arose in the world.
His city was named Haṃsavatī, his father was a king named Ānanda, his mother was a goddess named Sumedhā, and the bodhisattva was named Prince Uttara.
On the day of his son’s birth, having renounced the great renunciation, gone forth, and successively attained omniscience while applying himself to effort, having uttered the joyous exclamation "Through many births I wandered in *saṃsāra*," having spent seven weeks on the *bodhi* throne, he extended his foot, [saying,] “I will establish [my teaching] on the earth.”
Then, having broken through the earth, a great lotus arose.
Its stalk supports were ninety cubits, its filament thirty cubits, its calyx twelve cubits, and the pollen was the size of nine pots.
The Teacher was forty-eight cubits in height.
The distance between his two arms was eighteen cubits, his forehead five cubits, and his hands and feet eleven cubits.
With a foot eleven cubits long, the pollen, the size of nine pots, from the calyx twelve cubits across, rose up and completely covered an area forty-eight cubits in extent, like *manosilā* powder that had been scattered.
From that time on, the Blessed One was known as Padumuttara.
Devila and Sujāta were his two chief disciples.
Amitā and Asamā were his two chief female disciples.
Sumana was his personal attendant.

The Profundity of Dependent Origination

There, dependent origination is called profound in four ways: profundity of meaning (attha), profundity of the Dhamma, profundity of teaching (desanā), and profundity of realization (paṭivedha).

There, the profound meaning is that old age and death are born from the condition of birth...and the profound meaning is that volitional formations are born from the condition of ignorance.

The profound Dhamma is that ignorance is the condition for volitional formations...and birth is the condition for old age and death.

The profound teaching is that in some suttas, dependent origination is taught in forward order, in some in reverse order, in some in both forward and reverse order, in some beginning from the middle in forward or reverse or both forward and reverse order, in some three connections and four groupings, in some two connections and three groupings, in some one connection and two groupings.

Furthermore, the profound realization is that ignorance is the state of not knowing, not seeing, and not penetrating the truth; the profound volitional formations are the state of constructing, planning, being colored, and being uncolored; the profound consciousness is the state of emptiness, inactivity, non-transference, rebirth, and manifestation; the profound name and form is the state of simultaneous arising, separateness, inseparateness, naming, and forming; the profound six sense bases are the state of being a lord, world, door, field, and object; the profound contact
being in contact, collision, association, and aggregation; being the basis of feeling, which experiences the taste of the object, pleasure, pain, and neutrality; being the basis of craving, which delights in, is attached to, flows like a river, is a thirst, an ocean of craving that is difficult to fill; being the basis of grasping, which is taking, holding, clinging, adhering, and difficult to overcome; being the basis of existence, which throws [beings] into the accumulation, conditioning, womb, destiny, duration, and abodes; being the basis of birth, which is being born, completely born, descent, production, manifestation; [and] being the basis of old age and death, which is decay, decline, disintegration, and change—[all this] is profound.

Thus, the nature of ignorance, etc., by which penetration ignorance, etc., are fully penetrated in terms of their essential characteristics—this is known as *depth of penetration*.

The Explanation of Depreciation

Now, what was said, “Depreciating,” there the meaning is this: Ānanda, do not say, “But it appears to me as plain as plain can be!”

If it appears to you as plain as plain can be, why have you not become a stream-enterer on your own, by means of your own nature, but you penetrate the path of stream-entry only after having stood in the method given by me?

Ānanda, this *nirvāṇa* itself is profound, but the principle of conditionality has become spread out for you. So why do you not realize the fruit of once-returning by completely uprooting these four defilements: the coarse bond of sensual lust, the bond of aversion, the underlying tendency to coarse sensual lust, [and] the underlying tendency to aversion? Why do you not realize the fruit of non-returning by completely uprooting these four defilements that are associated with subtle sensations? Why do you not realize arahantship by completely uprooting these eight defilements: the five bonds beginning with lust for form, the underlying tendency to lust for existence, the underlying tendency to conceit, [and] the underlying tendency to ignorance?

Why have you not penetrated the knowledge of a disciple, like Sāriputta and Moggallāna, who fulfilled the perfections for one incalculable period and one hundred thousand aeons? Why have you not penetrated the knowledge of a *pratyekabuddha*, like the *pratyekabuddhas*, who fulfilled the perfections for two incalculable periods and one hundred thousand aeons? Or, if this appears to you as fully spread out in every way, then why do you not realize the omniscient knowledge of a Buddha, like the Buddhas, who fulfilled the perfections for four, eight, or sixteen incalculable periods and one hundred thousand aeons? Are you indifferent to these distinguished achievements? Look how much you have done wrong! You, a disciple abiding in limited knowledge, say of the extremely deep principle of conditionality, “It appears spread out to me!” This statement of yours is contrary to the explanation of the Buddhas. It is not proper for such a bhikkhu to utter something contrary to the explanation of the Buddhas.

Indeed, Ānanda, have not four incalculable periods and one hundred thousand aeons passed by for me just while striving to penetrate this principle of conditionality? And there is no gift that has not been given by me, no perfection that has not been fulfilled, for the sake of penetrating the principle of conditionality.

When I was about to penetrate the principle of conditionality, this great earth did not tremble even by two finger-breadths when I was dispersing the army of Māra as though it were powerless, nor when I was developing the recollection of former lives in the first watch of the night, nor when I was developing the divine eye in the middle watch of the night.

Thus, when asked by a wise person, Ānanda, a learned individual, as when it is said, "the soul (*jīva*) is the body (*śarīra*)," silence should be maintained because the question should be set aside, or it should be said, "This has not been explained by the Tathāgata." Without proceeding in this way, as when it is said, "The eye (*cakkhu*) is eternal (*sassataṃ*) or non-eternal (*asassata*)," it should be said unequivocally that it is non-eternal (*asassataṃ*). Thus, it should be stated unequivocally that it is.

Furthermore, through what condition [do] aging and death (*jarāmaraṇa*) [arise]? What is that condition called, through which aging and death (*jarāmaraṇa*) arise? When asked, it should be said, "With birth (*jāti*) as condition, [there is] aging and death (*jarāmaraṇa*)." Thus, it should be said that this is the meaning.

The same method [applies] to all the remaining cases.

With name and form (*nāmarūpa*) as condition, contact (*phassa*). Now, because when it is said, "With the six sense bases (*saḷāyatana*) as condition," only the six kinds of resultant contacts, namely eye-contact and so on, are included, but here, wishing to show the distinct conditionally arisen contacts, whether included or not included by this phrase "With the six sense bases (*saḷāyatana*) as condition," and also another distinctive condition for contact (*phassa*) different from the six sense bases (*saḷāyatana*), therefore, it should be understood that [this] was said.

What has the Exalted One explained in this section? The origin (*nidāna*) of the conditions has been explained.

Because this *sutta*, having made the conditions free from knots and tangles, has been explained, it is called the Great Origin (*mahānidāna*).

With name and form (*nāmarūpa*) as condition, contact (*phassa*). Great Origin (*mahānidāna*).

Now, to show how those conditions are true, not untrue, and not otherwise, [the text] begins by saying, “*jāti* (birth) is the condition for aging and death,” and so forth.

There, "*pariyāyena*," means by way of *kāraṇa* (cause).

"*sabbena sabbaṃ sabbathāsabbaṃ*"—this is a pair of particles.

Its meaning is: "In every way, by every nature, if all *jāti* were not to exist."

In the case of *bhava* (becoming) and so on, the meaning should be understood in this way.

"*kassacī*" means this is an indefinite expression, [referring] to anyone whatsoever among the *deva*s (gods) and so forth.

"*kimhicī*" this too is an indefinite expression, [referring to] wherever whatsoever among the nine realms of existence (*kāma-bhava* etc.).

"*seyyathidaṃ*" is a particle used to divide the meaning of what has been indefinitely stated; its meaning is: "What was said, 'to anyone, anything,' I will divide its meaning for you."

Then, dividing it, [the text] says, "either for the *devatā*s (gods) or for the state of being a *deva*," and so forth.

There, "*devānaṃ vā devattāyā*" means that this *jāti* of aggregates for the *devatā*s is for the state of being a *deva*, by which *jāti* of aggregates the *devatā*s are called "*devā*s."

If *jāti* were not to exist in every way, the meaning should be understood in all instances in this way.

Here, "*devā*" means gods by rebirth (*upapattidevā*).

"*gandhabbā*" means deities dwelling in roots, tree-trunks, etc. (*mūlakhandhādīsu adhivatthadevatāva*).

"*yakkhā*" means non-humans (*amanussā*).

"*bhūtā*" means whatever beings are generated (*ye keci nibbattasattā*).

"*tadatthāyā*" means for the state of being a *deva*, *gandhabba*, and so forth.

"*jātinirodhā*" means the cessation of *jāti*, which is the non-existence of *jāti*.

*hetū* (cause), etc., are all synonyms for *kāraṇa* (cause).

For, because a *kāraṇa* diminishes and proceeds for its own fruit, therefore it is called “*hetu*”.

Since it brings forth that fruit, as if commanding, “Come, take it!” therefore it is called *nidāna*.

Since the fruit originates and arises from it, and since it proceeds in dependence on that, therefore it is called *samudaya* (origin) and *paccaya* (condition).

This method [of explanation] applies everywhere.

Moreover, "*yadidaṃ jātī*" here, "*yadidaṃ*" is a particle.

Its meaning should be understood in all instances in accordance with the gender.

Here, however, “*yā esā jātī*” this is its meaning.

To this extent, the Blessed One, having shown the previous craving that is the root of the round [of rebirths] ( *vaṭṭamūlabhūtaṃ purimataṇhaṃ*), now, as if striking [someone] on the back or grabbing [him] by the hair and pulling [him] away while he wails, the Blessed One pulls the teaching down from its usual sequence and speaks the present passage to show, by way of nine terms, obsessional craving (*samudācārataṇhaṃ*): "Thus indeed, Ānanda, craving ( *taṇhā*) is in dependence upon feeling ( *vedanaṃ paṭicca*)," and so forth.

Therein, craving (*taṇhā*): There are two kinds of craving ( *taṇhā*): acquisitive craving ( *esanataṇhā*) and possessive craving ( *esitataṇhā*).

Acquisitive craving (*esanataṇhā*): Acquisitive craving is the craving ( *taṇhā*) because of which one travels along rough and rugged paths (*ajapathasaṅkupathādīni paṭipajjitvā*) seeking wealth (*bhoge esati gavesati*).

Possessive craving (*esitataṇhā*): Possessive craving ( *esitataṇhā*) is the craving ( *taṇhā*) for those things which have been sought after and obtained (*esu esitesu gavesitesu paṭiladdhesu*).

Both of these are designations for obsessional craving (*samudācārataṇhā*).

Therefore, this twofold craving (*duvidhāpesā*) arises in dependence upon feeling (*vedanaṃ paṭicca taṇhā*).

Pursuit (*pariyesanā*): “Pursuit” (*pariyesanā*) is the pursuit of objects such as visible forms (*rūpādiārammaṇa*), etc., and it exists when there is craving ( *taṇhāya sati hoti*).

Gain (*lābho*): “Gain” (*lābho*) is the obtaining of objects such as visible forms (*rūpādiārammaṇa paṭilābho*), etc., and it exists when there is pursuit ( *pariyesanāya sati hoti*).

Decision-making (*vinicchayo*): Decision-making (*vinicchayo*), however, is fourfold by way of knowledge ( *ñāṇa*), craving ( *taṇhā*), views ( *diṭṭhi*) and thought ( *vitakka*).

Therein, “One should know how to decide on happiness. Knowing how to decide on happiness, one should be devoted to inner happiness” ( *sukhavinicchayaṃ jaññā, sukhavinicchayaṃ ñatvā ajjhattaṃ sukhamanuyuñjeyyā*): This is decision-making as knowledge ( *ñāṇavinicchayo*).

“Decision-making” ( *vinicchayo*): There are two kinds of decision-making ( *vinicchayā*): decision-making as craving ( *taṇhāvinicchayo*) and decision-making as views ( *diṭṭhivinicchayo*).

Eight hundred craving-related wanderings that have thus come are decision-making as craving (*taṇhāvinicchayo*). Sixty-two views are decision-making as views (*diṭṭhivinicchayo*).

“Desire, lord of gods, has its source in thought” ( *chando kho, devānaminda, vitakkanidāno*): Since it is said “Desire, lord of gods, has its source in thought”, in this sutta here “decision-making” ( *vinicchayo*) is intended as thought ( *vitakkoyeva āgato*).

For having gained something ( *lābhaṃ labhitvā*), one thinks of it as desirable or undesirable ( *iṭṭhāniṭṭhaṃ*), beautiful or ugly ( *sundarāsundarañca*), and then decides ( *vinicchināti*): "This much will be for visible forms ( *rūpārammaṇatthāya*), this much for sounds, etc. ( *saddādiārammaṇatthāya*). This is for me ( *mayhaṃ*), this for others ( *parassa*). This I will use ( *paribhuñjissāmi*), this I will keep ( *nidahissāmī*)."

Thus it is said: “In dependence upon gain there is decision-making” ( *lābhaṃ paṭicca vinicchayo*).

Desire and lust (*chandarāgo*): Because of unwholesome thoughts ( *akusalavitakkena*), weak lust (*dubbalarāgo*) and strong lust ( *balavarāgo*) arise towards the object thought about ( *vitakkitavatthusmiṃ*). This here is craving (*taṇhā*).

Desire (*chando*): “Desire” ( *chando*) is a designation for weak lust (*dubbalarāgassa adhivacanaṃ*).

Attachment (*ajjhosāna*): “Attachment” (*ajjhosāna*) is strong conviction of “I” ( *ahaṃ*) and “mine” ( *mamanti balavasanniṭṭhānaṃ*).

Grasping (*pariggaho*): “Grasping” (*pariggaho*) is the act of grasping by way of craving ( *taṇhā*) and views ( *diṭṭhi*) (*pariggahaṇakaraṇaṃ*).

Avarice (*macchariya*): “Avarice” (*macchariya*) is intolerance of others sharing in prosperity (*parehi sādhāraṇabhāvassa asahanatā*).

Therefore, the ancients say the following about its meaning ( *vacanatthaṃ vadanti*): "Let this wonder be for me alone ( *idaṃ acchariyaṃ mayhameva hotu*), may this wonder not be for others ( *mā aññesaṃ acchariyaṃ hotūti pavattattā*)." Because it is so inclined, it is called avarice ( *macchariyanti vuccatī*).

Protection (*ārakkho*): "Protection" (*ārakkho*) is guarding ( *rakkhaṇaṃ*) well by way of closing doors, covering containers, hiding treasures, and so forth ( *dvārapidahanamañjūsagopanādivasena suṭṭhu*).

Because it superintends, it is superintendence (*adhikaraṇaṃ*): this is a name for cause (*kāraṇassetaṃ nāmaṃ*).

Superintendence of protection ( *ārakkhādhikaraṇa*): this is a neuter verbal noun ( *bhāvanapuṃsakaṃ*); its meaning ( *attho*) is the cause of protection ( *ārakkhahetūti*).

The taking up of clubs ( *daṇḍassa ādānaṃ*) to restrain others ( *paranisedhanatthaṃ*) in cases of using clubs and so forth ( *daṇḍādānādīsu*) is the taking up of clubs ( *daṇḍādānaṃ*).

The taking up of weapons ( *satthassa ādānaṃ*) such as swords ( *dhārādino*) is the taking up of weapons ( *satthādānaṃ*).

Quarrel (*kalaho*): "Quarrel" (*kalaho*) is either a bodily quarrel ( *kāyakalahopi*) or a verbal quarrel ( *vācākalahopi*).

A prior opposition is strife (*viggaho*).
The final, the final dispute.

"You, you" is a disrespectful expression, "you, you."

112. Now to show obsessional craving (*taṇhā*) in reverse order, the Buddha, beginning with the section on "grounds for defense," traces back the teaching.

There, *kāma-taṇhā* means craving for forms (rūpa) and so forth, which arises through attachment (*rāga*) to the qualities of the five sense objects.

*Bhava-taṇhā* means attachment (*rāga*) associated with the eternalist view (*śāśvatadṛṣṭi*).

*Vibhava-taṇhā* means attachment (*rāga*) associated with the annihilationist view (*ucchedadṛṣṭi*).

"These two phenomena" (*ime dve dhammā*) means craving that is the root of the round (*vaṭṭamūlataṇhā*) and obsessional craving (*samudācārataṇhā*); these are the two phenomena.

“Being a duality” (*dvayenā*) means although having reached a state of unity by way of the characteristic of craving (*taṇhā-lakkhaṇa*), due to the round root (*vaṭṭamūla*) and habitual craving (*samudācāra*), through two parts, in feeling (*vedanā*) there is a single convergence (*ekasamosaraṇā*); the meaning is a single condition through the condition of feeling (*vedanāpaccayena ekapaccayāti attho*).

"Convergence" (*samosaraṇa*) is threefold: convergence by descent (*osaraṇasamosaraṇaṃ*), convergence by co-nascence (*sahajātasamosaraṇaṃ*), and convergence by condition (*paccayasamosaraṇañca*).

There – “then all of those are convergences of desire (*kāmasamosaraṇāni bhavantī*)” – this is called “convergence by descent” (*osaraṇasamosaraṇaṃ nāma*).

“These phenomena, friend, are rooted in desire (*chandamūlakā*), originate from contact (*phassasamudayā*), and converge upon feeling (*vedanāsamosaraṇā*)” (A.Ni. 8.83) this is called “convergence by co-nascence” (*sahajātasamosaraṇaṃ nāma*).

“Being a duality, converge into a unity in feeling” (*dvayena vedanāya ekasamosaraṇā*) this, however, is known as "convergence by condition" (*paccayasamosaraṇa*).

114. “Ānanda, by which qualities (yehi ākārehi)” and so forth, the qualities (ākārā) are said to be the mutually dissimilar characteristics of feeling and so forth.

They are also called “traits” (liṅgāni) because, when carefully looked at, they betray the concealed meanings.

“Indicators” (nimittāni) because of being the cause of recognizing that and that.

“Designations” (uddesā) because of what should be designated in that and that way.

Therefore, this is the meaning here: “Ānanda, when there are present those qualities, traits, signs, and indicators through which there is a description of the ‘mental body,’ the mental group; that is, when there is present feeling’s quality, trait, and sign of being what is felt, and its indicator ‘feeling’; when there is present perception’s quality, trait, and sign of perceiving, and its indicator ‘perception’; when there is present the mental formations’ quality, trait, and sign of volition, and their indicator ‘volition’; when there is present consciousness’s quality, trait, and sign of cognizing, and its indicator ‘consciousness’—the description of the ‘mental body’ occurs as ‘this is the mental body.’

When feeling and so forth, and qualities and so forth, the causes for the description of the mental body are not present, would designation-contact be discerned in the material body? The mind-contact, which is synonymous with designation-contact and which arises in the mind-door taking as its basis the four aggregates, would that be discerned in the material body? Would it arise taking as its basis one or another of the five kinds of sensitive matter?”

Then the Venerable Ānanda, rejecting the arising of that origination from the material body just as one would the arising of a mango fruit from a rose-apple tree when mango trees are absent, said: “Certainly not, venerable sir (no hetaṃ bhante).”

In the second question, the meaning of form (rūpa) should be understood in terms of the mode of disintegration, the trait of disintegration, the indicator of disintegration, and by way of designation, and the meaning of qualities, etc.

“Impaction-contact (paṭighasamphasso)” is contact that arises by taking as its basis the material aggregate that is with impaction (sappaṭighaṃ rūpakkhandhaṃ vatthuṃ katvā uppajjanakasamphasso).

Here also, the Elder, rejecting the arising of the origination of a rose-apple from a mango tree when rose-apple trees are absent, just as he would the arising of the mental body from the material body, said: “Certainly not, venerable sir.”

Impaction-contact (paṭighasamphasso). The third question is stated in terms of both.

There the Elder, rejecting the arising of the origination of mangoes and rose-apples in space, just as he would the arising of the contact of the two in the absence of name and form (nāmarūpa), said: “Certainly not, venerable sir.”

Thus, having shown the conditions for the two kinds of contact separately, now, in order to show without making distinctions, that name and form (nāmarūpa) is the condition for both of them, he began the fourth question: “By which qualities, Ānanda…”

That is, this name and form (yadidaṃ nāmarūpa): that name and form (nāmarūpa) which is in the six doors, this itself is the cause, this itself is the condition, is the meaning.

For in the eye-door, the eye and the rest as well as visible form objects and the rest are form, and the aggregates associated [with contact] are name (nāma). Thus that fivefold contact is still “contact with name and form (nāmarūpa) as condition."

In the mind-door, too, the heart-basis and any material object are form (rūpa).

The phenomena associated with contact and any immaterial object are name (nāma).

Thus mind-contact, too, is “contact with name and form (nāmarūpa) as condition.”

But name and form (nāmarūpa) is a condition in many ways.

"Tatrānandā" (in that delight) is in these four kinds of theorists.

"Etarahi vā" (in the present) means now only, not afterwards.

This is said by way of annihilationism ( *ucchedavāda*).

"Tatthabhāviṃ vā" (existing there) means existing there or in another world.

This is said by way of eternalism ( *sassatavāda*).

"Atathaṃ vā pana santaṃ" (or not real, but existent) means having an unreal nature, [yet] existing.

"Tathattāyā" (for the sake of suchness) means for the sake of that state.

"Upakappessāmī" (I will bring about) means I will accomplish.

The third position shows their dispute.

For the annihilationist thinks: "The self, which the eternalist posits as not subject to annihilation, is not thus. I will convert him to the way it really is—subject to annihilation. I will make him understand his eternalism and cause him to accept annihilationism."

The eternalist, too, thinks: "The self, which the annihilationist posits as non-eternal, is not thus. I will convert him to the way it really is—eternal. I will make him understand his annihilationism and cause him to accept eternalism."

"Evaṃ santaṃ kho" (thus, existent indeed) means thus, the meaning is that they describe the self as having material form, limited.

"Rūpi" (having material form) means having attained the form *kasiṇa*.

"Parittattānudiṭṭhi anusetī" (a settled view as having material form and limited underlies this) means this view underlies this, [thinking] "the self is limited," but it does not underlie this like a vine or creeper.

It should be understood that it underlies this in the sense that it has not been abandoned.

"Iccālaṃ vacanāyā" (it is enough to say) means it is proper to say that such a view underlies that person.

This method applies to all the other cases.

"Evaṃ...arūpi" (thus...formless) means here, *arūpakasiṇalābhiṃ*, *arūpakkhandhagocaraṃ vāti*, this is how the meaning should be understood.

To this extent sixteen kinds of theorists have been shown: four who gain meditative attainments, their respective disciples, four rationalists, and their respective disciples.

128. *Tatrā* means in those stations of consciousness (*vijñānaṭṭhiti*).

*Tañca pajānāti* means he understands that station of consciousness (*viññāṇaṭṭhiti*).

*Tassā ca samudaya* means, "from the arising of ignorance (*avidyā*) arises form (*rūpa*)," and so forth, by this method, he understands its arising.

*Tassā ca atthaṅgama* means, "from the cessation of ignorance (*avidyā*) ceases form (*rūpa*)," and so forth, by this method, he understands its passing away.

*Assāda* means, whatever pleasure and joy arise in dependence on form (*rūpa*)…pe… whatever pleasure and joy arise in dependence on consciousness (*vijñāṇa*), this is the satisfaction in consciousness (*vijñāṇassa assāda*). Thus, he understands its satisfaction.

*Ādīnava* means, whatever form (*rūpa*)…pe… whatever consciousness (*vijñāṇa*) is impermanent (*anitya*), suffering (*duḥkha*), and subject to change (*vipariṇāmadhamma*), this is the unsatisfactoriness in consciousness (*vijñāṇassa ādīnava*). Thus he understands its unsatisfactoriness.

*Nissaraṇa* means, whatever removal of desire and lust for form (*rūpa*)…pe… whatever removal of desire and lust for consciousness (*vijñāṇa*), the abandoning of desire and lust, this is the escape from consciousness (*vijñāṇassa nissaraṇa*). Thus, he understands its escape.

*Kallaṃ nu tenā* means, is it proper for one to seek enjoyment in it? Is it right for that *bhikkhu* to seek enjoyment in that station for consciousness (*viññāṇaṭṭhiti*) as "I" or "mine" by way of craving (*taṇhā*), conceit (*māna*), or views (*diṭṭhi*)?

This method of interpretation should be applied to all the other stations for consciousness.

But where there is no form (*rūpa*), the origin (*samudaya*) should be interpreted by way of the four aggregates (*khandha*) only; and where there is no consciousness (*vijñāṇa*) it should be interpreted by way of the one aggregate (*khandha*).

“through the origination of nutriment (*āhāra*)” and “through the cessation of nutriment (*āhāra*),” thus here the passage should be interpreted with these phrases.

*Yato kho, Ānanda, bhikkhū* means, when, Ānanda, a *bhikkhu*.

*Anupādā vimutto* means, released through lack of clinging (*upādāna*) to the four clingings.

*Paññāvimutto* means, liberated by wisdom (*prajñā*).

Having brought about the future non-occurrence of the mental body (*nāmakāya*) and the material body (*rūpakāya*), he is liberated solely by the power of wisdom (*prajñā*) without realizing the eight emancipations (*vimokkha*). This is the meaning.

This type is fivefold: the dry-insight meditator (*sukkhavipassako*) and those who attain *arhattva* after having become established in one or another of the first *dhyāna* and so forth.

It was also said: “What individual is liberated through wisdom (*paññāvimutto*)? Herein, some individual does not dwell suffusing the eight emancipations (*vimokkha*) with the body (*kāya*); but having seen with wisdom (*prajñā*), his cankers (*āsava*) are destroyed. This individual is called ‘one liberated by wisdom (*paññāvimutto*)’ ”.

“Ānanda, there are these eight liberations. Which eight?

Possessed of material form, one sees forms: this is the first liberation ( *paṭhamo vimokkho*).

Not perceiving form internally, one sees forms externally: this is the second liberation ( *dutiyo vimokkho*).

One is resolved only upon the beautiful: this is the third liberation ( *tatiyo vimokkho*).

With the complete surmounting of perceptions of form, with the disappearance of perceptions of sensory impact, with non-attention to perceptions of diversity, aware that ‘space is infinite,’ one enters upon and abides in the base of infinite space: this is the fourth liberation ( *catuttho vimokkho*).

Having completely surmounted the base of the infinity of space, ‘Consciousness is infinite,’ one enters and dwells in the base of the infinity of consciousness. This is the fifth liberation ( *pañcamo vimokkho*).

Having completely surmounted the base of the infinity of consciousness, ‘There is nothing,’ one enters and remains in the base of nothingness. This is the sixth liberation ( *chaṭṭho vimokkho*).

Having completely surmounted the base of nothingness, one enters and remains in the base of neither perception nor non-perception. This is the seventh liberation ( *sattamo vimokkho*).

Having completely surmounted the base of neither perception nor non-perception, one enters and dwells in the cessation of perception and feeling. This is the eighth liberation ( *aṭṭhamo vimokkho*).

These, Ānanda, are the eight liberations ( *aṭṭha vimokkhā*).

129. Thus, having shown the conclusion and name of one *bhikkhu*, in order to show it to another, he said, "These eight..." etc.

There, what is *vimokkha* (liberation) in the sense of what? In the sense of being intent [on something]. But what is this being intent? It is said to be in the sense of freeing thoroughly from the adverse phenomena (*paccanīkadhammehi*) and in the sense of freeing thoroughly by way of delight (*abhirativasena*) in the object (*ārammaṇe*). [It is like] the way a little boy sleeps in his father’s lap, his body completely relaxed, occurrence on the object without constraint, free from apprehension.

But this meaning is not in the last *vimokkha*, it is in all the previous ones.

Here, in “*rūpī rūpāni passati* (one possessing material form sees material forms),” the material form (*rūpa*) is the fine-material-sphere *jhāna* (*rūpajjhānaṃ*) aroused through a blue *kasióa* (*nīlakasiṇa*), etc., using as the blue *kasióa*, etc., something internal (*ajjhattaṃ*) such as the hairs of the head, etc. One having that material form is *rūpī*.

“*bahiddhā rūpāni passati* (one sees material forms externally)” means that he sees material forms (*rūpāni*) that are external (*bahiddhāpi*)—blue *kasiṇas*, etc.—with the eye of *jhāna* (*jhānacakkhunā*).

By this, the four fine-material-sphere *jhānas* (*rūpāvacarajjhānāni*) are shown for a person who arouses *jhāna* through the *kasiṇas* based on internal and external objects.

“*ajjhattaṃ arūpasaññī* (perceiving no material form internally)” means that internally (*ajjhattaṃ*) he is not perceiving material form (*na rūpasaññī*); the meaning is that he has not aroused the fine-material-sphere *jhānas* on his own head hairs, etc.

By this, the fine-material-sphere *jhānas* (*rūpāvacarajjhānāni*) are shown for a person who does the preparatory work externally (*bahiddhā*) and arouses *jhānas* only externally.

*ajjhattaṃ arūpasaññī*

Here, in “*subhantveva adhimutto hotī* (one is intent on beauty),” the *jhānas* are shown in the very pure blue (*nīla*), etc., color *kasiṇas* (*vaṇṇakasiṇesu*).

In the meditative absorption (*antoappanāyaṃ*) itself there is no concern with the idea of the beautiful (*subhanti ābhogo natthi*). But because one who dwells in meditation taking as his object a pure and beautiful *kasióa* can be described as “released upon the idea of the beautiful (*subhanti adhimutto hotīti vattabbatam āpajjati*),” the teaching is expounded in those words.

But in the *Paṭisambhidāmagga* it is said: “How is the *vimokkha* ‘one is intent on beauty’? Here, a *bhikkhu* dwells pervading one direction with a mind accompanied by loving-kindness (*mettāsahagatena cetasā*)... Because beings are developed in loving-kindness (*mettāya bhāvitattā*), they are not hostile (*appaṭikūlā honti*). He dwells pervading one direction with a mind accompanied by compassion, joy, equanimity... Because beings are developed in equanimity, they are not hostile. Such is the emancipation: ‘One is released upon the idea of the beautiful’ ” (Paṭi. Ma. 1.212).

*subhantveva adhimutto hotī*

In “*sabbaso rūpasaññāna* (altogether transcending perceptions of form),” etc., whatever should be said, all that has been said in the *Visuddhimagga*.

“*ayaṃ aṭṭhamo vimokkho* (this is the eighth emancipation)” means that the eighth emancipation is the highest emancipation, since it is completely purified and liberated from the four aggregates (*khandhānaṃ*).

The venerable Ānanda was satisfied and delighted in the Blessed One’s words (*attamano āyasmā ānando bhagavato bhāsitaṃ abhinandīti*).

130. "Anuloma" means "in forward order": from the beginning to the end. "Paṭiloma" means "in reverse order": from the end back to the beginning. "Anulomapaṭiloma" means this is said to be because of the very nature of the attainments, by moving here and there, having stayed there. "Yatthicchaka" means indicating the place: wherever place he wishes. "Yadicchaka" means indicating the attainment: whatever attainment he wishes. "Yāvatichaka" means indicating the length of time, for as long a period of time as he wishes. "Samāpajjatī" means he enters into that attainment. "Vuṭṭhātī" means he stands up, having risen up from that.

"Ubhatobhāgavimutto" means liberated in both ways by two parts, liberated from the material body by the immaterial attainment, liberated from the name-body by the path. And this was said:

"Liberated in both ways, as a flame thrown by the force of the wind, (Upasiva, said the Blessed One), goes to its end and cannot be reckoned; even so the sage liberated from the name-body, goes to his end and cannot be reckoned." (Sutta Nipāta 1080);

The “one liberated in both ways” is five fold: those who attain *arahatship* after emerging from one or another of the immaterial attainments, and the one who, having been a non-returner, attains *arahatship* after emerging from cessation. Some say: “Since the fourth fine-material-sphere *jhāna* has two factors and is associated with equanimity, it is just like an immaterial *jhāna*. Therefore one who attains *arahatship* after emerging from the fourth fine-material-sphere *jhāna* is also liberated in both ways.”

This question about the "one liberated in both ways" arose in the Loha Pāsāda and, depending on the explanation of Tipiṭaka Cūḷasumanatthera, eventually reached a decision.

In the Giri Vihāra, it is said, a pupil of the Elder heard the question from the mouth of an alms-wandering monk and said: “Friend, before our teacher explained this matter in the Bronze Palace, no one knew the answer.” What did the Elder say, venerable sir? The fourth *jhāna* of the fine-material-sphere, although it has two factors, is accompanied by equanimity, and suppresses the defilements, it still operates in proximate range to the defilements, in the field for their growth. For in five-constituent existence the defilements assail the mind with the decisive support of a object, such as the colour blue, etc., and the fine-material-sphere *jhāna* does not surmount such objects. Therefore ‘liberated in both ways’ signifies one who attains *arahatship* after completely turning away from material form and suppressing the defilements by means of an immaterial *jhāna.” This, friend, the Elder said.

Having said this he quoted the *sutta*: “Who is the individual liberated in both ways? Herein, some individual dwells suffusing the eight emancipations with the body, and having seen with wisdom, his cankers are destroyed. This individual is called one liberated in both ways” (Puggala Paññatti 24).

"By this, Ānanda, liberation in both ways," means, Ānanda, from this liberation in both ways.

The rest is clear throughout.

By this, Ānanda, liberation in both ways. Thus the Mahānidāna Sutta Vaṇṇanā in the Sumaṅgalavilāsinī Dīghanikāyaṭṭhakathā is finished.
