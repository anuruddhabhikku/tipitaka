Atha kho sambahulānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ pacchābhattaṃ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantānaṃ upaṭṭhānasālāyaṃ sannisinnānaṃ sannipatitānaṃ ayamantarākathā <pb ed="M" n="3.0131"/> udapādi – ‘‘acchariyaṃ, āvuso, abbhutaṃ, āvuso! Yāvañcidaṃ tena bhagavatā jānatā passatā arahatā sammāsambuddhena kāyagatāsati <note>kāyagatā sati (syā. kaṃ. pī.)</note> bhāvitā bahulīkatā mahapphalā vuttā mahānisaṃsā’’ti. Ayañca hidaṃ tesaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ antarākathā vippakatā hoti, atha kho bhagavā sāyanhasamayaṃ paṭisallānā vuṭṭhito yena upaṭṭhānasālā tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā paññatte āsane nisīdi. Nisajja kho bhagavā bhikkhū āmantesi – ‘‘kāya nuttha, bhikkhave, etarahi kathāya sannisinnā, kā ca pana vo antarākathā vippakatā’’ti? ‘‘Idha <pb ed="P" n="3.0089"/>, bhante, amhākaṃ pacchābhattaṃ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantānaṃ upaṭṭhānasālāyaṃ sannisinnānaṃ sannipatitānaṃ ayamantarākathā udapādi – ‘acchariyaṃ, āvuso, abbhutaṃ, āvuso! Yāvañcidaṃ tena bhagavatā jānatā passatā arahatā sammāsambuddhena kāyagatāsati bhāvitā bahulīkatā mahapphalā vuttā mahānisaṃsā’ti. Ayaṃ kho no, bhante, antarākathā vippakatā, atha bhagavā anuppatto’’ti.

Thus have I heard. Once the Blessed One was dwelling at Anāthapindika’s monastery, in the Jeta Grove, near Sāvatthi.

Then, after the meal, as a number of monks were returning from their alms round, they gathered in the service hall, and this discussion arose: “Amazing, friends, astounding, friends, how well it was said by that Blessed One who knows, who sees, accomplished and fully enlightened, that mindfulness of the body (kāyagatāsati), when developed and cultivated, is of great fruit and great benefit.”

However, this discussion of these monks was interrupted. Then the Blessed One, having arisen from seclusion in the evening, approached the service hall; having approached, he sat down on the seat that was prepared.

Having sat down, the Blessed One addressed the monks, “For what topic are you gathered together here, monks? And what was the discussion that was interrupted?”

“Here, venerable sir (bhante), after the meal, as we were returning from our alms round, we gathered in the service hall, and this discussion arose: ‘Amazing, friends, astounding, friends, how well it was said by that Blessed One who knows, who sees, accomplished and fully enlightened, that mindfulness of the body (kāyagatāsati), when developed and cultivated, is of great fruit and great benefit.’

This, venerable sir (bhante), was the discussion that was interrupted, when the Blessed One arrived.”

154. “And how, O monks, when mindfulness related to the body (kāyagatāsati) is developed and cultivated, is it of great fruit and great benefit? Here, O monks, a monk, having gone to the forest, or to the foot of a tree, or to an empty house, sits down, crosses his legs, keeps his body erect, and establishes mindfulness in front of him. Ever mindful he breathes in, mindful he breathes out. Breathing in long, he understands: ‘I breathe in long’; or breathing out long, he understands: ‘I breathe out long.’ Breathing in short, he understands: ‘I breathe in short’; or breathing out short, he understands: ‘I breathe out short.’ He trains thus: ‘I shall breathe in experiencing the whole body of breath’; he trains thus: ‘I shall breathe out experiencing the whole body of breath.’ He trains thus: ‘I shall breathe in tranquillising the bodily formation’ (kāyasaṅkhāraṃ); he trains thus: ‘I shall breathe out tranquillising the bodily formation.’ As he abides thus diligent, ardent, and resolute, his memories and intentions based on the household life are abandoned. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it (samādhiyati). That too is how a bhikkhu develops mindfulness of the body.

“And further, O monks, when walking, a bhikkhu understands: ‘I am walking’; when standing, he understands: ‘I am standing’; when sitting, he understands: ‘I am sitting’; when lying down, he understands: ‘I am lying down’; or, in whatever way his body is disposed, he knows it is (disposed) in just that way. As he abides thus diligent, ardent, and resolute, his memories and intentions based on the household life are abandoned. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it. That too is how a bhikkhu develops mindfulness of the body.

“And further, O monks, a bhikkhu is one who acts in full awareness (sampajānakārī) when going forward and returning; who acts in full awareness when looking ahead and looking away; who acts in full awareness when flexing and extending his limbs; who acts in full awareness when wearing his robes and carrying his outer robe and bowl; who acts in full awareness when eating, drinking, consuming food, and tasting; who acts in full awareness when defecating or urinating; who acts in full awareness when walking, standing, sitting, falling asleep, waking up, talking, and remaining silent. As he abides thus diligent, ardent, and resolute, his memories and intentions based on the household life are abandoned. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it. That too is how a bhikkhu develops mindfulness of the body.

Just as a dexterous butcher or his apprentice, having killed a cow, would sit at a crossroads cutting it up into pieces, the monk reflects on this very body—however it stands, however it is disposed—in terms of properties: ‘In this body there is the earth property (pathavīdhātu), the water property (āpodhātu), the fire property (tejodhātu), & the wind property (vāyodhātū)’.

“Again, bhikkhus, quite secluded from sensual pleasures… a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the first jhāna.

Whenever a strong man loosens the embankment (āḷiṃ muñceyya), would water come out?" "Yes, bhante."

"So too, bhikkhus, when anyone has developed and cultivated mindfulness of the body, then when he inclines his mind towards realizing any state that may be realized by direct knowledge, he attains the ability to witness any aspect therein, there being a suitable basis.

Suppose, bhikkhus, there were a chariot (ratha) on even ground at the crossroads (catumahāpathe), harnessed to thoroughbreds (ājaññaratho), waiting with goad (patoda) lying ready; a skilled trainer (yoggācariyo), a charioteer (sārathi) who tames horses (assadammasārathi), having mounted it, could guide it as he wishes, as he desires, taking the reins (rasmiyo) in his left hand and the goad in his right, or bring it back; so too, bhikkhus, when anyone has developed and cultivated mindfulness of the body, then when he inclines his mind towards realizing any state that may be realized by direct knowledge, he attains the ability to witness any aspect therein, there being a suitable basis."

159. Bhikkhus, when mindfulness of the body (kāyagatā-sati) has been cultivated, developed, increased, used as a vehicle, used as a foundation, established, practiced, and well undertaken, these ten benefits are to be expected.

One is able to endure discontent and delight, and discontent does not endure oneself; one dwells overcoming discontent whenever it arises.

One is able to endure fear and dread, and fear and dread do not endure oneself; one dwells overcoming fear and dread whenever they arise.

One is able to endure cold and heat, hunger and thirst, and contact with gadflies, mosquitoes, wind, the sun, and creeping things; one is able to endure ill-spoken, unwelcome words and arisen bodily feelings that are painful, racking, sharp, piercing, disagreeable, distressing, and menacing to life.

[One obtains] the four jhānas, which are of the mind, abidings in happiness here and now, one obtains them at will, without trouble, and without difficulty.

One experiences various kinds of spiritual power. Being one, one becomes many; being many, one becomes one; one wields bodily mastery even as far as the Brahma-world.

With the divine ear element (dibbāya sotadhātuyā), which is purified and surpasses the human, one hears both kinds of sounds, the divine and the human, those that are far as well as near.

One understands the minds of other beings and persons, having encompassed them with one's own mind. One understands a mind with lust (sarāgaṃ) as a mind with lust, and a mind without lust (vītarāgaṃ) as a mind without lust, and so on. One understands a mind with aversion as a mind with aversion, and a mind without aversion as a mind without aversion. One understands a mind with delusion as a mind with delusion, and a mind without delusion as a mind without delusion. One understands a constricted mind as a constricted mind, and a scattered mind as a scattered mind. One understands a developed mind as a developed mind, and an undeveloped mind as an undeveloped mind. One understands a surpassed mind as a surpassed mind, and an unsurpassed mind as an unsurpassed mind. One understands a concentrated mind as a concentrated mind, and an unconcentrated mind as an unconcentrated mind. One understands a liberated mind as a liberated mind, and an unliberated mind as an unliberated mind.

One recollects one's manifold past lives, that is, one birth, two births... Thus with their aspects and particulars one recollects one's manifold past lives.

With the divine eye (dibbena cakkhunā), which is purified and surpasses the human, one sees beings passing away and reappearing, inferior and superior, fair and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate, and one understands how beings pass on according to their actions.

Through the destruction of the āsavas (influxes, defilements, or taints), one realizes for oneself with direct knowledge, here and now enters upon and abides in the liberation of mind (cetovimutti) and liberation by wisdom (paññāvimutti) that are taintless.

Bhikkhus, when mindfulness of the body has been repeatedly practiced, developed, cultivated, used as a vehicle, used as a basis, established, consolidated, and well undertaken, these ten benefits may be expected.

This is what the Buddha said. The monks were delighted and rejoiced at his words.

The Kāyagatāsati Sutta (Mindfulness of the Body Discourse) is finished, the ninth.
