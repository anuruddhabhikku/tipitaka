Thus have I heard:

Once the Blessed One (bhagavā) was dwelling at Sāvatthi in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

There the Blessed One addressed the monks (bhikkhus), saying: “Bhikkhus!”

“Venerable sir (bhadante),” those monks replied to the Blessed One.

The Blessed One said this: “I will teach you an exposition of non-conflict (araṇavibhaṅgaṃ), O monks (bhikkhave).

Listen to that, pay close attention and I will speak.”

“As you say, lord (evaṃ, bhante),” those monks responded to the Blessed One.

The Blessed One said this:

"One should not pursue sensual pleasures (kāmasukha), which are low, coarse, worldly, ignoble, and connected to harm. Nor should one pursue self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga), which is painful, ignoble, and connected to harm.

Avoiding both of these extremes, monks (bhikkhave), the middle path (majjhimā paṭipadā) has been fully awakened to by the Tathāgata, which produces vision (cakkhukaraṇī), which produces knowledge (ñāṇakaraṇī), which leads to peace (upasamāya), to direct knowledge (abhiññāya), to self-awakening (sambodhāya), to Nirvāṇa (nibbānāya).

One should know what it is to extol (ussādanañca jaññā) and what it is to disparage (apasādanañca jaññā); knowing both what it is to extol (ussādanañca ñatvā) and what it is to disparage (apasādanañca ñatvā), one should neither extol (nevussādeyya) nor disparage (na apasādeyya), but should teach only the Dharma (dhammameva deseyya).

One should know how to define pleasure (sukhavinicchayaṃ jaññā); knowing how to define pleasure (sukhavinicchayaṃ ñatvā), one should pursue pleasure within oneself (ajjhattaṃ sukhamanuyuñjeyya).

One should not utter covert speech (rahovādaṃ na bhāseyya), and one should not utter overt sharp speech (sammukhā na khīṇaṃ bhaṇe).

One should speak unhurriedly (ataramānova bhāseyya), not hurriedly (no taramāno).

One should not insist on local language (janapadaniruttiṃ nābhiniveseyya), and one should not override normal usage (samaññaṃ nātidhāveyyāti)—this is the summary (uddeso) of the exposition of non-conflict (araṇavibhaṅgassa)."

324.
"One should not pursue sensual pleasure (kāmasukha), which is low (hīnaṃ), vulgar (gammaṃ), coarse (pothujjanikaṃ), ignoble (anariyaṃ), and unbeneficial (anatthasaṃhitaṃ); and one should not pursue self-mortification (attakilamathānuyogaṃ), which is painful (dukkhaṃ), ignoble (anariyaṃ), and unbeneficial (anatthasaṃhitaṃ)."

So it was said. And with reference to what was this said? The pursuit of the enjoyment (somanassānuyogaḥ) of one whose pleasure (sukhino) is linked to sensual desires (kāmapaṭisandhi) - low (hīnaḥ), vulgar (gammaḥ), coarse (pothujjanikaḥ), ignoble (anariyaḥ), and unbeneficial (anatthasaṃhitaḥ) - is a state beset by suffering (sadukkho), vexation (saupaghāto), despair (saupāyāso), and fever (sapariḷāho); it is the wrong way (micchāpaṭipadā).

Disengagement (ananuyogaḥ) from the pursuit of the enjoyment (somanassānuyogam) of one whose pleasure is linked to sensual desires (kāmapaṭisandhi) - low, vulgar, coarse, ignoble, and unbeneficial - is a state without suffering (adukkho), vexation (anupaghāto), despair (anupāyāso), and fever (apariḷāho); it is the right way (sammāpaṭipadā).

The pursuit of self-mortification (attakilamathānuyogaḥ) - painful (dukkho), ignoble (anariyaḥ), and unbeneficial (anatthasaṃhitoḥ) - is a state beset by suffering (sadukkho), vexation (saupaghāto), despair (saupāyāso), and fever (sapariḷāho); it is the wrong way (micchāpaṭipadā).

Disengagement (ananuyogaḥ) from the pursuit of self-mortification (attakilamathānuyogam) - painful (dukkhaṃ), ignoble (anariyaṃ), and unbeneficial (anatthasaṃhitaṃ) - is a state without suffering (adukkho), vexation (anupaghāto), despair (anupāyāso), and fever (apariḷāho); it is the right way (sammāpaṭipadā).

“One should not pursue sensual pleasure, which is low, vulgar, coarse, ignoble, and unbeneficial; and one should not pursue self-mortification, which is painful, ignoble, and unbeneficial.” So it was with reference to this that it was said.

Kiñcetaṃ paṭicca vuttaṃ? Ayameva ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo, seyyathidaṃ – sammādiṭṭhi <pb ed="T" n="3.0425"/>, sammāsaṅkappo, sammāvācā, sammākammanto, sammāājīvo, sammāvāyāmo, sammāsati, sammāsamādhi. ‘Ete kho ubho ante anupagamma majjhimā paṭipadā tathāgatena abhisambuddhā, cakkhukaraṇī ñāṇakaraṇī upasamāya abhiññāya sambodhāya nibbānāya saṃvattatī’ti – iti yaṃ taṃ vuttaṃ, idametaṃ paṭicca vuttaṃ.

“Avoiding both of these extremes, the middle way realized by the Tathāgata—producing vision, producing knowledge—leads to calm, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to Unbinding.” So it was said.

And with reference to what was this said? This noble eightfold path (ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo), namely: right view (sammādiṭṭhi), right thought (sammāsaṅkappo), right speech (sammāvācā), right action (sammākammanto), right livelihood (sammāājīvo), right effort (sammāvāyāmo), right mindfulness (sammāsati), right concentration (sammāsamādhi).

So it was with reference to this that it was said: ‘The Middle Way discovered by the Tathagata avoids both these extremes…to Nibbana.’

326. “One should know what it is to extol (ussādana) and what it is to disparage (apasādana); and knowing both, one should neither extol nor disparage but should teach only the dharma.” So it was said.

What was said with reference to what? And how, bhikkhus, does there come to be extolling and disparaging, and failure to teach only the dharma? “All those engaged in the pursuit of the enjoyment of one whose pleasure is linked to sensual desires—low, worldly, ignoble, and unbeneficial—are beset by suffering, vexation, despair, and fever, and they have entered upon the wrong way.” Thus, some disparage.

“All those disengaged from the pursuit of the enjoyment of one whose pleasure is linked to sensual desires—low, worldly, ignoble, and unbeneficial—are without suffering, vexation, despair, and fever, and they have entered upon the right way.” Thus, some extol.

“All those engaged in the pursuit of self-mortification—painful, ignoble, and unbeneficial—are beset by suffering, vexation, despair, and fever, and they have entered upon the wrong way.” Thus, some disparage.

“All those disengaged from the pursuit of self-mortification—painful, ignoble, and unbeneficial—are without suffering, vexation, despair, and fever, and they have entered upon the right way.” Thus, some extol.

“All those who have not abandoned the fetter of being (bhava-saṃyojana) are beset by suffering, vexation, despair, and fever, and they have entered upon the wrong way.” Thus, some disparage.

“All those who have abandoned the fetter of being are without suffering, vexation, despair, and fever, and they have entered upon the right way.” Thus, some extol. In this way, bhikkhus, there comes to be extolling and disparaging, and failure to teach only the dharma.

327. “And how, monks, does there come to be neither extolling nor disparaging, but just the teaching of the Dharma?

‘All those who pursue pleasure tied to sensual desires, who are attached to indulgence, which is inferior, vulgar, common, ignoble and unbeneficial, all these are full of suffering, afflicted, distressed and tormented, and have embarked on the wrong path’—one does not say this.

But instead says: ‘Indulgence is suffering, afflicted, distressed and tormented, and the wrong path’—in this way, one teaches the Dharma alone.

“‘All those who pursue pleasure tied to sensual desires, who are not attached to indulgence, which is inferior, vulgar, common, ignoble and unbeneficial, all these are free from suffering, unafflicted, undistressed and untormented, and have embarked on the right path’—one does not say this.

But instead says: ‘Non-indulgence is free from suffering, unafflicted, undistressed and untormented, and the right path’—in this way, one teaches the Dharma alone.

“‘All those who pursue self-mortification, which is painful, ignoble and unbeneficial, all these are full of suffering, afflicted, distressed and tormented, and have embarked on the wrong path’—one does not say this.

But instead says: ‘Self-mortification is suffering, afflicted, distressed and tormented, and the wrong path’—in this way, one teaches the Dharma alone.

“‘All those who pursue self-mortification, who are not attached to self-mortification, which is painful, ignoble and unbeneficial, all these are free from suffering, unafflicted, undistressed and untormented, and have embarked on the right path’—one does not say this.

But instead says: ‘Non-self-mortification is free from suffering, unafflicted, undistressed and untormented, and the right path’—in this way, one teaches the Dharma alone.

“‘All those who have not abandoned the fetter of being ( bhava-saṃyojana), all these are full of suffering, afflicted, distressed and tormented, and have embarked on the wrong path’—one does not say this.

But instead says: ‘As long as the fetter of being (bhava-saṃyojana) is unabandoned, being (bhava) too is unabandoned’—in this way, one teaches the Dharma alone.

“‘All those who have abandoned the fetter of being (bhava-saṃyojana), all these are free from suffering, unafflicted, undistressed and untormented, and have embarked on the right path’—one does not say this.

But instead says: ‘When the fetter of being (bhava-saṃyojana) is abandoned, being (bhava) also is abandoned’—in this way, one teaches the Dharma alone. Thus, monks, there comes to be neither extolling nor disparaging, but just the teaching of the Dharma.

‘One should know what it is to extol and what it is to disparage, and knowing both, one should neither extol nor disparage, but should teach the Dharma alone’—so it was said with reference to this.

328. “'One should know how to define pleasure (sukhavinicchayaṃ jaññā); knowing how to define pleasure, one should pursue pleasure within oneself (sukhavinicchayaṃ ñatvā ajjhattaṃ sukhamanuyuñjeyyā’ti)’—so it was said.

For what reason was this said? These are the five cords of sensual pleasure (pañca kāmaguṇā), monks.

What are the five? Forms cognizable by the eye that are wished for, desired, agreeable, and likeable, connected with sensual desire and provocative of lust (cakkhuviññeyyā rūpā iṭṭhā kantā manāpā piyarūpā kāmūpasaṃhitā rajanīyā); sounds cognizable by the ear...odours cognizable by the nose...flavours cognizable by the tongue...tangibles cognizable by the body that are wished for, desired, agreeable, and likeable, connected with sensual desire and provocative of lust (kāyaviññeyyā phoṭṭhabbā iṭṭhā kantā manāpā piyarūpā kāmūpasaṃhitā rajanīyā)—these are the five cords of sensual pleasure (pañca kāmaguṇā), monks.

Now, monks, whatever pleasure or happiness arises in dependence on these five strands of sensuality, that is called sensual pleasure (kāmasukhaṃ), filthy pleasure (mīḷhasukhaṃ), coarse pleasure (puthujjanasukhaṃ), ignoble pleasure (anariyasukhaṃ).

‘It should not be pursued (na āsevitabbaṃ), it should not be developed (na bhāvetabbaṃ), it should not be cultivated (na bahulīkātabbaṃ), it should be feared (bhāyitabbaṃ etassa sukhassā’ti)’—I say.

Here, monks, quite secluded from sensual pleasures (kāmehi), secluded from unwholesome states (akusalehi dhammehi), a monk enters and dwells in the first jhāna, which is accompanied by thought and examination, with rapture and happiness born of seclusion (vivekajaṃ pītisukhaṃ paṭhamaṃ jhānaṃ upasampajja viharati).

With the subsiding of thinking and pondering, by gaining inner tranquility and oneness of mind, he enters and remains in the second jhāna, which is without thinking and pondering, born of concentration, filled with delight and joy (avitakkaṃ avicāraṃ samādhijaṃ pītisukhaṃ dutiyaṃ jhānaṃ upasampajja viharati).

With the fading away as well of rapture, he remains equanimous and mindful...he enters upon and abides in the third jhāna...the fourth jhāna (tatiyaṃ jhānaṃ… catutthaṃ jhānaṃ upasampajja viharati).

This is called the bliss of renunciation (nekkhamma sukhaṃ), the bliss of seclusion (paviveka sukhaṃ), the bliss of peace (upasama sukhaṃ), the bliss of enlightenment (sambodhi sukhaṃ).

‘It should be pursued (āsevitabbaṃ), it should be developed (bhāvetabbaṃ), it should be cultivated (bahulīkātabbaṃ), it should not be feared (na bhāyitabbaṃ etassa sukhassā’ti)’—I say.

‘One should know how to define pleasure (sukhavinicchayaṃ jaññā); knowing how to define pleasure, one should pursue pleasure within oneself (sukhavinicchayaṃ ñatvā ajjhattaṃ sukhamanuyuñjeyyā’ti)’—so it was with reference to this that was said.

“Here, bhikkhus, insistence on local language and overriding of normal usage is a state beset by suffering (sadukkho), with conflict (saupaghāto), with vexation (saupāyāso), and with fever (sapariḷāho); it is wrong practice (micchāpaṭipadā).
Therefore this is a state with conflict (saraṇo).
“Here, bhikkhus, non-insistence on local language and non-overriding of normal usage is a state without suffering (adukkho), without conflict (anupaghāto), without vexation (anupāyāso), and without fever (apariḷāho); it is right practice (sammāpaṭipadā).
Therefore this is a state without conflict (araṇo).

“Therefore, bhikkhus, you should train yourselves thus: ‘We shall know the state with conflict (saraṇañca dhammaṃ jānissāma), and we shall know the state without conflict (araṇañca dhammaṃ jānissāma); and knowing the state with conflict (saraṇañca dhammaṃ ñatvā) and knowing the state without conflict (araṇañca dhammaṃ ñatvā), we shall enter upon the way without conflict (araṇapaṭipadaṃ paṭipajjissāmā).’
Now, bhikkhus, Subhuti is a clansman (kulaputto) who has entered upon the way without conflict (araṇapaṭipadaṃ paṭipanno).”

This the Blessed One (bhagavā) said.
Gratified, the monks (bhikkhū) delighted in the Blessed One’s (bhagavato) words (bhāsitaṃ abhinandunti).

The Arahant-Vibhanga Sutta, the ninth, is finished (araṇavibhaṅgasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ navamaṃ).
