Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was living in the Sakyan country at Sāmagāma.

Now on that occasion, Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta had just died in Pāvā.

On his death the Nigaṇṭhas split into two factions. They kept arguing, quarreling, & disputing, stabbing one another with weapons of the mouth: “You don’t understand this Dhamma-Vinaya. I understand this Dhamma-Vinaya.

How can you understand this Dhamma-Vinaya? You’re practising the wrong way. I’m practicing the right way.

I’m being consistent. You’re inconsistent.

What should have been said first you said last, what should have been said last you said first.

What you cogitated so long has been turned inside out.

Your position has been overthrown.

You’re trapped, so go try to release your position or disentangle yourself if you can!”

Nothing but slaughter, as it were, was being committed among the Nigaṇṭha Nāṭa-sons.

And his white-clothed lay disciples were disgusted, dismayed, and disappointed with the Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta’s pupils, as they were with his badly proclaimed and badly expounded Dhamma and Discipline, which was unemancipating, unconducive to peace, expounded by one not fully enlightened, and was now with its shrine broken, left without a refuge.

41. It is like this, the Sāmagāmasutta.

There, sāmagāme means in a village named thus, because of the abundance of sāmāka grass.

Adhunā kālaṅkato means now he has died.

Dvedhikajātā is dvejjhajātā dvebhāgajātā.

Among strife, etc., bhaṇḍanaṃ is a preliminary quarrel; that which is increased by means of punishment, giving, etc., and by means of transgression of the precepts is kalaho; the contradictory speech "you do not know this dharmavinaya" is vivādo.

Vitudantā means vitujjantā.

Sahitaṃ me means my speech is connected with the meaning atthasaṃhitaṃ.

Adhiciṇṇaṃ te viparāvatta means that which is habitual to you, strengthened by long practice, that has turned back, having come to my argument vādaṃ.

Āropito te vādo means a fault doso has been imputed by me upon you.

Cara vādappamokkhāyā means, having taken a food packet, wander around approaching them to seek release from argument, vādappamokkha.

Nibbeṭhehi vā means then free yourself from the imputed argument āropitavādato.

Sace pahosī means if you are able sakkosi.

Vadhoyevā means it is death itself maraṇameva.

Nāṭaputtiyesū means among the disciples antevāsikesu of Nāṭaputta.

Nibbinnarūpā means having an agitated nature ukkaṇṭhitasabhāvā, they do not perform salutations abhivādanādīni.

Virattarūpā means with departed love vigatapemā.

Paṭivānarūpā means having a nature turned away from their prostrations nipaccakiriyato nivattasabhāvā.

Yathā ta means just as, in a doctrine and discipline dhammavinaye of bad exposition etc. durakkhātādi, one should be with a nature disgusted, detached and turned away nibbinnavirattapaṭivānarūpehi, so is the meaning attho of being born jātāti.

Durakkhāte means badly spoken dukkathite.

Duppavedite means badly explained duviññāpite.

Anupasamasaṃvattanike means incapable asamattho of calming passions rāgādīnaṃ .

Bhinnathūpe means with a broken support bhinnapatiṭṭhe.

Here, the support thūpo is like Nāṭaputta for them, but that is considered broken bhinno mato.

Therefore it was said, "with a broken support bhinnathūpe."

Appaṭisaraṇe means without refuge paṭisaraṇavirahite due to the absence abhāvena of that itself tasseva.

Among the Nāṭaputtiyas, disgusted in appearance nibbinnarūpā, detached in appearance virattarūpā, turned away in appearance paṭivānarūpā, just as yathā ta, badly proclaimed durakkhāte, badly taught duppavedite, not conducive to calm anupasamasaṃvattanike, with a broken stupa bhinnathūpe, without refuge appaṭisaraṇe.

Why did this Nāṭaputta, a resident of Nāḷanda, die at Pāvā?

It is said that having heard the qualities of the Buddha buddhaguṇe spoken bhāsite in ten verses gāthāhi with penetrating truth paṭividdhasaccena by the householder gahapatinā Upāli, he vomited hot blood uṇhaṃ lohitaṃ chaḍḍesi.

Then, having taken him unwell aphāsukaṃ gahetvā, they went to Pāvaṃ, and there he died kālamakāsi.

And while dying kālaṃ kurumāno, he said, "My doctrine laddhi is not a means of escape aniyyānikā, it is without essence sārarahitā, we are ruined naṭṭhā, may the remaining people avasesajano not be filled with misery apāyapūrako.

If I were to say, "My teaching sāsanaṃ is not a means of escape aniyyānikā," they will not believe saddahissanti.

Why should I not have two people dvepi jane learn uggaṇhāpeyyaṃ without one method ekanīhārena; they will dispute vivadissanti with each other aññamaññaṃ at my death mamaccayena.

The teacher satthā, having regarded that dispute vivādaṃ paṭicca, will tell one dhamma talk kathaṃ, then they will realize jātissantī the greatness mahantabhāvaṃ of the teaching sāsanassa."
Then, a certain resident approached him and said:
“Venerable sir, you are weak. Please explain to me the essence of this dharma, according to your teacher's authority.”
“Friend, you should take it that after my passing, the dharma is eternal.”
Another approached him and led him to grasp annihilation.
Thus, without making even two people of like mind, he passed away after causing many to learn in various ways.
Having performed his funeral rites, they gathered together and asked each other, "Friend, to whom did the teacher explain the essence?" One stood up and said, "To me."
"What did he explain?" "Eternality."
Another refuted him and said, "He explained the essence to me."
Thus, all of them, "He explained the essence to me, I am the elder," having stirred up a dispute with each other, and having set in motion abuse, insults, and beatings with hands and feet, not going together on one path, they departed in various directions, some becoming householders.

Even while the Blessed One was living, there was no dispute in the community of monks (bhikkhusaṃgha).
Indeed, the teacher himself, as soon as a cause of dispute arose among them, either by going himself or by having those monks summoned, would explain one reason in the teachings leading to mindfulness, gentleness, loving-kindness, reflection, non-violence, and conciliation, and would pacify the dispute.
Thus, even while living, he was the support of the community (saṃgha).
Even while passing into complete extinction (parinirvāyamāṇopi), he passed away after making a cause of non-dispute.
For the Blessed One taught in the sūtras the four great references (cattāro mahāpadesā) (A.
N. 4.180; D.
N. 2.187) that are until this day the support and refuge of the monks.
Similarly, in the khandhakas are taught the four great references (Mahāva.
305) and in the sūtras are stated the four ways of answering questions (cattāri pañhabyākaraṇāni) (A.
N. 4.42).
Therefore, he said: "Ānanda, the dharma (teaching) and the discipline (vinaya) that I have taught and established will be your teacher after my passing away" (D.
N. 2.216).

Now, Cunda the novice, was the younger brother of the Dharma-commander.

The monks addressed him as Cunda the novice both when he had not yet received the higher ordination and continued to do so even after he became a senior monk.

Therefore it was said, “Cunda the novice.”

Why did he approach? It seems that when Nāṭaputta died in Jambudīpa, people were discussing it in various places, saying, "Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta was considered to be the one teacher, but after his death, such a dispute arose among his disciples. But the renunciate Gotama is well-known in Jambudīpa, like the moon and the sun. What kind of dispute will arise among the disciples when the renunciate Gotama has attained final nirvāṇa?”

Having heard this discussion, the senior monk thought, "I will take this matter and report it to the one with ten powers (i.e. the Buddha), and the Teacher, having considered this event, will deliver a discourse."

He left and went to Sāmagāma, where the venerable Ānanda was.

The meaning is that instead of going directly to the Blessed One, he went to the venerable Ānanda, who was his preceptor.

It was thus in his mind, "My preceptor is very wise, he will report this teaching to the Teacher, and then the Teacher will teach the dharma accordingly."

The meaning of "kathāpābhata" is the basis of the story, for the basis is indeed called pābhata.

As it was said:

"Even with a small gift, the wise,
skilled in giving,
can build themselves up,
like a small fire fanned by the wind."

(Jā. 2.1.4);

The meaning of "dassanāyā" is for the sake of seeing.

Did the Blessed One not see [Ānanda] before? No, it is not that he had not seen him before, for this venerable one would attend him eighteen times a day, nine times during the day and nine times during the night.

But although he was willing to go a hundred or a thousand times a day, he would not go without a reason, he would go only after taking up a question.

Thus, being willing to go to him that day, he said this:

The meaning of "ahitāya dukkhāya devamanussāna" is: How does a dispute that has arisen in a monastery, in the midst of the saṃgha, lead to harm and suffering for gods and humans? Just as in the Kosambaka Khandhaka (Mahāva. 451), when a dispute arose between two monks, their disciples argued in that monastery, and the community of nuns, taking their advice, argued, then their supporters argued, and then the guardian deities of the people were divided into two factions.

Here, Ānanda, one bhikkhu reproves another bhikkhu for such and such a grave offence, one involving defeat (pārājika) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantena): ‘Does the venerable one remember having committed such and such a grave offence, one involving defeat or bordering on defeat?’ He says: ‘I do not, friends (āvuso), remember having committed such and such a grave offence, one involving defeat or bordering on defeat.’

Despite the denial, the former presses the latter further: ‘Come on, Venerable (āyasmā), try harder to remember whether you’ve committed such and such a heavy offense, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam)?’

He says: ‘I do not, friends (āvuso), remember having committed such and such a grave offence, one involving defeat or bordering on defeat. But, friends (āvuso), I remember having committed such and such a minor offence (appamattikaṃ āpattiṃ)’

Despite the denial, the former presses the latter further: ‘Come on, Venerable (āyasmā), try harder to remember whether you’ve committed such and such a heavy offense, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam)?’

He says: ‘Indeed, friend (āvuso), having committed a minor offense (appamattikaṃ āpattiṃ), I would acknowledge it even if not asked.

But why would I, having committed such and such a grave offense, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam), not acknowledge it when asked?’

He says: ‘Indeed, friend (āvuso), if you had committed a minor offense (appamattikaṃ āpattiṃ) you would not acknowledge it if not asked, so why would you, having committed such and such a grave offense, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam), acknowledge it when asked?’ Come on, Venerable (āyasmā), try harder to remember whether you’ve committed such and such a heavy offense, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam)?’

He says: ‘I remember, friends (āvuso), having committed such and such a grave offense, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam).

That was said to me in a moment of panic; that was said to me in a moment of confusion – I do not remember committing such and such a grave offence, one involving defeat (pārājikaṃ) or bordering on defeat (pārājikasāmantam)’

Thus, Ānanda, does the pronouncement of bad character against someone take place. And so there comes to be the settlement of some litigations here by the pronouncement of bad character against someone.

53. ‘‘Kathañcānanda , tiṇavatthārako hoti? Idhānanda, bhikkhūnaṃ bhaṇḍanajātānaṃ kalahajātānaṃ vivādāpannānaṃ viharataṃ bahuṃ assāmaṇakaṃ ajjhāciṇṇaṃ hoti bhāsitaparikkantaṃ. Tehānanda, bhikkhūhi sabbeheva samaggehi sannipatitabbaṃ. Sannipatitvā ekatopakkhikānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ byattena byattatarena (sī. pī. ka.) bhikkhunā uṭṭhāyāsanā ekaṃsaṃ cīvaraṃ katvā añjaliṃ paṇāmetvā saṅgho ñāpetabbo – ‘Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. Idaṃ amhākaṃ bhaṇḍanajātānaṃ kalahajātānaṃ vivādāpannānaṃ viharataṃ bahuṃ assāmaṇakaṃ ajjhāciṇṇaṃ bhāsitaparikkantaṃ . Yadi saṅghassa pattakallaṃ, ahaṃ yā ceva imesaṃ āyasmantānaṃ āpatti yā ca attano āpatti, imesañceva āyasmantānaṃ atthāya attano ca atthāya, saṅghamajjhe tiṇavatthārakena deseyyaṃ, ṭhapetvā thullavajjaṃ ṭhapetvā gihipaṭisaṃyutta’’’nti. ‘‘Athāparesaṃ ekatopakkhikānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ byattena bhikkhunā uṭṭhāyāsanā ekaṃsaṃ cīvaraṃ katvā añjaliṃ paṇāmetvā saṅgho ñāpetabbo – ‘Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. Yadi saṅghassa pattakallaṃ, ahaṃ yā ceva imesaṃ āyasmantānaṃ āpatti yā ca attano āpatti, imesañceva āyasmantānaṃ atthāya attano ca atthāya, saṅghamajjhe tiṇavatthārakena deseyyaṃ, ṭhapetvā thullavajjaṃ ṭhapetvā gihipaṭisaṃyutta’’’nti. ‘‘Evaṃ kho, ānanda, tiṇavatthārako hoti, evañca panidhekaccānaṃ adhikaraṇānaṃ vūpasamo hoti yadidaṃ – tiṇavatthārakena.
53. How, Ānanda, does the covering over with grass ( tiṇavatthāraka) occur? Here, Ānanda, when monks have taken to quarreling and brawling and are deep in disputes, they may have said and done many things improper for a recluse. Those bhikkhus should all meet together in concord. Then, having met together, a capable ( byatta) monk among the monks on one side (or holding one view) should rise from his seat, put his upper robe over one shoulder, pay respect with joined palms, and inform the saṃgha:

‘Bhante, may the saṃgha hear me. When we took to quarreling and brawling and were deep in disputes, we said and did many things improper for a recluse. If it seems appropriate to the saṃgha, then for the benefit of these venerables and myself, I’ll confess in the midst of the saṃgha by the method of covering over with grass ( tiṇavatthāraka), except for heavy offenses (thullavajja) and offenses connected with householders (gihipaṭisaṃyutta)’’.

Then a capable monk among the monks on one side should rise from his seat, put his upper robe over one shoulder, pay respect with joined palms, and inform the saṃgha:

‘Bhante, may the saṃgha hear me. When we took to quarreling and brawling and were deep in disputes, we said and did many things improper for a recluse. If it seems appropriate to the saṃgha, then for the benefit of these venerables and myself, I’ll confess in the midst of the saṃgha by the method of covering over with grass, except for heavy offenses and offenses connected with householders.’

Thus, Ānanda, is the covering over with grass ( tiṇavatthāraka) . And so there comes to be the settlement (vūpasama) of some litigations (adhikaraṇānaṃ) here by the covering over with grass ( tiṇavatthāraka).
53.Kathañcānanda, tiṇavatthārakoti ettha idaṃ kammaṃ tiṇavatthārakasadisattā tiṇavatthārakoti vuttaṃ. Yathā hi gūthaṃ vā muttaṃ vā ghaṭṭiyamānaṃ duggandhatāya bādhati, tiṇehi avattharitvā suppaṭicchāditassa panassa so gandho na bādhati, evameva yaṃ adhikaraṇaṃ mūlānumūlaṃ gantvā vūpasamiyamānaṃ kakkhaḷattāya vāḷattāya bhedāya saṃvattati, taṃ iminā kammena vūpasantaṃ gūthaṃ viya tiṇavatthārakena paṭicchannaṃ vūpasantaṃ hotīti idaṃ kammaṃ tiṇavatthārakasadisattā tiṇavatthārakoti vuttaṃ. Tassa idhānanda, bhikkhūnaṃ bhaṇḍanajātānantiādivacanena ākāramattameva dassitaṃ, khandhake āgatāyeva panettha kammavācā pamāṇaṃ. Ṭhapetvā thullavajjaṃ ṭhapetvā gihipaṭisaṃyuttanti. Ettha pana thullavajjanti thūllavajjaṃ pārājikañceva saṅghādisesañca. Gihipaṭisaṃyuttanti gihīnaṃ hīnena khuṃsanavambhanadhammikapaṭissavesu āpannā āpatti. Adhikaraṇānanti idha āpattādhikaraṇameva veditabbaṃ. Kiccādhikaraṇassa pana vasena idha na kiñci vuttaṃ. Kiñcāpi na vuttaṃ, sammukhāvinayeneva panassa vūpasamo hotīti veditabbo.
53. How, Ānanda, is it a 'covering-over like grass' ( tiṇavatthārako)? Here, this action is called "covering-over like grass" because of its similarity to covering-over like grass ( tiṇavatthāraka-sadisattā ).

Just as, if excrement ( gūthaṃ ) or urine ( muttaṃ ) is being stirred, it stinks and is offensive, but if it is covered over with grass ( tiṇehi avattharitvā ) and well-concealed, that smell is not offensive, so too, any legal question ( adhikaraṇaṃ ) that, after going from root to root ( mūlānumūlaṃ gantvā ) and being appeased ( vūpasamiyamānaṃ ), tends towards harshness ( kakkhaḷattāya ), violence ( vāḷattāya ), and division ( bhedāya saṃvattati ), is calmed by this action, covered over like excrement is covered by grass, and is appeased—therefore, this action is called "covering-over like grass" because of its similarity to covering-over like grass.

Regarding this, by the statement "Here, Ānanda, among the monks who are quarrelsome (bhikkhūnaṃ bhaṇḍanajātāna)," etc., only a general idea (ākāramattameva) has been shown; in the khandhaka, the formal act (kammavācā) itself, which has come down [to us], is the standard (pramāṇaṃ).

"[Excepting] a grave offense (thullavajjaṃ), [excepting matters] connected with laypeople (gihipaṭisaṃyuttaṃ)."

Here, thullavajjaṃ means a grave offense, a defeat ( pārājikañceva ), and a formal suspension ( saṃghādisesañca ).

"Gihipaṭisaṃyuttaṃ" means an offense ( āpatti ) incurred by laypeople in connection with base flattery, boasting, and making deceptive promises related to the Dharma.

"Adhikaraṇāna" here should be understood as offenses regarding legal questions ( āpattādhikaraṇameva veditabbaṃ ).

However, nothing has been said here concerning the procedural questions ( kiccādhikaraṇassa pana vasena ).

Even though nothing has been said, it should be understood that its appeasement ( vūpasamo hoti ) comes about only through the sammukhāvinaya.

54. ‘‘Chayime , ānanda, dhammā sāraṇīyā piyakaraṇā garukaraṇā saṅgahāya avivādāya sāmaggiyā ekībhāvāya saṃvattanti. Katame cha? Ayampi dhammo sāraṇīyo piyakaraṇo garukaraṇo saṅgahāya avivādāya sāmaggiyā ekībhāvāya saṃvattati. ‘‘Puna caparaṃ, ānanda, bhikkhu – ye te lābhā dhammikā dhammaladdhā antamaso pattapariyāpannamattampi tathārūpehi lābhehi – apaṭivibhattabhogī hoti, sīlavantehi sabrahmacārīhi sādhāraṇabhogī. Taṃ vo bhavissati dīgharattaṃ hitāya sukhāyā’’ti. Idamavoca bhagavā. Attamano āyasmā ānando bhagavato bhāsitaṃ abhinandīti. Sāmagāmasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ catutthaṃ.
54. Ananda, there are these six memorable qualities that create love and respect, and conduce to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

What are the six? Here, Ananda, a bhikkhu maintains bodily acts of loving-kindness (mettā) both in public and in private towards his companions in the holy life. This too is a condition (dhamma) that is conducive to amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity.

Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu maintains verbal acts of loving-kindness (mettā) both in public and in private towards his companions in the holy life. This too is a condition (dhamma) that is conducive to amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity.

Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu maintains mental acts of loving-kindness (mettā) both in public and in private towards his companions in the holy life. This too is a condition (dhamma) that is conducive to amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity.

Furthermore, Ānanda, whatever gains a bhikkhu obtains that are in accordance with the Dharma (dhamma) and have been righteously gained, even the mere contents of his alms bowl, he shares without reservation with his virtuous companions in the holy life. This too is a condition (dhamma) that is conducive to amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity.

Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu dwells both in public and in private possessing in common with his companions in the holy life those virtues (śīla) that are unbroken, untorn, unblotched, unmottled, liberating, commended by the wise, not misapprehended, and conducive to concentration (samādhi). This too is a condition (dhamma) that is conducive to amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity.

Furthermore, Ānanda, a bhikkhu dwells both in public and in private possessing in common with his companions in the holy life that view (dṛṣṭi) that is noble and emancipating, and leads the one who practises in accordance with it to the complete destruction of suffering (duḥkha). This too is a condition (dhamma) that is conducive to amiability, that engenders feelings of endearment, engenders feelings of respect, leading to a sense of fellowship, a lack of disputes, harmony, & a state of unity.

These are the six memorable qualities that create love and respect, and conduce to helpfulness, to non-dispute, to concord, and to unity.

Ānanda, if you undertake and maintain these six memorable qualities, do you see any course of speech, trivial or gross, that you could not endure?

"No, venerable sir."

Therefore, Ānanda, undertake and maintain these six memorable qualities. That will lead to your welfare and happiness for a long time.

Thus spoke the Lord.

The venerable Ānanda was satisfied and delighted in the Blessed One’s words.

The Sāmagāma Sutta, the fourth [discourse], is finished.
