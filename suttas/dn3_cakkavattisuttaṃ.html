80. Thus have I heard:

Once the Blessed One was staying among the Magadhans at Mātulā.

There the Fortunate One addressed the monks, saying: “Monks!”

“Venerable sir,” those monks replied to the Blessed One.

The Blessed One said this: “Be islands unto yourselves, monks, refuges unto yourselves, seeking no external refuge; with the Dharma as your island, the Dharma as your refuge, seeking no other refuge.

And how, monks, does a monk live as an island unto himself, a refuge unto himself, seeking no other refuge; with the Dharma as his island, the Dharma as his refuge, seeking no other refuge? Here, monks, a monk abides contemplating the body as body, earnestly, clearly comprehending, and mindfully, having overcome desire and sorrow in regard to the world.

He abides contemplating feelings as feelings,… mind as mind,… mental objects as mental objects, earnestly, clearly comprehending, and mindfully, having put aside hankering and fretting for the world.

This is how, monks, a monk lives as an island unto himself, a refuge unto himself, seeking no other refuge; with the dharma as his island, the dharma as his refuge, seeking no other refuge.”

“Keep to your own preserves, monks, to your ancestral haunts.

If you conduct yourselves in your own preserves, in your own ancestral territory, Māra will find no opportunity, Māra will find no support.

It is just by the building-up of wholesome states (kuśalānām dharmānām), monks, that this merit (puṇyaṃ) increases.”

80. Thus I have heard: the Cakkavattisutta.

Here follows an explanation of words that are not obvious: "Mātulāya" means "in a city called Evaṃnāmaka."

Having made that city a pasture-ground, he lived in a nearby forest grove.

“There, the Blessed One addressed the monks”– here follows a sequential account:

It is said that at the time of the origin of this sūtra, the Blessed One, at dawn, arising from the attainment of great compassion, while surveying the world, having seen the dharma-understanding of eighty-four thousand living beings dwelling in the city of Mātula through this sūtra-discourse illuminating the lineage of the future, arrived in the city of Mātula early in the morning, surrounded by a retinue of twenty thousand monks.

The Kṣatriyas dwelling in the city of Mātula, having heard "The Blessed One has arrived," went out to meet him, invited the One with Ten Strengths, and, having respectfully led [him] into the city, prepared a seat, had the Blessed One sit on a precious divan, and gave a great donation to the bhikṣu-saṃgha headed by the Buddha.

The Blessed One, having finished his meal, thought: "If I were to teach the dharma to these people in this place, this region is crowded; there will be no space for the people to stand or sit, but there should be a great gathering."

Then, without giving approval of the meal to the royal families, he took his bowl and left the city.

The people thought: "The Teacher goes without giving approval to us; truly, the meal was displeasing. It is not possible to grasp the mind of the Buddhas. Trusting the Buddhas is like grasping a cobra with raised hood by the neck; come, let us ask forgiveness of the Tathāgata."

All the city-dwellers departed together with the Blessed One.

While going, the Blessed One saw a single mātula-tree in the Magadha territory, standing, complete with branches and twigs, providing dense shade, with a surface of about the size of a hide, and thought, “If I sit down at the base of this tree, there will be space for the great assembly to sit and stand."

Having turned back and gone off the road, approaching the base of the tree, he looked at Ānanda Thera, the treasurer of the dharma.

The Thera, knowing from the sign of the glance alone that "the Teacher wishes to sit down," spread out the Sugata's great robe and gave [it to him].

The Blessed One sat down on the prepared seat.

Then the people sat down in front of him.

On both sides and in back was the bhikṣu-saṃgha; in the sky, the gods stood. Thus, in the midst of the great assembly, there the Blessed One addressed the bhikṣus.

"Those bhikṣus" means the bhikṣus sitting there, accepting the dharma.

"Attadīpā" means "live making yourselves a lamp, a refuge, a shelter, a destination, an ultimate support." This is the meaning.

"Attasaraṇā" – this is a synonym for that very [term].

"Anaññasaraṇā" – this is a term rejecting other refuges.

For another is not a refuge for another, because another's effort does not purify another.

And it was said, "Oneself is indeed one's own lord; who else could be the lord?" (Dhp. 160).

Therefore [he] said, "Anaññasaraṇā."

What here is the self (ātmā)? It is the mundane and supramundane dharma.

Therefore [he] said: "dharma-lamp, dharma-refuge, other-less refuge."

"Contemplating the body in the body," etc., are extensively explained in the Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna.

Those bhikṣus are lamps unto themselves, refuges unto themselves, and refuges with no other.

"Gocare" means "in a suitable place to wander."

"Sake" means "in one's own possession."

"Pettike visaye" means "in the ancestral realm."
" caratanti carantānaṃ:" "carata" means for those who wander.
"‘‘caranta’’ntipi pāṭho, ayamevattho:" "caranta" is also a reading, the meaning is the same.
" na lacchatīti na labhissati na passissati:" "na lacchati" means he will not obtain, he will not see.
" māroti devaputtamāropi, maccumāropi, kilesamāropi:" "māra" means he raised up the son of a god, he raised up death, he raised up defilements.
" otāranti randhaṃ chiddaṃ vivaraṃ:" "otāra" means a hole, a gap, an opening.

“But what, your majesty, is the duty of an Āryan wheel-turning monarch?”

“It is this, my son: Yourself depending on the dharma, honouring the dharma, revering the dharma, cherishing the dharma, doing homage to the dharma and venerating the dharma, having the dharma as your badge and banner, acknowledging the dharma as your master, you should establish guard, ward and protection according to dharma for your own household, your troops, your nobles and vassals, for brāhmaṇas and householders, town and country folk, ascetics and brāhmaṇas, for beasts and birds.

Let no crime prevail in your kingdom, and to those who are in need, give property.

And whatever ascetics and brāhmaṇas in your kingdom have renounced the life of sensual infatuation and are devoted to forbearance and gentleness, each one taming himself, each one calming himself and each one striving for the end of craving, if from time to time they should come to you and consult you as to what is wholesome (kuśala) and what is unwholesome (akuśala), what is blameworthy (sāvajja) and what is blameless (anavajja), what is to be followed and what is not to be followed, and what action will in the long run lead to harm and suffering, or what action will in the long run lead to welfare and happiness, having listened to them, you should reject what is unwholesome and undertake and adopt what is wholesome. That, my son, is the duty of an Āryan wheel-turning monarch.”

110. “Keep to your own preserves, monks, to your ancestral haunts. If you do so, your life-span will increase, your beauty will increase, your happiness will increase, your wealth will increase, your power will increase.

‘And what is beauty for a monk? Here, a monk is virtuous, he dwells restrained by the restraint of the pātimokkha, accomplished in conduct and resort, seeing danger in the slightest faults, undertaking and training himself in the rules of training. That is beauty for a monk.

‘And what is happiness for a monk? Here, a monk, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states, enters upon and abides in the first jhāna, which is accompanied by applied and sustained thought, with rapture and pleasure born of seclusion. With the stilling of applied and sustained thought…the second jhāna…the third jhāna…the fourth jhāna he enters upon and abides. That is happiness for a monk.

<p rend="centre">Cakkavattisuttavaṇṇanā niṭṭhitā.

110. “Here, monks, in the monk’s increase in āyusmi,”— monks, what I said to you, “I will increase you in life-span (āyunāpi vaḍḍhissathāti),”—there, this is what is meant by "in the monk’s āyusmi," this is the cause of long life. Therefore, he shows that these four constituents of psychic power should be cultivated by you who desire to increase in life-span.

“In beauty (vaṇṇasmiṃ),”—what I said to you, “I will increase you in beauty (vaṇṇenapi vaḍḍhissathāti),”—this is the cause of beauty there. For the bodily beauty of the virtuous one increases by means of non-remorse, etc., and the beauty of qualities increases by means of the beauty of fame. Therefore, he shows that you who desire to increase in beauty should be endowed with virtue.

“In happiness (sukhasmiṃ),”—what I said to you, “I will increase you in happiness (sukhenapi vaḍḍhissathāti),”—there, this is the happiness of jhāna, which is of various kinds, such as the joy and happiness born of seclusion. Therefore, he shows that these four jhānas should be cultivated by you who desire to increase in happiness.

“In wealth (bhogasmiṃ),”—what I said to you, “I will increase you in wealth (bhogenapi vaḍḍhissathāti),”—this is the enjoyment of the divine abodes, which spreads in all directions, bringing non-opposition to limitless beings, and has eleven blessings, beginning with pleasant sleep. Therefore, he shows that these divine abodes should be cultivated by you who desire to increase in wealth.

“In strength (balasmiṃ),”— what I said to you, “I will increase you in strength (balenapi vaḍḍhissathāti),”—this is the strength known as the fruit of arahantship, which arises at the end of the destruction of the corruptions. Therefore, he shows that effort should be made to attain arahantship by you who desire to increase in strength.

“Just as this, monks, is the power of Māra (yathayidaṃ, bhikkhave, mārabalaṃ),”—just as this power of Māra, the son of the gods, the Māra of death, and the Māra of the corruptions, is difficult to endure and overcome, so I do not see even one other power in the world. This very fruit of arahantship overpowers, overcomes, and subdues that power as well. Therefore, he shows that effort should be made here itself.

“Thus is merit (evamidaṃ puññaṃ),”—thus also this supramundane merit increases up to the destruction of the corruptions. Concluding the connection of skillful things that lead to liberation, he concluded the teaching with the peak of arahantship. At the end of the discourse, twenty thousand monks attained arahantship, and eighty-four thousand beings drank the nectar of immortality.

The Cakkavattisutta Commentary in the Sumaṅgalavilāsinī, Commentary to the Dīgha Nikāya, is finished.
