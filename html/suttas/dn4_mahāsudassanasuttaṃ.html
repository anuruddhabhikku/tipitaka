241. Thus have I heard: On one occasion the Blessed One was staying at Kusinārā in the Upavattana, the Sāl grove of the Mallas, between the twin Sāl trees, at the time of his parinirvāṇa.

Then venerable Ānanda approached the Blessed One, and having approached, bowed down to the Blessed One and sat down to one side.

Seated to one side, venerable Ānanda said to the Blessed One: “Do not, venerable sir (bhante), let the Blessed One attain parinirvāṇa in this small town, this town of wattle and daub, this branch town.

There are, venerable sir (bhante), other great cities.

Namely: Campā, Rājagaha, Sāvatthī, Sāketa, Kosambī, Bārāṇasī; let the Blessed One attain parinirvāṇa there.

For in those places there are many wealthy nobles (khattiyamahāsālā), wealthy Brahmins (brāhmaṇamahāsālā), and wealthy householders (gahapatimahāsālā) who are devoted to the tathāgata. They will perform the funeral rites (sarīrapūjā) for the tathāgata.”

241. Thus have I heard: the Mahāsudassana Sutta.

Here, the description of the words that did not occur before [is as follows]: In "sabbaratanamayo" (made of all jewels), one brick was made of gold (sovaṇṇamayā), one of silver (rūpiyamayā), one of beryl (veḷuriyamayā), one of crystal (phalikamayā), one of red stone (lohitaṅkamayā), one of agate (masāragallamayā), and one of all jewels (sabbaratanamayā). This wall (pākāro), inside all the walls, was sixty cubits in height (saṭṭhihattho).

But some elders say, "A city is suitable to be seen by those who stand inside and look around, therefore, the entire outer wall was sixty cubits, the rest were successively lower."

Some [say], "It is suitable to be seen by those who stand outside and look around, therefore, the entire innermost [wall] was sixty cubits, the rest were successively lower."

Some [say], "It is suitable to be seen by those who stand both inside and outside and look around, therefore, the middle wall was sixty cubits, and inside and outside [of that wall], there were three [walls] each that were successively lower."

Esikā means a gate pillar.

Tiporisaṅgā means one porisa, which is five cubits (hatthaṃ) according to the cubit (hattha) of a man (purisaṃ) of middle stature (majjhimapurisassa); therefore, tiporisaparikkhepā means a circumference of fifteen cubits (pannarasahatthaparimāṇāti attho).

How were they situated? On the outer side (bāhirapasse) of the city (nagarassa), there was one [pillar] relying on each large gate wing (mahādvārabāhaṃ nissāya ekeko), one [pillar] relying on each small gate wing (khuddakadvārabāhaṃ nissāya ekeko), [and] three [pillars] between the large and small gates (mahādvārakhuddakadvārānaṃ antarā tayo tayoti).

Among the rows of palm trees (tālapantīsu) made of all jewels (sabbaratanamayānaṃ tālānaṃ), one was made of gold (sovaṇṇamayanti); it should be understood that the characteristic described for the wall (pākāre vuttalakkhaṇameva) is the same [here]. This same method (eseva nayo) [applies] to the leaves and fruits (paṇṇaphalesupi).

Those rows of palm trees (tā pana tālapantiyo) were eighty cubits in height (asītihatthā ubbedhena), standing as single [trees] (ekekā hutvā ṭhitā) in the space between the walls (pākārantare) on a level (samatale) ground area with scattered sand (vippakiṇṇavāluke bhūmibhāge).

Esikā, tiporisaṅgā

Vaggū means clever, beautiful.

Rajanīyo means capable of delighting.

Khamanīyo means it is bearable even when being heard for a day; it is not disgusting.

Madanīyo means generating the intoxication and pride of men (mānamadapurisamadajanano).

Pañcaṅgikassā means endowed with these five parts (imehi pañcahaṅgehi samannāgatassa): ātataṃ (covered), vitataṃ (stretched), ātatavitataṃ (covered and stretched), susiraṃ (hollow), ghanaṃ (solid).

There, ātataṃ is a single-faced drum (ekatalaṃ tūriyaṃ) among drums and so on (bherīādīsu) covered with skin (cammapariyonaddhesu).

Vitataṃ means double-faced.

Ātatavitataṃ means covered all around.

Susiraṃ means flute and so on (vaṃsādi).

Ghanaṃ means cymbals and so on (sammādi).

Suvinītassā means well-composed (sumucchitassa) through actions such as tightening and loosening (ākaḍḍhanasithilakaraṇādīhi).

Suppaṭitāḷitassā means well-struck (suṭṭhu paṭitāḷitassa) to know the state of being established in measure (pamāṇe ṭhitabhāvajānanatthaṃ).

Sukusalehi samannāhatassā means played by those who are clever and skilled (ye vādituṃ chekā kusalā, tehi vāditassa) at playing [instruments].

Dhuttā means dice players (akkhadhuttā).

Soṇḍā means spirituous liquor drinkers (surāsoṇḍā).

They, through the desire to appear again and again (punappunaṃ pātukāmatāvasena), [experience] thirst (pipāsā).

In performances (paricāresu), (D. II, 132) they danced and played (naccantā kīḷiṃsu) moving their hands or feet (hatthaṃ vā pādaṃ vā cāletvā).

Vaggū, rajanīyo, khamanīyo, madanīyo, pañcaṅgikassā, ātataṃ, vitataṃ, ātatavitataṃ, susiraṃ.
Dense, well-disciplined, well-trained, well-controlled by skillful means, thirst is dispelled, O Soṇḍā.

Kusāvatī, the Royal Capital

Once upon a time, Ānanda, there was a king named Mahāsudassana, a Kṣatriya, anointed to rule (khattiyo muddhābhisitto), a wheel-turning monarch, righteous and following the Dharma (chakravartin dharmiko dhammarājā (Mahāparinibbānasutta), victorious in the four directions, who had achieved stability in his realm.

And this Kusinārā, Ānanda, was the capital city of King Mahāsudassana, named Kusāvatī. It extended twelve yojanas from east to west, and seven yojanas from north to south.

Kusāvatī, Ānanda, the capital city, was powerful, rich, prosperous, crowded with people, and well-supplied with food.

Just as, Ānanda, the city of the gods, Āḷakamandā, is powerful, rich, and prosperous, crowded with yakṣas, and well-supplied with food, so too, Ānanda, was the capital city of Kusāvatī powerful, rich, prosperous, crowded with people, and well-supplied with food.

Kusāvatī, Ānanda, the capital city, was never silent with ten sounds day or night, that is to say: the sound of elephants, the sound of horses, the sound of chariots, the sound of kettle drums, the sound of side drums, the sound of lutes, the sound of singing, the sound of cymbals, the sound of gongs, and as the tenth, the sound of, "Eat, drink, and be merry!"

Kusāvatī, Ānanda, the capital city, was surrounded by seven encircling walls (sattahi pākārehi parikkhittā ahosi). One wall was made of gold (sovaṇṇamayo), one of silver (rūpiyamayo), one of beryl (veḷuriyamayo), one of crystal (phalikamayo), one of ruby (lohitaṅkamayo), one of emerald (masāragallamayo), and one of all sorts of gems (sabbaratanamayo).

In Kusāvatī, Ānanda, the royal capital, the gates were of four colors. One gate was made of gold (sovaṇṇamayaṃ), one of silver (rūpiyamayaṃ), one of beryl (veḷuriyamayaṃ), and one of crystal (phalikamayaṃ).

At each gate, seven pillars (satta esikā) were set, sunk three or four purisas deep, and twelve purisas in height. One pillar (ekā esikā) was made of gold (sovaṇṇamayā), one of silver (rūpiyamayā), one of beryl (veḷuriyamayā), one of crystal (phalikamayā), one of ruby (lohitaṅkamayā), one of emerald (masāragallamayā), and one of all sorts of gems (sabbaratanamayā).

Kusāvatī, Ānanda, the capital city, was surrounded by seven rows of palm trees (sattahi tālapantīhi parikkhittā ahosi). One row of palm trees (ekā tālapanti) was made of gold (sovaṇṇamayā), one of silver (rūpiyamayā), one of beryl (veḷuriyamayā), one of crystal (phalikamayā), one of ruby (lohitaṅkamayā), one of emerald (masāragallamayā), and one of all sorts of gems (sabbaratanamayā).

The gold palm trees (sovaṇṇamayassa tālassa) had trunks of gold (sovaṇṇamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of silver (rūpiyamayāni).

The silver palm trees (rūpiyamayassa tālassa) had trunks of silver (rūpiyamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of gold (sovaṇṇamayāni).

The beryl palm trees (veḷuriyamayassa tālassa) had trunks of beryl (veḷuriyamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of crystal (phalikamayāni).

The crystal palm trees (phalikamayassa tālassa) had trunks of crystal (phalikamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of beryl (veḷuriyamayāni).

The ruby palm trees (lohitaṅkamayassa tālassa) had trunks of ruby (lohitaṅkamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of emerald (masāragallamayāni).

The emerald palm trees (masāragallamayassa tālassa) had trunks of emerald (masāragallamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of ruby (lohitaṅkamayāni).

The palm trees of all sorts of gems (sabbaratanamayassa tālassa) had trunks of all sorts of gems (sabbaratanamayo khandho ahosi), and leaves (pattāni) and fruits (phalāni) of all sorts of gems (sabbaratanamayāni).

The sound of those palm trees, Ānanda, when stirred by the wind (tāsaṃ kho panānanda, tālapantīnaṃ vāteritānaṃ saddo ahosi), was delightful (vaggu), pleasing (rajanīyo), and agreeable (khamanīyo).
And intoxicating.

Just as, Ānanda, the sound of a five-piece orchestra, well-tuned, well-beaten, and played by skilled musicians, is delightful, pleasing, agreeable, and intoxicating, so too, Ānanda, was the sound of those palm leaves stirred by the wind delightful, pleasing, agreeable, and intoxicating.

And, Ānanda, those libertines and drunkards in the capital city of Kuśāvatī at that time, were refreshed by the sound of those palm leaves stirred by the wind.

‘Ānanda, King Mahāsudassana was endowed with the seven treasures (sapta ratnāṇi) and the four powers (catūhi iddhi).

What are the seven? Here, Ānanda, on the fifteenth day, the uposatha day, when King Mahāsudassana had washed his head and gone up to the excellent upper terrace of the palace to observe the uposatha, a divine wheel-treasure (divyaṃ cakraratanaṃ) appeared to him, with a thousand spokes (sahasrāraṃ), with a felly (sanemikaṃ), with a hub (sanābhikaṃ), complete in every way (sabbākāraparipūraṃ).

On seeing it, King Mahāsudassana thought: “I have heard that when a head-anointed noble king (khattiyassa muddhāvasittassa) sees such a wheel on the uposatha day of the fifteenth, having washed his head and ascended to the upper palace chamber for the uposatha, there appears to him the divine wheel-treasure (divyaṃ cakkaratanaṃ) with its thousand spokes (sahasrāraṃ), its tire (sanemikaṃ), and its nave (sanābhikaṃ), complete in every aspect (sabbākāraparipūraṃ), then that king becomes a Wheel-turning Monarch (rājā cakkavattī).”

May I become such a Wheel-turning Monarch (rājā cakkavattī)!”

The Elder, having come from inside the village, entered the leaf hut and sat down on the wooden platform. those eighty-four thousand couches – made of gold ( sovaṇṇamaya ), made of silver ( rūpiyamaya ), made of ivory ( dantamaya ), made of hardwood (sāramaya), covered with long-fleeced woolen blankets (gonakatthatāni), with patchwork quilts (paṭikatthatāni), with embroidered coverlets (paṭalikatthatāni), with coverings of choice deer hide (kadalimigapavarapaccattharaṇāni) – with awnings (sauttaracchadāni), with cushions red on both sides (ubhatolohitakūpadhānāni) were mine; those eighty-four thousand elephants adorned with gold (sovaṇṇālaṅkārāni), with golden banners (sovaṇṇadhajāni), covered with nets of gold (hemajālapaṭicchannāni), headed by the Upoṣatha Nāga-king, were mine; those eighty-four thousand horses adorned with gold (sovaṇṇālaṅkārāni), with golden banners (sovaṇṇadhajāni), covered with nets of gold (hemajālapaṭicchannāni), headed by the Vālāhaka horse-king, were mine; those eighty-four thousand chariots with coverings of lion skins (sīhacammaparivārāni), coverings of tiger skins (byagghacammaparivārāni), coverings of leopard skins (dīpicammaparivārāni), coverings of yellow woolen blankets (paṇḍukambalaparivārāni), adorned with gold (sovaṇṇālaṅkārāni), with golden banners (sovaṇṇadhajāni), covered with nets of gold (hemajālapaṭicchannāni), headed by the Vejayanta chariot, were mine; those eighty-four thousand jewels, of which the jewel-gem was the chief, were mine; those eighty-four thousand women, of which Subhaddādevī was the chief, were mine; those eighty-four thousand householders, of which the householder-gem was the chief, were mine; those eighty-four thousand nobles, the followers of the counselor-gem, were mine; those eighty-four thousand milk cows, easy to milk, with bronze milk pails, were mine; those eighty-four koṭis of thousands of cloths, of fine linen (khomasukhumānaṃ), of fine cotton (kappāsikasukhumānaṃ), of fine silk (koseyyasukhumānaṃ), of fine wool (kambalasukhumānaṃ), were mine; and twice daily, morning and evening, eighty-four thousand rice-soup dishes were brought to me as food.

271. "That which I, at that time, inhabit (ajjhāvasāmī)" means that the city where I dwell is only one; in the remaining [cities], sons, daughters, and slaves lived. The same method applies to palaces and peaked houses. Among couches and so on, he enjoys only one couch; the rest are for the enjoyment of his sons and so on. Among women, only one attends to him; the rest are merely attendants. "I put on (paridahāmī)" means I wear only one pair of garments; the rest are for the sixteen hundred thousand men who wander around, surrounded by a retinue of over eighty thousand. "I eat (bhuñjāmī)" means I eat only a nāḷi measure of rice with the highest restraint; he shows that the rest is for the eight hundred thousand men who wander around, surrounded by a retinue of over forty thousand. One pot of cooked rice is enough for ten people.

However, it should be understood that these eighty-four thousand cities, thousands of palaces, and thousands of peaked houses arose from the overflow (nissandena) of a single leaf hut. The eighty-four thousand couches arose from the overflow of the couch given for lying down. The eighty-four thousand elephants, thousands of horses, and thousands of chariots arose from the overflow of the seat given for sitting. The eighty-four thousand jewels arose from the overflow of a single lamp. The eighty-four thousand ponds arose from the overflow of a single pond. The eighty-four thousand women, thousands of sons, thousands of householders, containers for enjoyment, bowls, dishes, water filters, ārākaṇṭaka, pipphalaka, nail clippers, keys, earwax removers, footstools, shoes, umbrellas, scissors, staffs, [all arose from the overflow of his] giving. The eighty-four thousand cows arose from the overflow of giving dairy products. The eighty-four thousand crores of cloths arose from the overflow of giving clothes for wearing and wrapping. The eighty-four thousand pots of cooked rice arose from the overflow of giving food.

272. ‘‘Passānanda, sabbete saṅkhārā atītā niruddhā vipariṇatā. Evaṃ aniccā kho, ānanda, saṅkhārā; evaṃ addhuvā kho, ānanda, saṅkhārā; evaṃ anassāsikā kho, ānanda, saṅkhārā! Yāvañcidaṃ, ānanda , alameva sabbasaṅkhāresu nibbindituṃ, alaṃ virajjituṃ, alaṃ vimuccituṃ. ‘‘Chakkhattuṃ kho panāhaṃ, ānanda, abhijānāmi imasmiṃ padese sarīraṃ nikkhipitaṃ, tañca kho rājāva samāno cakkavattī dhammiko dhammarājā cāturanto vijitāvī janapadatthāvariyapatto sattaratanasamannāgato, ayaṃ sattamo sarīranikkhepo. Na kho panāhaṃ, ānanda, taṃ padesaṃ samanupassāmi sadevake loke samārake sabrahmake sassamaṇabrāhmaṇiyā pajāya sadevamanussāya yattha tathāgato aṭṭhamaṃ sarīraṃ nikkhipeyyā’’ti. Idamavoca bhagavā, idaṃ vatvāna sugato athāparaṃ etadavoca satthā – ‘‘Aniccā vata saṅkhārā, uppādavayadhammino; Uppajjitvā nirujjhanti, tesaṃ vūpasamo sukho’’ti. Mahāsudassanasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ catutthaṃ.
272. ‘See, Ānanda, all those conditioned states (saṅkhārā) of the past have vanished and changed! Thus, Ānanda, conditioned states (saṅkhārā) are impermanent (aniccā); thus, Ānanda, conditioned states (saṅkhārā) are unstable (addhuvā); thus, Ānanda, conditioned states (saṅkhārā) can bring us no comfort (anassāsikā)! Ānanda, it is enough to experience revulsion (nibbindituṃ) towards all formations (sabbasaṅkhāresu), enough to become dispassionate (virajjituṃ) towards them, enough to be liberated (vimuccituṃ) from them.’

‘Six times, Ānanda, I recall discarding the body (sarīraṃ nikkhipitaṃ) in this place, and at the seventh time I discarded it as a wheel-turning monarch (cakkavattī), a righteous king (dhammiko dhammarājā) who had conquered the four quarters (cāturanto) and established a firm rule (vijitāvī janapadatthāvariyapatto), and who possessed the seven treasures (sattaratanasamannāgato). This is the seventh discarding of the body (sarīranikkhepo). But, Ānanda, I do not see any place in this world (sadevake loke samārake sabrahmake) with its devas and maras and Brahmas, or in this generation with its ascetics and Brahmins (sassamaṇabrāhmaṇiyā pajāya), princes and people (sadevamanussāya), where the Tathāgata will for an eighth time discard the body (sarīraṃ nikkhipeyya.’

Thus spoke the Blessed One (bhagavā). Having said this, the Well-Gone (sugato), the Teacher (satthā) then said further:

"Impermanent (aniccā) indeed are conditioned states (saṅkhārā), by nature they rise and decay;

Having arisen, they are destroyed; their passing away is bliss (tesaṃ vūpasamo sukho."’

The Great Splendor Discourse (mahāsudassanasuttaṃ) is finished, the fourth.

272. Thus, the Blessed One, having extensively described the prosperity of Mahāsudassana, starting from the beginning, and showing all that as children's play with sand houses, said, ending with "seeing bliss" (passānandā).

There, "having undergone change" (vipariṇatā) means having gone to a state of non-perception, like an extinguished lamp due to the abandonment of nature.

"Thus, impermanent are conditioned things (evaṃ aniccā kho, ānanda, saṅkhārā)" means thus, having been, they are impermanent due to the state of non-being.

In this way, the Blessed One, just as a man, having built a ladder on a campaka tree a hundred cubits high, and having climbed up, and having taken a campaka flower, would descend, releasing the ladder, just so, having ascended to the prosperity of Mahāsudassana, which is a hundred thousand million leagues high, and having stood at the top of the prosperity, having taken the characteristic of impermanence, he descended, releasing the ladder.

Therefore, formerly, King Vasabha, having heard the long reciters of the sūtra reciting this sūtra in the eastern side of the iron palace, on the ambala support, thinking, "What, sir, has been said here by my ancestor? He only describes the prosperity in the place where he ate and drank," at the time when "Thus, impermanent are conditioned things, ānanda," was said, "This, sir, having seen with five eyes, was thus said by the one with vision," having contracted his left hand, and having clapped with his right hand, he gave approval, his heart delighted, saying, "Good, good!"

"Thus, unstable (evaṃ addhuvā)" means thus, like water bubbles and so on, devoid of a stable nature.

"Thus, without breath (evaṃ anassāsikā)" means thus, like water drunk in a dream, and like candana sandalwood smeared on, devoid of breath.

Thus, unstable. Thus, without breath.

"The body one should discard (sarīraṃ nikkhipeyya)" means the body one should abandon (sarīraṃ chaḍḍeyya).

Now, there is no discarding or attending to another body for the Tathāgata, ānanda, because of the abandonment of defilements, thus he speaks.

Having said this, he again addressed the elder: "The power of a wheel-turning monarch disappears on the seventh day after he renounces the world."

Moreover, on the seventh day after the death of Mahāsudassana, the seven jeweled walls, the seven jeweled palisades, the eighty-four thousand lotus ponds, the Dharma palace, the Dharma lotus pond, the jewel wheel—all this disappeared.

Moreover, among elephants and others, this is the nature: when their life is exhausted, they die together.

When life is remaining, the elephant jewel goes to the Uposatha clan, the horse jewel to the Valāhaka clan, the jewel gem goes to Mount Vipulla itself.

The power of the woman jewel disappears.

The eye of the householder jewel is just natural.

The skill of the advisor jewel is destroyed.

The body one should discard.

"Thus spoke the Blessed One (idamavoca bhagavā)" means the Blessed One spoke all this, both that which is established in the Pāli and that which is not established.

The rest is just of obvious meaning.

Thus spoke the Blessed One.

Thus ends the Mahāsudassana Sutta Commentary in the Sumaṅgalavilāsinī, Commentary to the Dīgha Nikāya.
