460. Evaṃ me sutaṃ – ekaṃ samayaṃ bhagavā rājagahe viharati veḷuvane kalandakanivāpe. Atha kho visākho upāsako yena dhammadinnā bhikkhunī tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā dhammadinnaṃ bhikkhuniṃ abhivādetvā ekamantaṃ nisīdi. Ekamantaṃ nisinno kho visākho upāsako dhammadinnaṃ bhikkhuniṃ etadavoca – ‘‘‘sakkāyo sakkāyo’ti, ayye, vuccati. Katamo nu kho, ayye, sakkāyo vutto bhagavatā’’ti? ‘‘Pañca kho ime, āvuso visākha, upādānakkhandhā sakkāyo vutto bhagavatā, seyyathidaṃ – rūpupādānakkhandho, vedanupādānakkhandho, saññupādānakkhandho, saṅkhārupādānakkhandho, viññāṇupādānakkhandho. Ime kho, āvuso visākha, pañcupādānakkhandhā sakkāyo vutto bhagavatā’’ti. ‘‘Sādhayye’’ti kho visākho upāsako dhammadinnāya bhikkhuniyā bhāsitaṃ abhinanditvā anumoditvā dhammadinnaṃ bhikkhuniṃ uttariṃ pañhaṃ apucchi – ‘‘‘sakkāyasamudayo sakkāyasamudayo’ti, ayye, vuccati. Katamo nu kho, ayye, sakkāyasamudayo vutto bhagavatā’’ti? ‘‘Yāyaṃ, āvuso visākha, taṇhā ponobbhavikā nandīrāgasahagatā tatratatrābhinandinī, seyyathidaṃ – kāmataṇhā bhavataṇhā vibhavataṇhā; ayaṃ kho, āvuso visākha, sakkāyasamudayo vutto bhagavatā’’ti. ‘‘‘Sakkāyanirodho sakkāyanirodho’ti, ayye, vuccati. Katamo nu kho, ayye, sakkāyanirodho vutto bhagavatā’’ti? ‘‘Yo kho, āvuso visākha, tassāyeva taṇhāya asesavirāganirodho cāgo paṭinissaggo mutti anālayo; ayaṃ kho, āvuso visākha, sakkāyanirodho vutto bhagavatā’’ti. ‘‘‘Sakkāyanirodhagāminī paṭipadā sakkāyanirodhagāminī paṭipadā’ti, ayye, vuccati. Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was living at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the Squirrels’ Sanctuary. Then the lay follower Visākha went to the bhikkhuni Dhammadinnā, and after paying homage to her, he sat down at one side. Seated at one side, the lay follower Visākha said to the bhikkhuni Dhammadinnā: “'Personality, personality' is said, lady. What now, lady, is the personality ( sakkāya) said by the Blessed One?” “Friend Visākha, these five aggregates affected by clinging (upādānakkhandha) are called personality by the Blessed One; that is, the material form aggregate affected by clinging (rūpupādānakkhandha), the feeling aggregate affected by clinging (vedanupādānakkhandha), the perception aggregate affected by clinging (saññupādānakkhandha), the formations aggregate affected by clinging (saṅkhārupādānakkhandha), and the consciousness aggregate affected by clinging (viññāṇupādānakkhandha). These five aggregates affected by clinging are called personality by the Blessed One.”

Saying, “Good, lady,” the lay follower Visākha delighted and rejoiced in the bhikkhuni Dhammadinnā’s words. Then he asked her a further question: “'Origin of personality, origin of personality' is said, lady. What now, lady, is the origin of personality (sakkāyasamudaya) said by the Blessed One?” “Friend Visākha, it is craving (taṇhā), which brings renewal of being (ponobbhavikā), is accompanied by delight and lust (nandīrāgasahagatā), and delights in this and that; that is, craving for sensual pleasures (kāmataṇhā), craving for being (bhavataṇhā), and craving for non-being (vibhavataṇhā). This is called the origin of personality by the Blessed One.”

“'Cessation of personality, cessation of personality' is said, lady. What now, lady, is the cessation of personality (sakkāyanirodha) said by the Blessed One?”

“Friend Visākha, it is the remainderless fading away and ceasing, the giving up, relinquishing, letting go, and rejecting of that same craving. This is called the cessation of personality by the Blessed One.”

“'The practice leading to the cessation of personality, the practice leading to the cessation of personality' is said, lady. What now, lady, is the practice leading to the cessation of personality (sakkāyanirodhagāminī paṭipadā) said by the Blessed One?”

“Friend Visākha, it is simply this Noble Eightfold Path (ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo) that is the practice leading to the cessation of personality, that is, right view (sammādiṭṭhi), right thought (sammāsaṅkappo); right speech (sammāvācā), right action (sammākammanto), right livelihood (sammāājīvo); right effort (sammāvāyāmo), right mindfulness (sammāsati), right concentration (sammāsamādhi).”

“Venerable lady, is that clinging (upādāna) the same as these five aggregates affected by clinging (pañcupādānakkhandha), or is the clinging something apart from the five aggregates affected by clinging?” “Friend Visākha, that clinging is neither the same as these five aggregates affected by clinging nor is clinging something apart from the five aggregates affected by clinging. It is the desire and lust (chandarāgo) in regard to the five aggregates affected by clinging that is the clinging there.”

460. Thus I have heard: the Cūḷavedallasutta.

There, Visākha the lay follower means Visākha, a lay follower by that name.

"By whom Dhammadinnā" means the nun named Dhammadinnā approached him.

Who is this Visākha? Who is Dhammadinnā? Why did she approach him? Visākha was the householder, the master of the house, at the time Dhammadinnā was a laywoman.

When the Blessed One, having fully awakened to the Perfect Enlightenment (sammāsambodhi), set in motion the supreme wheel of the Dharma, having trained Yasādayo and other sons of good families, having reached Uruvelā, and having trained a thousand ascetics with matted hair there, together with the ancient ascetics, and with the monks who had destroyed the āsavas, having gone to Rājagaha for the purpose of seeing the Buddha, he taught the Dharma to King Bimbisāra, who had come with a retinue of twelve nahutas.

Then, among the twelve nahutas who had come with the king, one nahuta took refuge in the status of lay follower (upāsaka), and eleven nahutas were established in the fruit of stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala) along with King Bimbisāra.

This lay follower was one of them, and having been established in the fruit of stream-entry at the very first sight together with them, then, having heard the Dharma one day, having attained the fruit of once-returning (sakadāgāmi-phala), and thereafter, having heard the Dharma one day, he was established in the fruit of non-returning (anāgāmi-phala).

Having become a non-returner (anāgāmī), when he was coming to his house, as he used to look here and there on other days, smiling, thus he came, but not coming in that way, with calmed senses, with a calmed mind, he went [home].

Dhammadinnā, having opened the lion-window and looking at the street, seeing his manner of coming, thinking, "What is this?", going to meet him, standing at the top of the stairs, extended her hand for support.

The lay follower waved his hand.

She thought, "I will know at the time of the morning meal."

Formerly the lay follower used to eat together with her.

But on that day, without looking at her, he ate alone like a monk practicing yoga.

She thought, "I will know at the time of the evening meal."

On that day the lay follower did not enter the auspicious chamber, but having had another chamber prepared and having had a suitable couch arranged, he lay down.

The laywoman, greatly saddened, thought, "What desire does he have outside, or has he been broken by some deceiver, or is there some fault of mine?", and

having become [so], "It is possible to know in one or two days," having gone to his presence, having paid homage, she stood there.

The lay follower asked, "Why, Dhammadinnā, have you come at an improper time?"

"Yes, lord, I have come, you are not as before, what desire do you have outside?" "There is none, Dhammadinnā."

"Is there another deceiver?" "There is none of that either."

"That being so, is there some fault of mine?"

"There is no fault of yours either."

"Then why do you not speak and converse with me as usual?" He thought, "This supramundane Dharma is heavy and weighty and should not be revealed. If I do not tell her, my heart, breaking, will meet its end right here." For the sake of helping her, he said, "Dhammadinnā, having heard the Dharma teaching of the Teacher, I have attained a supramundane Dharma, and such worldly activities are not fitting for one who has attained that.

If you wish, there are forty koṭis for you, and forty koṭis for me, so there is eighty koṭis of wealth; be the mistress here, and dwell in my place as a mother, or in the place of a sister.

I will maintain myself with just a morsel of food given by you.

Or if you do not do this, take these riches and go to your family home, or if you have no desire outside, I will keep you in the place of a sister or in the place of a daughter and support you."

She thought, "An ordinary man does not speak thus.

Surely he has penetrated the excellent supramundane Dharma.

But is that Dharma to be awakened to only by men, or is a woman also able to penetrate it?" She said this to Visākha - "Is this Dharma to be attained only by men, or is it possible for a woman to attain it?" "Dhammadinnā, what are you saying? Those who are committed, they are the heirs of this. Whoever has the necessary support, that one attains it."

That being so, permit my going forth.

"Good, noble lady, I too wish to join you in this very path, but not knowing your mind, I do not tell you." Just then, having gone to the presence of King Bimbisāra, having paid homage, he stood there.
“[She] replied, ‘The king [did].’”
The King asked: “Why have you come at this time, householder?”
Dhammadinnā said, “Great king, I will go forth.”
Why is it fitting for him to obtain? There is nothing else, [but] it is fitting to obtain a golden palanquin, O god, and to have the city cleaned.
The king, having given the golden palanquin, had the city cleaned.
Visākha, having bathed Dhammadinnā with scented water, having adorned her with all ornaments, having seated her on a golden palanquin, having had her surrounded by a group of relatives, honoring her with scents, flowers, etc., as if making a city procession, went to the nuns' residence and said, “Cause Dhammadinnā to go forth, ladies.”
The nuns said: “Householder, it is suitable to bear with one or two faults.”
“There is no fault, ladies; she goes forth out of faith.”

“But the Noble Eightfold Path, friend Visākha, is comprised within the three constituents”: Here, since the path has a basis and the three constituents are without a basis, therefore, this path, because it has a basis, like a city by a kingdom, is comprised within the three constituents that are without a basis.

There, the three beginning with right speech are virtue itself, therefore they are included in the virtue aggregate as of the same kind.

Although in the Pali, the designation “virtue aggregate” is made in a general way, like on the ground, the meaning should be understood in terms of function.

Among the three beginning with right effort, concentration cannot, of its own nature, cause absorption through unification on the object. But with energy accomplishing its function of exerting and mindfulness accomplishing its function of preventing wobbling, it can do so, having received help.

Here is an analogy: Just as among three friends who have entered a park saying, “We will play at the festival,” one might see a champak tree in full blossom but be unable to take it even by raising his hand.

Then the second would bend down and give his back [to climb on]. But even standing on his back, he would be unable to pick [the flowers] because of his unsteadiness.

Then the other would offer his shoulder. Standing on the back of one and supporting himself on the shoulder of the other, he would pick as many flowers as he wanted, adorn himself, and play at the festival.

This excellence should be seen thus. For the three states beginning with right effort, which are born together, are like the three friends who enter the park together.

The object is like the champak tree in full blossom.

Concentration, being unable to cause absorption through unification on the object of its own nature, is like the one who is unable to take [the flowers] even by raising his hand.

Effort is like the companion who bent down, giving his back to mount upon.

Mindfulness is like the friend who stood by, giving his shoulder for support.

Just as standing on the back of one and supporting himself on the shoulder of the other, he could pick as many flowers as he wanted, so too, when energy accomplishes its function of exerting and when mindfulness accomplishes its function of preventing wobbling, with the help so obtained concentration can bring about absorption by unification on the object.

So here in the concentration aggregate it is only concentration that is included as of the same kind. But effort and mindfulness are included because of their action in assisting.

Even in the case of right view and right thought, wisdom cannot of its own nature decide that the object is impermanent, suffering, and not-self, but applied thought can do so by repeatedly striking and giving [attention].

How? Just as a money changer, having a coin placed in his hand and being desirous of looking at it on all sides equally, cannot turn it over with the power of his eye only, but can turn it over with the segments of his fingers and look at it from here and there,

Even so, wisdom cannot of its own nature decide on an object in terms of impermanence, etc., but assisted by applied thought with its characteristic of directing the mind on to the object and its function of striking and threshing, as it were, hitting and turning over, it can take anything given and define it.

So here in the understanding aggregate it is only right view that is included as of the same kind. But right thinking is included because of its action in assisting.

So the path is included by the three aggregates.

465. Now, questioning, "What sensations cease upon attaining cessation (nirodha-samāpatti)?", he asked, "How many sensations are there, revered one?"

In "[Is it] physical?" etc., physical pleasure through the five sense doors, physical is known as such, mental pleasure through the mind door is known as mental.

There, "pleasure" indicates its inherent nature.

"Sāta" is a synonym that illuminates its delightful quality.

"Experiencing" (vedayita) illuminates the nature of experiencing; it is a general term for all sensations.

The same method applies to the remaining clauses.

In "[Pleasure is] pleasant when it lasts, painful when it changes," etc., the existence of pleasant sensation is pleasure, its non-existence is pain.

The existence of painful sensation is pain, its non-existence is pleasure.

The awareness of neither-painful-nor-pleasant sensation is pleasure, the non-awareness is pain—this is the meaning.

What underlying tendency underlies? Which underlying tendency underlies?

He asks the question about underlying tendencies, [saying that it] is like being asleep due to non-abandonment.

"No, friend Visākha, the underlying tendency to lust does not underlie all pleasant sensation" means that the underlying tendency to lust does not underlie all pleasant sensation.

That underlying tendency is not abandoned in all pleasant sensation; therefore, not all pleasant sensation arises from it—this is the meaning.

This method applies everywhere.

"What should be abandoned?" This is called the question of abandonment.

What underlying tendency underlies? No, friend Visākha, the underlying tendency to lust does not underlie all pleasant sensation. What should be abandoned?

"Lust is abandoned by that" means that with one expression, he answered two questions.

Here, a bhikṣu, having suppressed the underlying tendency to lust, attains the first jhāna; having made the jhāna-suppressed underlying tendency to lust suppressed in that way, develops vipaśyanā and eradicates it with the path of the non-returner (anāgāmin).

Having abandoned it with the path of the anāgāmin, he does not, in the first jhāna, experience that [lust] at all, due to its suppression in that way.

Therefore, he said, "There, the underlying tendency to lust does not underlie."

"That base" (tadāyatana) means that base, arahatship, is the foundation due to its nature of supreme consolation—this is the meaning.

"Thus, in the unsurpassed" (iti anuttaresū) means that in arahatship, which has thus attained the name "unsurpassed vimokṣa [liberation]."

"For one who establishes longing" (pihaṃ upaṭṭhāpayato) means for one who establishes aspiration (patthana).

"Displeasure arises because of longing" (uppajjati pihāpaccayā domanassa) means displeasure arises rooted in the establishing of aspiration.

But this does not arise rooted in the establishing of aspiration; rather, it arises rooted in loss (alābha) for one who, having longed, does not obtain; thus, it is said, "It arises because of longing."

There, although displeasure (domanassa) is definitely unwholesome (akusala), they say that this displeasure is suitable for cultivation.

Yogis take up a practice lasting three months, six months, or nine months.

Among them, whoever, having taken up that particular practice, strives and endeavors, thinking, "I will attain arahatship within this limited time," but is not able to attain it within the time that was determined, strong displeasure arises in him; tears flow like [streams of water] for the Elder Mahāphussadeva, who lived in Āḷindika.

It is said that the Elder completed the practice of going and returning for nineteen years.

For him, thinking, "In this turn, I will attain arahatship; in this turn, I will carry out the visuddhi-pavāraṇa [purification ceremony]", and thus binding his mind, nineteen years passed while he was practicing the śrāmaṇadharma [ascetic practices].

When the day of pavāraṇa [the end of the rainy season retreat] arrived, it was not a day of joy for the Elder due to the falling of his tears.

But in the twentieth year, he attained arahatship.

Lust is abandoned by that. That base. Thus, in the unsurpassed. For one who establishes longing. Displeasure arises because of longing.
"Having abandoned aversion by that" means that one abandons aversion through displeasure itself.

For aversion is not abandoned by aversion, nor is displeasure abandoned by displeasure.

But here a bhikṣu, having undertaken one of the practices beginning with a three-month one, reflects thus: "Look, bhikṣu, what deficiency do you have in morality, or in effort, or in wisdom? Surely your morality is well-purified, your effort is well-aroused, and wisdom proceeds being heroic."

Having reflected thus, making his effort firm, thinking, "I will not again allow this displeasure to arise," within three months, or within six months, or within nine months, by the path of non-returning, he uproots it.

In this way, he abandons aversion by aversion, and he abandons displeasure by displeasure.

"Having abandoned aversion by that, the underlying tendency of aversion does not underlie there" means that in such displeasure, the underlying tendency of aversion does not underlie.

It does not arise concerning that; the meaning is that the underlying tendency of aversion is abandoned there.

"Having abandoned ignorance by that" means that here a bhikṣu having suppressed the underlying tendency of ignorance, attains the fourth jhāna. Having suppressed the underlying tendency of ignorance with jhāna, just having suppressed it thus, developing insight, by the path of arhatship he uproots it.

Even though he has abandoned it by the path of arhatship, because it is suppressed, in the fourth jhāna it does not underlie.

Therefore, he said, "The underlying tendency of ignorance does not underlie there."

Now, while asking the question of correspondence, he said, beginning with "sukhāya panāyye".

In answering that, since suffering is the opposite of pleasure, and pleasure is the opposite of suffering, therefore, in the case of the two feelings, the correspondence of dissimilarity has been spoken of.

But equanimity is darkness, unmanifest, difficult to illuminate; ignorance too is like that. Therefore, here the correspondence of similarity has been spoken of.

But in however many instances ignorance causes darkness, in that many instances knowledge dispels the darkness; thus, the correspondence of dissimilarity has been spoken of.

"avijjāya kho, āvuso": Here, both these things are taintless, supramundane; thus, only the correspondence of similarity has been spoken of.

"vimuttiyā kho, āvuso": Here, by way of being taintless, by way of being supramundane, and by way of being uncharacterized, only the correspondence of similarity has been spoken of.

The meaning is that you are asking this with this intention.

467. Paṇḍitā means endowed with wisdom, skilled in the elements (dhātu), skilled in the sense fields (āyatana), skilled in dependent origination (paṭiccasamuppāda), skilled in what is possible and impossible.

Mahāpaññā means endowed with wisdom capable of grasping great meanings, great teachings (dharma), great etymologies (nirutti), and great eloquence (paṭibhāna).

Yathā taṃ dhammadinnāyā means: just as it was explained by the bhikkhuni Dhammadinnā, so too would I explain it in exactly the same way.

To this extent, this discourse (suttanta) is called spoken by the Victor (jinabhāsito), not spoken by a disciple (sāvakabhāsito).

Just as a letter written by royal officials does not count as a royal letter until it is stamped with the royal seal, but as soon as it is stamped it is called a royal letter, so too, because this discourse is stamped with the seal of the Buddha's word, "I would explain it in exactly the same way," it is called spoken by the Victor by way of borrowed speech.
