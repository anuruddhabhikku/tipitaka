298. Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was living at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the Squirrels’ Sanctuary.

At that time the venerable Samiddhi was staying in a forest hut.

Then the wanderer Potaliputta, while wandering and walking for exercise, went to the venerable Samiddhi and exchanged greetings with him.

Having concluded their greetings and cordial talk, he sat down to one side.

Seated to one side, the wanderer Potaliputta said to the venerable Samiddhi: "Face to face with the samaṇa Gotama, friend Samiddhi, I have heard and learned, face to face it was accepted: 'Vain is bodily action, vain is verbal action, only mental action is real.'"

"But, friend, is there an attainment on entering which one does not feel anything at all?"

"No, friend Potaliputta, do not speak thus; do not misrepresent the Blessed One.

It is not right to misrepresent the Blessed One.

The Blessed One would not say: ‘Vain is bodily action, vain is verbal action, only mental action is real.’

But there is, friend, an attainment on entering which one does not feel anything at all."

"How long is it, friend Samiddhi, since you went forth?" "Not long, friend! Three years."

"What shall we say now to the elder bhikkhus, when a bhikkhu so new thinks the Teacher must be defended?

Having done an intentional action by way of body, speech, or mind, friend Samiddhi, what does one feel?" "Having done an intentional action by way of body, speech, or mind, friend Potaliputta, one feels suffering ( duḥkha)."

Then, neither approving nor disapproving of the venerable Samiddhi’s words, the wanderer Potaliputta rose from his seat and departed.

There, mogha means empty, fruitless.

sacca means truly, factually.

Moreover, this was not heard face to face by this person, [but is found] in the Upāli Discourse (Majjhima Nikāya 2.56): "I proclaim mental action (manokamma) to be more blameworthy than the performance or the arising of evil action; not so is bodily action (kāyakamma), not so is verbal action (vacīkamma)," so the Blessed One said. That talk became well-known among the sectarians, and taking it, this one speaks.

atthi ca sā samāpattī: This is with reference to the question, "How, indeed, does the cessation of perception (abhisaññānirodha) occur?" in the Poṭṭhapāda Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 1.406 ff.), [which] speaks with reference to the arising of the discussion on the cessation of perception.

300. Then the Blessed One addressed the venerable Ānanda: “See, Ānanda, how this misguided man Udāyin interferes. I knew, Ānanda, that this misguided man Udāyin would unduly interfere right now.”

From the start the wanderer Potaliputta had asked Ānanda about the three kinds of feeling.

This misguided man Samiddhi would have answered the wanderer Potaliputta rightly if, when asked thus, he would have explained: ‘Friend Potaliputta, having done an intentional action by way of body, speech, or mind whose result is to be felt as pleasant, one feels pleasure. Having done an intentional action by way of body, speech, or mind whose result is to be felt as painful, one feels pain. Having done an intentional action by way of body, speech, or mind whose result is to be felt as neither painful nor pleasant, one feels what is neither painful nor pleasant.’

And again, Ānanda, who are these foolish, thoughtless wanderers of other sects, that they could understand the Tathāgata’s great exposition of action? If you, Ānanda, were to listen to the Tathāgata expounding the great exposition of action!”

300. Ummaṅga means "question-ummaṅga."
Ummujjamāno means "raising the head."
Ayoniso ummujjissatī means "he will raise his head unwisely," that is, "he will raise his head without a means."
Moreover, the Blessed One, knowing this, understood not by means of the divine eye, nor by means of knowing the minds of others, nor by means of all-knowing knowledge, but merely by intention.
Indeed, the intention of one who speaks is easily understood: One who desires to speak raises his neck, moves his jaw, his face throbs, he is unable to sit down.
The Blessed One, seeing that appearance of him, recognized, "This Udāyī is unable to sit down; whatever is unreal, that he will say," having looked [at him].
Ādiṃ yevā means ādimhiyeva.
Tisso vedanā means "What does he experience?" To one who asks, "I ask about three sensations," thus, having determined, three sensations are asked about.
Sukhavedaniya means "having pleasure as its cause."
The same rule applies to the rest.

Moreover, here, the four volitions associated with pleasure-tinged consciousness, wholesome in the desire realm, and the volitions of the three lower jhānas, thus, because of generating pleasant sensation in rebirth and in continued existence, it is called sukhavedaniyaṃ kammaṃ, "karma ripening as pleasure."

"Formerly, that was done by him," means that which was seen by this divine-eyed one doing the action; before that, it was done.

Having been done formerly, he is reborn in hell; having been done afterward, he is reborn; at the time of death, or even by wrong views such as, "Khandha is the best, Śiva is the best, the grandfather is the best, the world is scattered by Īśvara," he is reborn.

"In the seen Dharma" means that which is felt as immediately ripening, for him, it is in the seen Dharma; that which is to be experienced in rebirth, for him, having been reborn; that which is to be experienced in a subsequent life, for him, in a subsequent cycle, he experiences the result.

Formerly, that was done by him; in the seen Dharma. Thus, this renunciate or brāhmaṇa saw one mass of karma and one mass of result; the perfectly awakened one saw with this unseen [faculty] three masses of karma and two masses of result.

With this seen [faculty], and with the unseen, he saw four masses of karma and three masses of result.

These seven bases are called the knowledge of analysis of great karma of the Tathāgata.

In the second instance, nothing was seen by the divine-eyed one, but the Tathāgata saw three masses of karma and two masses of result.

These five individual bases are called the knowledge of analysis of great karma of the Tathāgata.

The same method applies in the remaining two instances.

"Incapable" means devoid of what has come to be, unwholesome.

"Seeming incapable" means it seems incapable; it overcomes, it rejects, this is the meaning.

Indeed, in much unwholesome action that has been accumulated, strong action, having repelled the result of weak action, creates an opportunity for its own result; this is both incapable and seeming incapable.

But having accumulated wholesome action, and near [death] unwholesome action has been done, that, having repelled the result of the wholesome, creates an opportunity for its own result; this is incapable, seeming capable.

Even in much wholesome action having been accumulated, strong action, having repelled the result of weak action, creates an opportunity for its own result; this is both capable and seeming capable.

But having accumulated unwholesome action, and near [death] wholesome action has been done, that, having repelled the result of the unwholesome, creates an opportunity for its own result; this is capable, seeming incapable.

Incapable, seeming incapable. Moreover, the meaning here should be understood in terms of the mode of presentation.

Indeed, it is said, "Because it seems incapable, it presents as incapable;" [this is] seeming incapable.

There, in the manner of "That person here is a killer of living beings," four persons are spoken of; among them, the action of the first is incapable and seeming incapable; because it is unwholesome, it is incapable, and because of his rebirth in hell, there, having become the cause of rebirth, the unwholesome arises.

The action of the second is incapable, seeming capable; because it is unwholesome, it is incapable.

But because of his rebirth in heaven, the wholesome, having become the cause of rebirth in heaven for those of other sects, arises.

The same method applies in the other two kinds of action.
