[124.] Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One (Bhagavā) was dwelling at Anāthapindika’s monastery, in the Jeta Grove, near Sāvatthi.

There the Fortunate One addressed the monks (bhikkhus), saying: "Monks (bhikkhavo)!"

"Venerable sir (bhadante)," those monks replied to the Blessed One.

The Blessed One said this:

“Bhikkhus, whatever fears (bhayāni) arise, all arise because of the fool (bāla), not because of the wise man (paṇḍita); whatever troubles (upaddavā) arise, all arise because of the fool, not because of the wise man; whatever calamities (upasaggā) arise, all arise because of the fool, not because of the wise man.

Just as a fire that starts in a shed made of rushes (naḷāgārā) or grass (tiṇāgārā) burns down even a house (kūṭāgārāni) with a peaked roof, with walls plastered (ullittāvalittāni) inside and outside, shut off (nivātāni), secured by bars (phusitaggaḷāni), with shuttered windows (pihitavātapānāni); so too, bhikkhus, whatever fears (bhayāni) arise, all arise because of the fool, not because of the wise man; whatever troubles (upaddavā) arise, all arise because of the fool, not because of the wise man; whatever calamities (upasaggā) arise, all arise because of the fool, not because of the wise man.

Thus, bhikkhus, the fool (bālo) brings fear (sappaṭibhayo), the wise man (paṇḍito) brings no fear (appaṭibhayo); the fool brings trouble (saupaddavo), the wise man brings no trouble (anupaddavo); the fool brings calamity (saupasaggo), the wise man brings no calamity (anupasaggo).

No fear (bhayaṃ) comes from the wise man (paṇḍitato), no trouble (upaddavo) comes from the wise man, no calamity (upasaggo) comes from the wise man.

Therefore, bhikkhus, you should train thus: ‘We shall be wise men (paṇḍitā), we shall be inquirers (vīmaṃsakā).’”

Having said this, the venerable Ānanda said to the Blessed One: "To what extent, venerable sir (bhante), is a wise bhikkhu (paṇḍito bhikkhu) 'an inquirer' (vīmaṃsako) fit to be called?"

"Ānanda, to the extent that a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements (dhātukusalo), skilled in the sense bases (āyatanakusalo), skilled in dependent origination (paṭiccasamuppādakusalo), skilled in what is possible and impossible (ṭhānāṭhānakusalo) – to this extent, Ānanda, a wise bhikkhu is ‘an inquirer’ fit to be called."

124. Thus I have heard: the bahudhātukasutta.

There, bhayānī and so forth, bhaya means mental trembling (cittutrāsa).

Upaddavo means a state of non-singleness of mind (anekaggatākāro).

Upasaggo means a state of being attached (upasaṭṭhākāro), a state of clinging there and there (tattha tattha lagganākāro).

Thus, their diversity should be understood: robbers relying on uneven places such as mountains, send [messengers] to the inhabitants of the country, "We, on such and such a day, will strike your village."

From that time of the communication, they encounter fear and dread, starting from when they heard it (sutakālato).

This is called mental trembling (cittutrāso nāma).

"Here, the angry robbers may bring misfortune," taking the essence of their possessions, entering the forest together with two-footed and four-footed [beings], they lie down there and there on the ground, being eaten by horseflies, mosquitoes, and so on, they enter the interiors of thickets, they trample on stumps and thorns.

Thus, for those wandering in this way, the scattered state is called a state of non-singleness of mind (anekaggatākāro nāma).

Then, when the robbers do not come on the day specified, "That teaching of the empty ones will come to pass, we will enter the village," with all their equipment, they enter the village, then, knowing that they have entered, surrounding the village, setting fire to the door, killing the people, the robbers go away plundering all the wealth.

Those remaining after the killing, extinguishing the fire, clinging to the shadows of storehouses, the shadows of walls, etc., sit there and there lamenting what is lost.

This is called a state of being attached (upasaṭṭhākāro lagganākāro nāma).

Naḷāgārā means houses covered with reeds (naḷehi paricchannā agārā), the remaining equipment here is made of trees (rukkhamayā honti).

The same rule applies to a grass house (tiṇāgārepi eseva nayo).

Bālato uppajjantī means arising relying only on the fool (bālameva nissāya uppajjanti).

For a fool (bālo hi), an unlearned person (apaṇḍitapuriso), desiring a kingdom (rajjaṃ vā) or a sub-kingdom (uparajjaṃ vā) or anything else (aññaṃ vā) that is great (mahantaṃ ṭhānaṃ), taking a few widow's sons (vidhavāputte) like himself (attanā sadise), great rogues (mahādhutte), "Come, I will make you lords," relying on mountains, dense forests, etc., striking villages at the very borders, making known the state of banditry (dāmarikabhāvaṃ jānāpetvā) gradually strikes at market towns and also at districts, people abandoning their homes, desiring a safe place, depart, monks and nuns dwelling in reliance on them, abandoning their own dwelling places, depart.

In places gone and gone, almsfood and lodging are difficult to obtain (dullabhaṃ hoti).

Thus, fear has indeed come to the four assemblies (evaṃ catunnaṃ parisānaṃ bhayaṃ āgatameva hoti).

Even among those gone forth, two foolish monks having started a dispute with each other, begin accusation.

Thus, like the inhabitants of Kosambī, a great quarrel arises, fear has indeed come to the four assemblies, thus whatever fears arise, all those should be known as arising from the fool (sabbāni tāni bālato uppajjantīti veditabbāni).

Naḷāgārā, bālato uppajjantī.

Etadavocā means the teaching of the Dharma (dhammadesanā) was completed by the Blessed One (bhagavatā) without reaching the summit (matthakaṃ apāpetvāva niṭṭhāpitā).

“But to what extent, venerable sir, is one justified in saying ‘a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements’?”

“There are these eighteen elements (aṭṭhārasa dhātuyo), Ānanda: the eye element (cakkhudhātu), the form element (rūpadhātu), the eye-consciousness element (cakkhuviññāṇadhātu); the ear element (sotadhātu), the sound element (saddadhātu), the ear-consciousness element (sotaviññāṇadhātu); the nose element (ghānadhātu), the odour element (gandhadhātu), the nose-consciousness element (ghānaviññāṇadhātu); the tongue element (jivhādhātu), the flavour element (rasadhātu), the tongue-consciousness element (jivhāviññāṇadhātu); the body element (kāyadhātu), the tangible element (phoṭṭhabbadhātu), the body-consciousness element (kāyaviññāṇadhātu); the mind element (manodhātu), the mind-object element (dhammadhātu), the mind-consciousness element (manoviññāṇadhātu). When he knows and sees these eighteen elements, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the elements.”

“But, venerable sir, could there be any other way in which one could be justified in saying ‘a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements’?”

“There could, Ānanda. There are these six elements (cha dhātuyo): the earth element (pathavīdhātu), the water element (āpodhātu), the fire element (tejodhātu), the air element (vāyodhātu), the space element (ākāsadhātu), and the consciousness element (viññāṇadhātu). When he knows and sees these six elements, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the elements.”

“But, venerable sir, could there be any other way in which one could be justified in saying ‘a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements’?”

“There could, Ānanda. There are these six elements (cha dhātuyo): the pleasure element (sukhadhātu), the pain element (dukkhadhātu), the joy element (somanassadhātu), the grief element (domanassadhātu), the equanimity element (upekkhādhātu), and the ignorance element (avijjādhātu). When he knows and sees these six elements, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the elements.”

“But, venerable sir, could there be any other way in which one could be justified in saying ‘a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements’?”

“There could, Ānanda. There are these six elements (cha dhātuyo): the desire element (kāmadhātu), the renunciation element (nekkhammadhātu), the ill-will element (byāpādadhātu), the non-ill-will element (abyāpādadhātu), the harmfulness element (vihiṃsādhātu), [and] the harmlessness element (avihiṃsādhātu). When he knows and sees these six elements, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the elements.”

“But, venerable sir, could there be any other way in which one could be justified in saying ‘a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements’?”

“There could, Ānanda. There are these three elements (tisso dhātuyo): the desire realm (kāmadhātu), the form realm (rūpadhātu), and the formless realm (arūpadhātu). When he knows and sees these three elements, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the elements.”

“But, venerable sir, could there be any other way in which one could be justified in saying ‘a bhikkhu is skilled in the elements’?”

“There could, Ānanda. There are these two elements (dve dhātuyo): the conditioned element (saṅkhatādhātu) and the unconditioned element (asaṅkhatādhātu). When he knows and sees these two elements, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the elements.”

126. “In what way, venerable sir, is a bhikkhu skilled in the sense bases?”

“Ānanda, there are these six internal and external sense bases: the eye and forms (rūpa), the ear and sounds (śabda), the nose and odours (gandha), the tongue and flavours (rasa), the body and tangibles (sparśa), the mind and mind-objects (dharma). Ānanda, when a bhikkhu knows and sees these six internal and external sense bases, in that way, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in the sense bases.”

“In what way, venerable sir, is a bhikkhu skilled in dependent origination ( pratītyasamutpāda)?”

“Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu understands thus: ‘When this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases.’ That is, with ignorance ( avidyā) as condition, volitional formations (saṃskāra) come to be; with volitional formations as condition, consciousness (vijñāna); with consciousness as condition, name and form (nāmarūpa); with name and form as condition, the six sense bases (ṣaḍāyatana); with the six sense bases as condition, contact (sparśa); with contact as condition, feeling (vedanā); with feeling as condition, craving (tṛṣṇā); with craving as condition, clinging (upādāna); with clinging as condition, becoming (bhava); with becoming as condition, birth (jāti); with birth as condition, aging and death (jarāmaraṇa), sorrow (śoka), lamentation (parideva), pain (duḥkha), grief (daurmanasya), and despair (upāyāsa) come to be. Thus is the arising of this entire mass of suffering (duḥkhakkhandha).

But with the remainderless fading away and cessation of that very ignorance (avidyā) comes the cessation of volitional formations (saṃskāra); with the cessation of volitional formations, the cessation of consciousness (vijñāna); with the cessation of consciousness, the cessation of name and form (nāmarūpa); with the cessation of name and form, the cessation of the six sense bases (ṣaḍāyatana); with the cessation of the six sense bases, the cessation of contact (sparśa); with the cessation of contact, the cessation of feeling (vedanā); with the cessation of feeling, the cessation of craving (tṛṣṇā); with the cessation of craving, the cessation of clinging (upādāna); with the cessation of clinging, the cessation of becoming (bhava); with the cessation of becoming, the cessation of birth (jāti); with the cessation of birth, aging and death (jarāmaraṇa), sorrow (śoka), lamentation (parideva), pain (duḥkha), grief (daurmanasya), and despair (upāyāsa) cease. Thus is the cessation of this entire mass of suffering (duḥkhakkhandha)’. In this way, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda)."

Furthermore, would an ariyasāvaka deprive another of life? This is also impossible.

This was said in order to show the great blameworthiness of the state of a puthujjana (worldling), and to reveal the power of an ariyasāvaka.

The intention here is this: the state of a puthujjana is blameworthy, for it is there that a puthujjana will commit even the ānantariya deeds (deeds with immediate result), such as matricide etc.

Saṅghaṃ bhindeyyā means "he might cause a schism in the Saṅgha," one dwelling in communion, established within a common boundary, might cause a schism in the Saṅgha in five ways.

It was said, "In five ways, Upāli, is the Saṅgha divided: through a legal procedure (kammena), through recitation (uddesena), through speaking (voharanto), through a proclamation (anussāvanena), and through voting (salākaggāhenā)" (Pari. 458).

Duṭṭhacitto, lohitaṃ uppādeyyā, saṅghaṃ bhindeyyā

There, kammenā means by one or another of the four legal procedures, beginning with apalokana.

Uddesenā means by one or another of the five recitations of the Pātimokkha.

Voharanto means speaking, revealing the eighteen grounds for schism, such as "what is not the Dharma is the Dharma," and so on, by means of various utterances.

Anussāvanenā means, "Surely you know that I am of high birth, that I have gone forth, and that I have much learning; is it fitting for you to produce even the thought that one like me would accept what is not the Dharma, what is contrary to the Discipline, and the Teacher’s Dispensation? Is Avīci hell as cool for me as a grove of blue lotuses? Am I not afraid of the lower realms?", and by making a division in the words in this way, near the ear, by means of a proclamation.

Salākaggāhenā means, having proclaimed thus, having encouraged their minds, and having made [them] of unwavering Dharma, "Take this voting ticket," by means of taking a voting ticket.

Kammenā, uddesenā, voharanto, anussāvanenā, salākaggāhenā

Here, kamma alone, or uddesa, is the measure, but the speaking, the proclamation, and the taking of voting tickets are preliminary.

For by speaking, in dependence upon revealing the eighteen grounds, and by proclaiming, with the aim of arousing inclination there, the Saṅgha is not divided even by the taking of a voting ticket.

When, however, in this way four or more take a voting ticket and perform a distinct kamma or uddesa, then the Saṅgha is said to be divided.

Thus, it is impossible that a person endowed with right view could cause a schism in the Saṅgha.

There, first of all by means of action (kammato) – here, indeed, for a human being to intentionally deprive a human being, either his mother or his father, or even one of changed gender, of life, is an ānantariya-kamma; even if, thinking "I will ward off its ripening," having filled the entire universe with golden stūpas equal in measure to the great caitya, and having filled the entire universe and given a great gift to the Saṅgha of seated monks, and though not releasing hold of the edge of the saṃghāṭī (robe) of the Blessed Buddha, he lives wandering about, at the breaking up of the body he is reborn in hell itself.

But whoever, himself being a human being, deprives an animal mother or father, or whoever, himself being an animal, [deprives] a human being, or even just an animal [deprives] an animal, of life, his kamma is not an ānantariya-kamma, but it is weighty; the ānantariya-kamma remains having been committed.

But this question is spoken of in terms of the class of human beings.

By means of action

There, the eḷakacatukka, the saṅgāmacatukka, and the coracatukka are to be told.

Even with the intention of killing a goat, a person, standing in the place of a goat, if he kills a human mother or father, touches the ānantariya.
However, if one kills a lamb with the intention of killing the lamb, or with the intention of killing one's parents, it does not result in immediate retribution.
If one kills one's parents with the intention of killing one's parents, it certainly results in immediate retribution.
The same principle applies to the other pair of fourfold classifications.
And just as with one's parents, these fourfold classifications should also be understood in the case of an arhat.

Only by killing a human arhat does one incur immediate retribution, not by killing a yaksha or a ghost.
But the karma is weighty, just like that of immediate retribution.
And if a person gives a blow with a weapon or poison to a human arhat, even when that person was still a common person, and if that arhat attains arhatship and dies from that very cause, then it certainly constitutes the killing of an arhat.
However, whatever gift was given during the time when one was a common person, and one enjoys it having attained arhatship, it is considered to have been given to a common person.
There is no immediate retribution for killing a remaining noble individual.
But the karma is weighty, just like that of immediate retribution.

When Devadatta threw a stone, a fragment broke off and struck the Tathāgata's foot, and like a foot struck by an ax, the foot became internally bloody.

129. Ekissā lokadhātuyā means dasasahassilokadhātuyā.

There are three fields (khettāni): the field by birth (jātikhettaṃ), the field of authority (āṇākhettaṃ), and the field of range (visayakhettaṃ).

Therein, jātikhettaṃ means the ten-thousandfold world-system (dasasahassī lokadhātu).

For it shakes when the Tathāgata descends into his mother's womb, at his departure, at the time of his awakening, at the turning of the wheel of the Dharma, at the relinquishing of the life-sustaining aggregates, and at his final passing away.

Moreover, āṇākhettaṃ means a hundred thousand million world-spheres (koṭisatasahassacakkavāḷaṃ).

For here the authority (āṇā) of the Āṭānāṭiya, the Peacock Protection, the Banner-top Protection, the Jewel Protection, and so on, extends.

But there is no measure to the visayakhettassa.

For regarding the Buddhas, there is no such thing as a non-object, since it is said, "As far as the knowledge extends, so far extends the object; as far as the object extends, so far extends the knowledge; knowledge is the limit of the object, and the object is the limit of knowledge" (Paṭi. Ma. 3.5).

However, among these three fields, there is no sutra saying that Buddhas arise in another world-sphere, setting aside this world-sphere; but there is [a sutra saying] that they do not arise.

The three baskets (tīṇi piṭakāni) are the Basket of Discipline (vinayapiṭakaṃ), the Basket of Discourses (suttantapiṭakaṃ), and the Basket of Higher Doctrine (abhidhammapiṭakaṃ); the three recitations (tisso saṅgītiyo) are the recitation of the Elder Mahākassapa, the recitation of the Elder Yasa, and the recitation of the Elder Moggaliputtatissa.

These three recitations, when one has mastered the three baskets, in the Buddha's word, setting aside this world-sphere, there is no sutra saying that Buddhas arise elsewhere; but there is [a sutra saying] that they do not arise.

Tīṇi piṭakāni, tisso saṅgītiyo.

Apubbaṃ acarimaṃti means not before, not after (apure apacchā).

They do not arise at the same time; it is said that they arise before or after.

Therein, from the time he sat down at the Bodhi-seat, [thinking,] "I will not rise up from this seat until I attain awakening," until the seizing of rebirth in his mother's womb, it is not to be understood as "before."

For, with the seizing of rebirth by the Bodhisattva, with the shaking of the ten-thousandfold world-system itself, the delimitation of the field is done, and the arising of another Buddha is warded off.

From the time of his final passing away until a mustard-seed sized relic remains, it is not to be understood as "after."

For while the relics remain, the Buddhas remain.

Therefore, in this interval, the arising of another Buddha is warded off.

But when the final passing away of the relics has occurred, the arising of another Buddha is not warded off.

Apubbaṃ acarima.

The three disappearances (tīṇi hi antaradhānāni) are the disappearance of learning (pariyattiantaradhānaṃ), the disappearance of realization (paṭivedhaantaradhānaṃ), and the disappearance of practice (paṭipattiantaradhānaṃ).

Therein, pariyattī means the three baskets.

Paṭivedho means the realization of the truths.

Paṭipattī means the course of practice.

Therein, realization and practice both exist and do not exist.

For at one time, there are many monks who maintain realization; that monk must be pointed out by extending a finger, [saying,] "This is an ordinary person."

On this island itself, at one time, there was no monk called an ordinary person.

Those who fulfill the practice are also sometimes many and sometimes few.

Thus, realization and practice both exist and do not exist, but for the continuation of the teaching, learning is the measure (pariyatti pamāṇaṃ).

Tīṇi hi antaradhānāni, pariyattī, paṭivedho, paṭipattī, pariyatti pamāṇaṃ.

There is no such possibility.’ And he understands: ‘It is possible that a person engaging in good bodily conduct, for that reason, on the dissolution of the body, after death, could reappear in a happy destination, even in the heavenly world. There is such a possibility.’

‘It is impossible, it cannot happen that a person engaging in good verbal conduct… engaging in good mental conduct might on that account, for that reason, on the dissolution of the body, after death, reappear in a state of deprivation, in an unhappy destination, in perdition, even in hell. There is no such possibility.’ And he understands: ‘engaging in good verbal conduct… engaging in good mental conduct might on that account, for that reason, on the dissolution of the body, after death, reappear in a happy destination, even in the heavenly world.’

In this way, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called skilled in what is possible and what is impossible.”

vipākasamaṅgitā, completeness of result, should be understood as [occurring] only at the moment of result.

Here is this story: At Soṇagiri's foot, it is said that in the Acela monastery, there was a Dharma-speaker named Soṇatthera, his father was a dog-butcher.

The elder, while preventing him and being unable to establish him in restraint, ordained him against his will in old age, [saying], "Do not perish, old man!"

For him, lying on his sickbed, hell arose. Huge, huge dogs came from Soṇagiri's foot and surrounded him as if wanting to devour him.

He, greatly frightened, said, "Ward them off, father Soṇa! Ward them off, father Soṇa!"

What is it, great elder?

You do not see, father?

He described that situation.

Soṇatthero [thought]: “How could my father be born in hell? I will be a support for him.” Having novices bring various flowers, and having a ground-covering offering and a seat offering made in the stūpa precinct and the Bodhi tree precinct, and having his father brought on a palanquin to the stūpa precinct, and having him seated on the palanquin, [he said]: “This great elder offering has been done for your benefit, [thinking], ‘This is my Blessed One, the maker of the refuge of the wretched,’ and having venerated the Blessed One, gladden your mind.”

That great elder, seeing the offering and doing likewise, gladdened his mind. Immediately, the heaven world arose for him. The Nandana forest, the Cittalatā forest, the Mixed forest, the Phārusaka forest, celestial mansions, and dramas surrounded him and stood ready.

He said: "Go away, go away, Soṇa!"

What is this, elder? These, father, are your mothers coming.

The elder thought, "Heaven has arisen for the great elder!"
