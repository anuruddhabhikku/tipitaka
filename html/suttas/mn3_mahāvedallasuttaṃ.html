449. Evaṃ me sutaṃ – ekaṃ samayaṃ bhagavā sāvatthiyaṃ viharati jetavane anāthapiṇḍikassa ārāme. Atha kho āyasmā mahākoṭṭhiko sāyanhasamayaṃ paṭisallānā vuṭṭhito yenāyasmā sāriputto tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā āyasmatā sāriputtena saddhiṃ sammodi. Sammodanīyaṃ kathaṃ sāraṇīyaṃ vītisāretvā ekamantaṃ nisīdi. Ekamantaṃ nisinno kho āyasmā mahākoṭṭhiko āyasmantaṃ sāriputtaṃ etadavoca – ‘‘‘Duppañño duppañño’ti, āvuso, vuccati. Kittāvatā nu kho, āvuso, duppaññoti vuccatī’’ti? ‘‘‘Nappajānāti nappajānātī’ti kho, āvuso, tasmā duppaññoti vuccati. ‘‘Kiñca nappajānāti? ‘Idaṃ dukkha’nti nappajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhasamudayo’ti nappajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhanirodho’ti nappajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā’ti nappajānāti. ‘Nappajānāti nappajānātī’ti kho, āvuso, tasmā duppaññoti vuccatī’’ti. ‘‘‘Sādhāvuso’ti kho āyasmā mahākoṭṭhiko āyasmato sāriputtassa bhāsitaṃ abhinanditvā anumoditvā āyasmantaṃ sāriputtaṃ uttariṃ pañhaṃ apucchi – ‘‘‘Paññavā paññavā’ti, āvuso, vuccati. Kittāvatā nu kho, āvuso, paññavāti vuccatī’’ti? ‘‘‘Pajānāti pajānātī’ti kho, āvuso, tasmā paññavāti vuccati. ‘‘Kiñca pajānāti? ‘Idaṃ dukkha’nti pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhasamudayo’ti pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhanirodho’ti pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā’ti pajānāti. ‘Pajānāti pajānātī’ti kho, āvuso, tasmā paññavāti vuccatī’’ti. ‘‘‘Viññāṇaṃ viññāṇa’nti, āvuso, vuccati. Kittāvatā nu kho, āvuso, viññāṇanti vuccatī’’ti? ‘‘‘Vijānāti vijānātī’ti kho, āvuso, tasmā viññāṇanti vuccati. ‘‘Kiñca vijānāti? Sukhantipi vijānāti, dukkhantipi vijānāti, adukkhamasukhantipi vijānāti. ‘Vijānāti vijānātī’ti kho, āvuso, tasmā viññāṇanti vuccatī’’ti. ‘‘Yā cāvuso, paññā yañca viññāṇaṃ – ime dhammā saṃsaṭṭhā udāhu visaṃsaṭṭhā? Labbhā ca panimesaṃ dhammānaṃ vinibbhujitvā vinibbhujjitvā vinibbhujjitvā (ka.) vinibbhujitvā nānākaraṇaṃ paññāpetu’’nti? ‘‘Yā cāvuso, paññā yañca viññāṇaṃ – ime dhammā saṃsaṭṭhā, no visaṃsaṭṭhā. Na ca labbhā imesaṃ dhammānaṃ vinibbhujitvā vinibbhujitvā nānākaraṇaṃ paññāpetuṃ. Yaṃ hāvuso yañcāvuso (syā. kaṃ. ka.), pajānāti taṃ vijānāti, yaṃ vijānāti taṃ pajānāti. Tasmā ime dhammā saṃsaṭṭhā, no visaṃsaṭṭhā. Na ca labbhā imesaṃ dhammānaṃ vinibbhujitvā vinibbhujitvā nānākaraṇaṃ paññāpetu’’nti. vinibbhujjitvā vinibbhujjitvā (ka.) yañcāvuso (syā. kaṃ. ka.) ‘‘Yā cāvuso, paññā yañca viññāṇaṃ – imesaṃ dhammānaṃ saṃsaṭṭhānaṃ no visaṃsaṭṭhānaṃ kiṃ nānākaraṇa’’nti? ‘‘Yā cāvuso, paññā yañca viññāṇaṃ – imesaṃ dhammānaṃ saṃsaṭṭhānaṃ no visaṃsaṭṭhānaṃ paññā bhāvetabbā, viññāṇaṃ pariññeyyaṃ. Idaṃ nesaṃ nānākaraṇa’’nti.
Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was staying at Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

Then, in the evening, the Venerable Mahākoṭṭhika rose from his meditation, went to the Venerable Sāriputta, exchanged greetings with him, and sat down at one side. Seated to one side, the Venerable Mahākoṭṭhika said to the Venerable Sāriputta:

“‘Lacking in wisdom, lacking in wisdom,’ friend, it is said. In what way, friend, is one said to be lacking in wisdom?”

“Because one does not understand, friend, therefore one is said to be lacking in wisdom.

And what does one not understand? One does not understand ‘This is suffering ( duḥkha)’; one does not understand ‘This is the origin of suffering ( duḥkhasamudaya)’; one does not understand ‘This is the cessation of suffering (duḥkhanirodha)’; one does not understand ‘This is the way leading to the cessation of suffering (duḥkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā)’. Because one does not understand, friend, therefore one is said to be lacking in wisdom.”

“Good, friend.” The Venerable Mahākoṭṭhika delighted in and approved of the Venerable Sāriputta’s words. Then he asked the Venerable Sāriputta a further question:

“‘Wise, wise,’ friend, it is said. In what way, friend, is one said to be wise?”

“Because one understands, friend, therefore one is said to be wise.

And what does one understand? One understands ‘This is suffering (duḥkha)’; one understands ‘This is the origin of suffering (duḥkhasamudaya)’; one understands ‘This is the cessation of suffering (duḥkhanirodha)’; one understands ‘This is the way leading to the cessation of suffering (duḥkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā)’. Because one understands, friend, therefore one is said to be wise.”

“‘Consciousness, consciousness,’ friend, it is said. In what way, friend, is one said to be consciousness (vijñāna)?”

“Because one is conscious (vijānāti), friend, therefore one is said to be consciousness.

And what is one conscious of? One is conscious of pleasure, one is conscious of pain, one is conscious of neither-pleasure-nor-pain. Because one is conscious, friend, therefore one is said to be consciousness.”

“Now, friend, this wisdom (prajñā) and this consciousness (vijñāna) – are these things connected, or disconnected? And is it possible to completely separate these things and point to their difference?” “Now, friend, this wisdom and this consciousness – these things are connected, not disconnected. It is not possible to completely separate these things and point to their difference. For what one understands, friend, that one is conscious of; and what one is conscious of, that one understands. Therefore, these things are connected, not disconnected. It is not possible to completely separate these things and point to their difference.”

“Now, friend, this wisdom and this consciousness – what is the distinction between these things that are connected, not disconnected?” “Now, friend, this wisdom and this consciousness – with regard to these things …
Wisdom should be cultivated with regard to the relatedness of phenomena, not the unrelatedness, and consciousness should be understood. These are their variations.
449.Evaṃme sutanti mahāvedallasuttaṃ. Tattha āyasmāti sagāravasappatissavacanametaṃ. Mahākoṭṭhikoti tassa therassa nāmaṃ. Paṭisallānā vuṭṭhitoti phalasamāpattito vuṭṭhito. Duppañño duppaññoti ettha paññāya duṭṭhaṃ nāma natthi, appañño nippaññoti attho. Kittāvatā nu khoti kāraṇaparicchedapucchā, kittakena nu kho evaṃ vuccatīti attho. Pucchā ca nāmesā adiṭṭhajotanāpucchā, diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā, vimaticchedanāpucchā, anumatipucchā, kathetukamyatāpucchāti pañcavidhā hoti. Tāsamidaṃ nānākaraṇaṃ – Katamā adiṭṭhajotanāpucchā? Pakatiyā lakkhaṇaṃ aññātaṃ hoti adiṭṭhaṃ atulitaṃ atīritaṃ avibhūtaṃ avibhāvitaṃ, tassa ñāṇāya dassanāya tulanāya tīraṇāya vibhūtāya vibhāvanatthāya pañhaṃ pucchati. Ayaṃ adiṭṭhajotanāpucchā. adiṭṭhajotanāpucchā Katamā diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā? Pakatiyā lakkhaṇaṃ ñātaṃ hoti diṭṭhaṃ tulitaṃ tīritaṃ vibhūtaṃ vibhāvitaṃ, aññehi paṇḍitehi saddhiṃ saṃsandanatthāya pañhaṃ pucchati. Ayaṃ diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā. diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā Katamā vimaticchedanāpucchā? Pakatiyā saṃsayapakkhando hoti vimatipakkhando, dveḷhakajāto, ‘‘evaṃ nu kho, na nu kho, kiṃ nu kho, kathaṃ nu kho’’ti, so vimaticchedanatthāya pañhaṃ pucchati. Ayaṃ vimaticchedanāpucchā (mahāni. 150; cūḷani. puṇṇakamāṇavapucchāniddesa 12). vimaticchedanāpucchā ‘‘Taṃ kiṃ maññatha, bhikkhave, rūpaṃ niccaṃ vā aniccaṃ vāti? Aniccaṃ, bhante’’ti (mahāva. 21) evarūpā anumatiṃ gahetvā dhammadesanākāle pucchā anumatipucchā nāma. anumatipucchā ‘‘Cattārome , bhikkhave, satipaṭṭhānā, katame cattāro’’ti (saṃ. ni. 5.390) evarūpā bhikkhusaṅghaṃ sayameva pucchitvā sayameva vissajjetukāmassa pucchā kathetukamyatāpucchā nāma. Tāsu idha diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā adhippetā. kathetukamyatāpucchā Thero hi attano divāṭṭhāne nisīditvā sayameva pañhaṃ samuṭṭhapetvā sayaṃ vinicchinanto idaṃ suttaṃ ādito paṭṭhāya matthakaṃ pāpesi. Ekacco hi pañhaṃ samuṭṭhāpetuṃyeva sakkoti na nicchetuṃ; ekacco nicchetuṃ sakkoti na samuṭṭhāpetuṃ; ekacco ubhayampi na sakkoti; ekacco ubhayampi sakkoti. Tesu thero ubhayampi sakkotiyeva. Kasmā? Mahāpaññatāya. Mahāpaññaṃ nissāya hi imasmiṃ sāsane sāriputtatthero, mahākaccānatthero, puṇṇatthero, kumārakassapatthero, ānandatthero, ayameva āyasmāti sambahulā therā visesaṭṭhānaṃ adhigatā. Na hi sakkā yāya vā tāya vā appamattikāya paññāya samannāgatena bhikkhunā sāvakapāramīñāṇassa matthakaṃ pāpuṇituṃ, mahāpaññena pana sakkāti mahāpaññatāya sāriputtatthero taṃ ṭhānaṃ adhigato. Paññāya hi therena sadiso natthi. Teneva naṃ bhagavā etadagge ṭhapesi – ‘‘etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, mama sāvakānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ mahāpaññānaṃ yadidaṃ sāriputto’’ti (a. ni. 1.189). sāriputtatthero Tathā na sakkā yāya vā tāya vā appamattikāya paññāya samannāgatena bhikkhunā bhagavatā saṃkhittena bhāsitassa sabbaññutaññāṇena saddhiṃ saṃsanditvā samānetvā vitthārena atthaṃ vibhajetuṃ, mahāpaññena pana sakkāti mahāpaññatāya mahākaccānatthero tattha paṭibalo jāto, teneva naṃ bhagavā etadagge ṭhapesi – ‘‘etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, mama sāvakānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ saṃkhittena bhāsitassa vitthārena atthaṃ vibhajantānaṃ yadidaṃ mahākaccāno’’ti (a. ni. 1.197). mahākaccānatthero Tathā na sakkā yāya vā tāya vā appamattikāya paññāya samannāgatena bhikkhunā dhammakathaṃ kathentena dasa kathāvatthūni āharitvā satta visuddhiyo vibhajantena dhammakathaṃ kathetuṃ, mahāpaññena pana sakkāti mahāpaññatāya puṇṇatthero catuparisamajjhe alaṅkatadhammāsane cittabījaniṃ gahetvā nisinno līḷāyanto puṇṇacando viya dhammaṃ kathesi. Teneva naṃ bhagavā etadagge ṭhapesi – ‘‘etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, mama sāvakānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ dhammakathikānaṃ yadidaṃ puṇṇo mantāṇiputto’’ti (a. ni. 1.196). puṇṇatthero Tathā yāya vā tāya vā appamattikāya paññāya samannāgato bhikkhu dhammaṃ kathento ito vā etto vā anukkamitvā yaṭṭhikoṭiṃ gahetvā andho viya, ekapadikaṃ daṇḍakasetuṃ āruḷho viya ca gacchati. Mahāpañño pana catuppadikaṃ gāthaṃ nikkhipitvā upamā ca kāraṇāni ca āharitvā tepiṭakaṃ buddhavacanaṃ gahetvā heṭṭhupariyaṃ karonto kathesi. Mahāpaññatāya pana kumārakassapatthero catuppadikaṃ gāthaṃ nikkhipitvā upamā ca kāraṇāni ca āharitvā tehi saddhiṃ yojento jātassare pañcavaṇṇāni kusumāni phullāpento viya sinerumatthake vaṭṭisahassaṃ telapadīpaṃ jālento viya tepiṭakaṃ buddhavacanaṃ heṭṭhupariyaṃ karonto kathesi. Teneva naṃ bhagavā etadagge ṭhapesi – ‘‘etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, mama sāvakānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ cittakathikānaṃ yadidaṃ kumārakassapo’’ti (a. ni. 1.217). kumārakassapatthero Tathā yāya vā tāya vā appamattikāya paññāya samannāgato bhikkhu catūhi māsehi catuppadikampi gāthaṃ gahetuṃ na sakkoti. Mahāpañño pana ekapade ṭhatvā padasatampi padasahassampi gaṇhāti. Ānandatthero pana mahāpaññatāya ekapaduddhāre ṭhatvā sakiṃyeva sutvā puna apucchanto saṭṭhi padasahassāni pannarasa gāthāsahassāni valliyā pupphāni ākaḍḍhitvā gaṇhanto viya ekappahāreneva gaṇhāti. Gahitagahitaṃ pāsāṇe khatalekhā viya, suvaṇṇaghaṭe pakkhittasīhavasā viya ca gahitākāreneva tiṭṭhati. Teneva naṃ bhagavā etadagge ṭhapesi – ‘‘etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, mama sāvakānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ gatimantānaṃ yadidaṃ ānando , satimantānaṃ, dhitimantānaṃ, bahussutānaṃ, upaṭṭhākānaṃ yadidaṃ ānando’’ti (a. ni. 1.219-223). Ānandatthero Na hi sakkā yāya vā tāya vā appamattikāya paññāya samannāgatena bhikkhunā catupaṭisambhidāpabhedassa matthakaṃ pāpuṇituṃ. Mahāpaññena pana sakkāti mahāpaññatāya mahākoṭṭhitatthero adhigamaparipucchāsavanapubbayogānaṃ vasena anantanayussadaṃ paṭisambhidāpabhedaṃ patto. Teneva naṃ bhagavā etadagge ṭhapesi – ‘‘etadaggaṃ, bhikkhave, mama sāvakānaṃ bhikkhūnaṃ paṭisambhidāpattānaṃ yadidaṃ mahākoṭṭhito’’ti (a. ni. 1.218). mahākoṭṭhitatthero Iti thero mahāpaññatāya pañhaṃ samuṭṭhāpetumpi nicchetumpīti ubhayampi sakkoti. So divāṭṭhāne nisīditvā sayameva sabbapañhe samuṭṭhapetvā sayaṃ vinicchinanto idaṃ suttaṃ ādito paṭṭhāya matthakaṃ pāpetvā, ‘‘sobhanā vata ayaṃ dhammadesanā, jeṭṭhabhātikena naṃ dhammasenāpatinā saddhiṃ saṃsandissāmi, tato ayaṃ dvinnampi amhākaṃ ekamatiyā ekajjhāsayena ca ṭhapitā atigarukā bhavissati pāsāṇacchattasadisā, caturoghanittharaṇatthikānaṃ titthe ṭhapitanāvā viya, maggagamanatthikānaṃ sahassayuttaājaññaratho viya bahupakārā bhavissatī’’ti diṭṭhasaṃsandanatthaṃ pañhaṃ pucchi. Tena vuttaṃ – ‘‘tāsu idha diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā adhippetā’’ti. Nappajānātīti ettha yasmā nappajānāti, tasmā duppaññoti vuccatīti ayamattho. Esa nayo sabbattha. "Kittāvatā nu kho" is an inquiry that delimits the reason; it means "By how much is it said thus?"

The inquiry is fivefold: the inquiry that illuminates what is unseen, the inquiry that compares what is seen, the inquiry that severs doubt, the inquiry of agreement, the inquiry motivated by a desire to speak.

Here is their differentiation:

What is the adiṭṭhajotanāpucchā (the inquiry that illuminates what is unseen)? The characteristic of a thing is unknown by nature, unseen, unweighed, unconsidered, unmanifested, undeveloped; one asks a question for the sake of knowledge, for the sake of seeing, for the sake of weighing, for the sake of considering, for the sake of manifesting, for the sake of developing it. This is the inquiry that illuminates what is unseen.

adiṭṭhajotanāpucchā

What is the diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā (the inquiry that compares what is seen)? The characteristic of a thing is known by nature, seen, weighed, considered, manifested, developed; one asks a question for the sake of comparing it with other wise people. This is the inquiry that compares what is seen.

diṭṭhasaṃsandanāpucchā

What is the vimaticchedanāpucchā (the inquiry that severs doubt)? By nature, there is a tendency to doubt, a tendency to wavering, one is born of two minds, [thinking,] "Is it thus, or is it not thus? What is it? How is it?" 450. "Why does one ask, “Vedanā vedanā [feeling, feeling]?" One asks because one will ask about the characteristics of feeling.

Even so, it should be understood that the intended meaning is only feeling associated with the three realms of sammasana practice and wandering.

"Why does [one] feel pleasure (sukham-pi vedeti)?" [One] feels and experiences pleasure as the object (ārammaṇaṃ) of experience.

The same method applies to the other two instances.

"If form ( rūpa), Mahāli, were entirely suffering (ekantadukkhaṃ) and associated with suffering, immersed in suffering and not free from pleasure, beings would not be passionate about form.

And because, Mahāli, form is pleasure, associated with pleasure, immersed in pleasure, and not free from suffering, beings are passionate about form. Being passionate, they are joined together. Being joined together, they are defiled.

And if feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness were entirely suffering, Mahāli...pe...they would be defiled" (Saṃ. Ni. 3.70). It is said that through this discourse on the Great Thicket (mahālisuttapariyāyena), the object here is described as pleasure, pain, neither-painful-nor-pleasant.

Moreover, having made prior pleasant feeling an object, another pleasant feeling is felt; having made prior painful feeling an object, another painful feeling is felt; having made prior neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling an object, another neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling is felt. Thus, the meaning here should be understood.

For feeling itself feels; there is no other feeler, as it is said.

Perception: What does one ask here about perception? The characteristic of all perception.

What is the characteristic of all-pervasive perception? The characteristic of all perception and the characteristic of all-pervasive perception are the same. Even so, it should be understood that the intended meaning is only perception associated with the three realms of sammasana practice and wandering.

"Nīlakampi sañjānāti [He also recognizes the blue]:" He recognizes a blue flower or cloth by making a preparation, or by bringing about attention or application.

In this case, preparatory perception, attentional perception, and applicational perception are all suitable.

"Blue in blue": arising-perception (uppajjanakasaññāpi) is also suitable.

The same method applies to yellow and other colors.

"Yā cāvuso, vedanā [Whatever feeling, friend, there is]": Here, having taken these three—feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), and consciousness (viññāṇa)—why is understanding (paññā) not taken? Because it does not encompass all.

For when understanding is taken, feelings and so on are obtained only when associated with understanding, not when dissociated.

But without taking that, if these are taken, at the very least two five-sense-consciousness-elements (pañcaviññāṇadhammāpi) are obtained associated with understanding and dissociated from it.

Just as if three men were to say, "A thread, a thread," the fourth would say, "A thread adorned with jewels (ratanāvutasuttaṃ)."

Among them, the former three obtain much thread, whether it is in a loom or in a basket, even a spider's thread (makkaṭakasuttampi).

One seeking a thread adorned with jewels obtains it slowly; this is how this should be understood.

Or, they say that understanding is not taken here because understanding, having achieved association with consciousness from below, is completely separate.

"Yaṃ hāvuso, vedetī [Whatever, friend, one feels]:" Whatever object feeling feels, perception also recognizes that same thing.

"Yaṃ sañjānāti [Whatever one perceives]:" Whatever object perception perceives, consciousness also cognizes that same thing. This is the meaning.

What feeling, friend?

What, friend, one feels?

What one perceives?

Now, here a distinction must be understood between sañjānāti (perceives), vijānāti (cognizes), and pajānāti (understands).

There, only the prefix is the distinction.

But the word jānāti (knows) is not distinct.

A distinction must also be understood in its meaning of knowing.

For perception, with regard to blue and so forth, is merely recognizing the object. It is not able to bring about penetration of the characteristics of impermanence (anitya), suffering (duḥkha), and not-self (anātman).

Consciousness, with regard to blue and so forth, both recognizes the object and brings about penetration of the characteristics of impermanence and so on. However, striving (ussakkitvā), it is not able to bring about the manifestation of the path (maggapātubhāvaṃ).
Wisdom (paññā), in terms of blue and so forth, cognizes even the object (ārammaṇa); it brings about the penetration of characteristics (lakkhaṇapaṭivedha) in terms of impermanence and so forth; having roused effort, it is capable of bringing about the manifestation of the path (maggapātubhāva).

Just as, when a heap of kahāpaṇa coins is made on a goldsmith's board, a village man or boy of undeveloped intelligence only knows that the kahāpaṇa coins are patterned, variegated, four-cornered, and round, but does not know that they are reckoned as valuable for human use and enjoyment.

The villager knows that they are figured, etc., and that they are reckoned as valuable for human use and enjoyment.

But he does not know such distinctions as, “This one is false, this one is genuine, this one is karata, this one is smooth.”

The great goldsmith knows that they are figured, etc., and that they are reckoned as valuable, and that they are false, etc.; moreover, knowing, even having seen their form, he knows; having heard the sound of their being struck, he knows; even having smelled their aroma, he knows; even having tasted their flavor, he knows; even having ascertained with his hand their condition of being heavy or light, he knows, “It was made in such-and-such a village”; he knows, “It was made in such-and-such a market town, in such-and-such a city, in the shade of such-and-such a mountain, on the bank of such-and-such a river”; he knows, “It was made by such-and-such a teacher.”

Just so, perception (saññā) on the part of a boy of undeveloped intelligence, like the sight of a kahāpaṇa, cognizes merely the object in terms of blue and so forth.

Consciousness (viññāṇa), on the part of a village man, like the sight of a kahāpaṇa, cognizes even the object in terms of blue and so forth; in terms of impermanence and so forth, it brings about the penetration of characteristics.

Wisdom (paññā), on the part of a great goldsmith, like the sight of a kahāpaṇa, cognizes even the object in terms of blue and so forth; in terms of impermanence and so forth, it brings about the penetration of characteristics; having roused effort, it brings about even the manifestation of the path.

But that distinction of theirs is difficult to penetrate.

Just as if sesame oil, mustard oil, madhuka oil, castor oil, and marrow oil – having poured these five oils into one bowl and having stirred them for a day with yamaka churning sticks, it would be difficult to separately extract each one, saying, "This is sesame oil, this is mustard oil"; even more difficult than that...

Just as, at the place where the five great rivers enter the ocean, this meaning should be understood even by the separate extraction of the water, [saying,] “This is water of the Ganges (gaṅgā), this is water of the Yamunā (yamunā)."

This, friend, is the way in which these teachings are the same in meaning, but only different in expression.”
That is what the venerable Sāriputta said.

The venerable Mahākoṭṭhika, pleased, rejoiced at the words of the venerable Sāriputta.

The Great Vedalla Sūtra, the third [sūtra], is complete.

459. “Yā cāyaṃ, āvuso (And what is this, friend)": here, what is being asked? Here, there is nothing else that is new.

What is being asked is whether [the questioner] is asking by gathering together what has already been stated below.

But where have they been stated? "One perceives blue (nīlampi), and one perceives yellow (pītakampi), and one perceives red (lohitakampi), and one perceives white (odātakampi)" (ma. ni. 1. 450) In this connection, the immeasurable (appamāṇā) liberation of mind (ceto-vimutti) has been described.

“There is nothing, [thinking] ‘[this is the] sphere of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana),’ that is what it means” (ma. ni. 1. 451) Here, [there is the] sphere of nothingness (ākiñcaññaṃ).

“One understands (pajānāti) with the eye of wisdom (prajñācakṣunā)” (ma. ni. 1. 451) Here [there is] emptiness (śūnyatā).

“How many conditions (paccayā), friend (āvuso), are there for the maintenance and emergence of the signless (animittāya) liberation of mind (ceto-vimuttiyā)?" Here, [there is] the signless (animittā).

Thus, having gathered together what was already stated below in this connection, he asks.

However, having rejected that, and saying “These [qualities] have been specified in that particular place,” he states that there are four other dharmas that have the same name (ekajāti).

There is one dharma that has four names; in the Aṭṭhakathā it has been decided that he asks here in order to make this evident and to cause [others] to speak.

In response to that, "Ayaṃ vuccatāvuso, appamāṇā cetovimuttī (This is said to be immeasurable, friend, liberation of mind)," this is named immeasurable (appamāṇā nāma) due to its immeasurable pervasion (pharaṇaappamāṇatāya).

This (ayañhi) pervades (pharati) immeasurable (appamāṇe) beings (satte), or it pervades each (ekasmimpi) being (satte) without remainder (asesetvā).

"Ayaṃ vuccatāvuso, ākiñcaññā (This is said to be the sphere of nothingness, friend)": the sphere of nothingness (ākiñcaññā) [is so called] because of the absence of any object of perception (ārammaṇakiñcanassa abhāvato).

"Attena vā (Or by the self)": emptiness (śūnyaṃ) through the self (attā), designated as self (atta), being (bhāva), nourishment (posa), person (puggala), and so on.

"Attaniyena vā (Or by what belongs to the self)": emptiness (śūnyaṃ) through what belongs to the self (attaniyena), designated as robes (cīvara), equipment (parikkhāra), and so on.

"Animittā (Signless)": because of the absence of the sign of desire (rāganimittādīnaṃ abhāveneva), [it is] indeed signless (animittā), referring to the attainment of the fruit of Arhatship (arahattaphalasamāpattiṃ sandhāya).

"Nānatthā ceva nānābyañjanā cā (And there are diverse meanings as well as diverse expressions)": the expressions (byañjanampi) of these are diverse (nānā), and the meanings (atthopi) [are also diverse].

There, the diversity (nānatā) of expressions (byañjanassa) is evident (pākaṭāva).

However (pana), as for the meaning (attho), the immeasurable liberation of mind (appamāṇā cetovimutti) is itself exalted (mahaggatā eva hoti), belonging to the realm of form (rūpāvacarā) from the sphere of the earth (bhūmantarato); in terms of object (ārammaṇato), it has beings as its object of perception (satta paññattiārammaṇā).

The sphere of nothingness (ākiñcaññā) is exalted (mahaggatā), belonging to the formless realm (arūpāvacarā) from the terrestrial sphere (bhummantarato); in terms of object (ārammaṇato), it does not have mentionable objects (na vattabbārammaṇā).

Emptiness (śūnyatā) belongs to the desire realm (kāmāvacarā) from the terrestrial sphere (bhummantarato); in terms of object (ārammaṇato), it has conditioned phenomena (saṃskhāra) as its object of perception (saṅkhārārammaṇā).

Here (ettha), insight (vipaśyanā) is considered to be emptiness (śūnyatā).

The signless (animittā) is supramundane (lokuttarā) from the terrestrial sphere (bhummantarato); in terms of object (ārammaṇato), it has nirvāṇa as its object of perception (nibbanārammaṇā).

Ayaṃ vuccatāvuso, ākiñcaññā (This is said to be the sphere of nothingness, friend) attena vā (Or by the self) attaniyena vā (Or by what belongs to the self) animittā (Signless) nānatthā ceva nānābyañjanā cā (And there are diverse meanings as well as diverse expressions) rāgo kho, āvuso, pamāṇakaraṇo (Desire, friend, is the means of measuring) in these and other such [instances], just as at the foot of a mountain there is water containing the essence of rotten leaves (pūtipaṇṇarasaudakaṃ nāma hoti), which is black (kāḷavaṇṇaṃ); to those who look (olokentānaṃ), it seems (viya khāyati) to be a hundred byāmas deep (byāmasatagambhīraṃ).

Even a measure of the back of the foot (piṭṭhipādottharaṇamattampi) is not obtained (na hoti) by someone taking (gahetvā) a staff (yaṭṭhiṃ) or a rope (rajjuṃ) and measuring (minantassa).

Just so (evamevaṃ), as long as (yāva) desire (rāga) and other such [defilements] do not arise (nuppajjanti), it is not possible (na sakkā honti) to recognize (sañjānituṃ) a person (puggalaṃ); he seems (khāyati) like a stream-enterer (sotāpanna), like a once-returner (sakadāgāmī), and like a non-returner (anāgāmī).

When (yadā) desire (rāga) and other such [defilements] arise (uppajjanti) for him (panassa), then (tadā) he is known (paññāyati) as being impassioned (ratto), corrupt (duṭṭho), and deluded (mūḷho).
Thus, these are said to be "prominent" as they arise as if demonstrating the measure of a person, saying, "This is how much."

How far, friend, do the immeasurable liberations of mind extend? As many as there are immeasurable liberations of mind.

How many are they? Twelve: the four Brahmā-vihāras, the four paths ( mārga), and the four fruits ( phala).

There, the Brahmā-vihāras are immeasurable due to their pervasive immeasurability.

The rest are immeasurable due to the absence of defilements ( kilesa) that are the means of measuring.

Even nirvāṇa is immeasurable, but it is not a liberation of mind ( ceto-vimutti); therefore, it is not included.

"Akuppā [unshakeable]" means the liberation of mind that is the fruit of arhat-ship; since that is the foremost of all of them, therefore it is said to be the highest.

"Rāgo kho, āvuso, kiñcano [Greed, friend, is an encumbrance]" means greed, having arisen, harasses, crushes, and fetters a person.

Therefore, it is called an encumbrance ( kiñcano).

People, it is said, while having oxen thresh grain, say, "Some are reddish-brown, some are black."

Thus, the meaning of crushing is to be understood as the meaning of encumbrance.

The same method applies in the cases of hate ( dveṣa) and delusion ( moha) as well.

"Ākiñcaññā cetovimuttiyo [The liberations of mind of no-thing-ness]" are nine things: the realm of no-thing-ness (ākiñcaññāyatana), the paths ( mārga) and fruits (phala).

There, the realm of no-thing-ness is such that there is no encumbrance that is its object; therefore, it is "no-thing-ness."

The paths ( mārga) and fruits ( phala) are "no-thing-ness" because of the non-existence of encumbrances, crushing, and defilements that are fetters.

Even nirvāṇa is no-thing-ness, but it is not a liberation of mind; therefore, it is not included.

Greed ( rāga)

How far, friend, does prominence extend?

Unshakeable.

Greed ( rāga), friend, is an encumbrance.

The liberations of mind of no-thing-ness.

"Rāgo kho, āvuso, nimittakaraṇo [Greed, friend, is the cause of signs]" etc., just as two calves belonging to two families are similar.

As long as a sign has not been made for them, it is not possible to know, "This calf belongs to such-and-such family, this one belongs to such-and-such family."

But when a certain sign has been made for them, such as a goad, spear, etc., then it is possible to know.

In the same way, as long as greed does not arise in a person, it is not possible to know whether [that person] is a noble one ( ārya) or an ordinary person ( pṛthagjana).

But greed, just as it arises, arises as if making a sign of recognition, "This person is named 'greedy';" therefore, it is said to be "the cause of signs."

The same method applies in the cases of hate and delusion as well.

Greed, friend, is the cause of signs.

"Animittā cetovimutti [The signless liberation of mind]" means thirteen things: insight (vipaśyanā), the four immaterial attainments ( ārūpya), the four paths ( mārga), and the four fruits (phala).

There, insight uproots the sign of permanence, the sign of pleasure, and the sign of self; therefore, it is called "signless."

The four immaterial attainments are called "signless" due to the absence of a sign of form ( rūpa).

The paths ( mārga) and fruits ( phala) are signless due to the absence of defilements that are the cause of signs.

Even nirvāṇa is signless, but it is not a liberation of mind; therefore, it is not included.

But why is the emptiness ( śūnyatā) liberation of mind not included? That, from statements such as, "Empty of greed," is invariably present everywhere; therefore, it is not included separately.

"Ekathā [Of one meaning]" means of one meaning in terms of object.

Immeasurable, no-thing-ness, emptiness, signless—indeed, all these are names for nirvāṇa.

Thus, in this way, it is of one meaning.

But in another place, they are immeasurable, in another no-thing-ness, in another emptiness, in another signless; thus, in this way, they are of diverse expression.
Thus, the elder completed the teaching in accordance with the connection.
