<pb ed="P" n="3.0216"/> <pb ed="M" n="3.0259"/> <note>yogācariyānaṃ (ka.)</note>
Thus have I heard:

Once the Blessed One was staying at Sāvatthī in Jeta's Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Park. There the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying: "Monks."

"Venerable sir," those monks replied to the Blessed One.

The Blessed One said this: "Monks, I will teach you an exposition of the six sense bases (ṣaḷāyatanavibhaṅga).

Listen to that, apply your minds thoroughly and I will speak."

"As you say, lord," those monks responded to the Blessed One.

The Blessed One said this:

"The six internal sense bases (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) should be understood. The six external sense bases (bāhirāni āyatanāni) should be understood. The six classes of consciousness (viññāṇakāyā) should be understood. The six classes of contact (phassakāyā) should be understood. The eighteen mental preoccupations (manopavicārā) should be understood. The thirty-six stations of beings (sattapadā) should be understood. Therein, by relying on this, abandon that. There are three foundations of mindfulness (satipaṭṭhānā) that the noble one (ariyo) cultivates, and cultivating which the teacher (satthā) is qualified to instruct a group (gaṇa). He is called the unsurpassed tamer of men (anuttaro purisadammasārathī) among the yoga practitioners (yoggācariyānaṃ)." This is an outline of the exposition of the six sense bases (saḷāyatanavibhaṅgassa).

305. “‘The six internal bases (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) should be understood.’ So it was said.

What was said in reference to this? ‘The eye-base (cakkhāyatana), the ear-base (sotāyatana), the nose-base (ghānāyatana), the tongue-base (jivhāyatana), the body-base (kāyāyatana), and the mind-base (manāyatana) – the six internal bases should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘‘‘The six external bases (bāhirāni āyatanāni) should be understood.’ So it was said.

What was said in reference to this? ‘The form-base (rūpāyatana), the sound-base (saddāyatana), the odour-base (gandhāyatana), the flavour-base (rasāyatana), the tangible-base (phoṭṭhabbāyatana), and the mind-object-base (dhammāyatana) – the six external bases should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘‘‘The six classes of consciousness (viññāṇakāyā) should be understood.’ So it was said.

What was said in reference to this? ‘Eye-consciousness (cakkhuviññāṇa), ear-consciousness (sotaviññāṇa), nose-consciousness (ghānaviññāṇa), tongue-consciousness (jivhāviññāṇa), body-consciousness (kāyaviññāṇa), and mind-consciousness (manoviññāṇa) – the six classes of consciousness should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘‘‘The six classes of contact (phassakāyā) should be understood.’ So it was said.

What was said in reference to this? ‘Eye-contact (cakkhusamphasso), ear-contact (sotasamphasso), nose-contact (ghānasamphasso), tongue-contact (jivhāsamphasso), body-contact (kāyasamphasso), and mind-contact (manosamphasso) – the six classes of contact should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘‘‘The eighteen kinds of mental exploration (manopavicārā) should be understood.’ So it was said.

What was said in reference to this? ‘On seeing a form (rūpa) with the eye (cakkhunā), one explores a form productive of joy (somanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ rūpaṃ upavicarati), one explores a form productive of grief (domanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ rūpaṃ upavicarati), one explores a form productive of equanimity (upekkhāṭṭhānīyaṃ rūpaṃ upavicarati).

On hearing a sound (saddaṃ sutvā) with the ear… On smelling an odour (gandhaṃ ghāyitvā) with the nose… On tasting a flavour (rasaṃ sāyitvā) with the tongue… On touching a tangible (phoṭṭhabbaṃ phusitvā) with the body… On cognizing a mind-object (dhammaṃ viññāya) with the mind, one explores a mind-object productive of joy (somanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ dhammaṃ upavicarati), one explores a mind-object productive of grief (domanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ dhammaṃ upavicarati), one explores a mind-object productive of equanimity (upekkhāṭṭhānīyaṃ dhammaṃ upavicarati).

Thus there are six kinds of exploration with joy (somanassūpavicārā), six kinds of exploration with grief (domanassūpavicārā), and six kinds of exploration with equanimity (upekkhūpavicārā). The eighteen kinds of mental exploration should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

306. “‘The thirty-six positions of beings should be understood.’ So it was said. But with reference to what was this said? Six kinds of joy based on the household life, six kinds of joy based on renunciation, six kinds of grief based on the household life, six kinds of grief based on renunciation, six kinds of equanimity based on the household life, six kinds of equanimity based on renunciation.

What are the six kinds of joy based on the household life? When one regards as an acquisition the acquisition of forms cognizable by the eye that are wished for, desired, agreeable, gratifying, delightful, and associated with worldliness—or when one recalls what was formerly acquired that has passed, ceased, and changed—joy arises. Such joy as this is called joy based on the household life. When one regards as an acquisition the acquisition of sounds cognizable by the ear…the acquisition of odors cognizable by the nose…the acquisition of flavors cognizable by the tongue…the acquisition of tangibles cognizable by the body…the acquisition of mind-objects cognizable by the mind that are wished for, desired, agreeable…joy arises. Such joy as this is called joy based on the household life. These are the six kinds of joy based on the household life.

What are the six kinds of joy based on renunciation? When, by knowing the impermanence, change, fading away, and cessation of forms, one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom that forms both formerly and now are all impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, joy arises. Such joy as this is called joy based on renunciation. “When, by knowing the impermanence, change, fading away, and cessation of sounds…of odors…of flavors…of tangibles… of mind-objects, one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom that mind-objects both formerly and now are all impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, joy arises. Such joy as this is called joy based on renunciation. These are the six kinds of joy based on renunciation.

307. What are the six kinds of grief based on the household life ( gehasitaṃ domanassaṃ)? When one regards as a non-acquisition the non-acquisition of forms cognizable by the eye ( cakkhuviññeyyānaṃ rūpānaṃ) that are wished for, desired, agreeable, gratifying, and associated with worldliness…the non-acquisition of sounds cognizable by the ear ( sotaviññeyyānaṃ saddānaṃ)…the non-acquisition of odors cognizable by the nose ( ghānaviññeyyānaṃ gandhānaṃ)…the non-acquisition of flavors cognizable by the tongue ( jivhāviññeyyānaṃ rasānaṃ)…the non-acquisition of tangibles cognizable by the body ( kāyaviññeyyānaṃ phoṭṭhabbānaṃ)…the non-acquisition of mind-objects cognizable by the mind ( manoviññeyyānaṃ dhammānaṃ) - or when one recalls what was formerly not acquired that has passed, ceased, and changed - grief (domanassaṃ) arises.

Such grief as this is called grief based on the household life (gehasitaṃ domanassaṃ).

These are the six kinds of grief based on the household life (gehasitāni domanassāni).

What are the six kinds of grief based on renunciation (nekkhamma-sitāni domanassāni)? When, by knowing the impermanence (aniccatāṃ), change (vipariṇāma), fading away (virāga), and cessation (nirodhaṃ) of forms (rūpānaṃ), one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom (sammappaññāya) that forms both formerly (pubbe ceva rūpā) and now (etarahi ca) are all impermanent (aniccā), suffering ( dukkhā), and subject to change (vipariṇāmadhammā), one generates a longing (pihaṃ) for the supreme liberations (anuttaresu vimokkhesu) thus: ‘When shall I enter upon (upasampajja) and abide in (viharissāmi) that base (tadāyatanaṃ) that the noble ones (ariyā) now (etarahi) enter upon (upasampajja) and abide in (viharanti)?’ In one who thus generates a longing for the supreme liberations, grief (domanassaṃ) arises with that longing (pihapaccayā) as condition.

Such grief as this is called grief based on renunciation (nekkhamma-sitaṃ domanassaṃ).

When, by knowing the impermanence (aniccatāṃ), change (vipariṇāma), fading away (virāga), and cessation (nirodhaṃ) of sounds (saddānaṃ)…of odors (gandhānaṃ)…of flavors (rasānaṃ)…of tangibles (phoṭṭhabbānaṃ)…of mind-objects (dhammānaṃ), one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom (sammappaññāya) that mind-objects both formerly (pubbe ceva dhammā) and now (etarahi ca) are all impermanent (aniccā), suffering ( dukkhā), and subject to change (vipariṇāmadhammā), one generates a longing (pihaṃ) for the supreme liberations (anuttaresu vimokkhesu) thus: ‘When shall I enter upon (upasampajja) and abide in (viharissāmi) that base (tadāyatanaṃ) that the noble ones (ariyā) now (etarahi) enter upon (upasampajja) and abide in (viharanti)?’ In one who thus generates a longing for the supreme liberations, grief (domanassaṃ) arises with that longing (pihapaccayā) as condition.

Such grief as this is called grief based on renunciation (nekkhamma-sitaṃ domanassaṃ).

These are the six kinds of grief based on renunciation (nekkhamma-sitāni domanassāni).

308. What are the six kinds of equanimity based on household life ( gehasitā upekkhā)? When a foolish, deluded ordinary person ( puthujjanassa) sees a form ( rūpaṃ) with the eye ( cakkhunā), equanimity ( upekkhā) arises. Such equanimity ( upekkhā) does not transcend the form ( rūpaṃ). Therefore, it is called equanimity based on household life ( gehasitā upekkhā).

On hearing a sound ( saddaṃ) with the ear ( sotena)… On smelling an odor ( gandhaṃ) with the nose ( ghānena)… On tasting a flavor ( rasaṃ) with the tongue ( jivhāya)… On touching a tangible ( phoṭṭhabbaṃ) with the body ( kāyena)… On cognizing a mind-object ( dhammaṃ) with the mind ( manasā), equanimity ( upekkhā) arises in a foolish, deluded ordinary person ( puthujjanassa) who has not overcome his limitations, who has not overcome the results of action, who is blind to danger, and who is untaught. Such equanimity ( upekkhā) does not transcend the mind-object ( dhammaṃ). Therefore, it is called equanimity based on the household life ( gehasitā upekkhā). These are the six kinds of equanimity based on household life ( gehasitā upekkhā).

What are the six kinds of equanimity based on renunciation ( nekkhammasitā upekkhā)? When, by knowing the impermanence ( aniccatā), change ( vipariṇāma), fading away ( virāga), and cessation ( nirodhaṃ) of forms ( rūpānaṃ), one sees as it actually is ( yathābhūtaṃ) with proper wisdom ( sammappaññāya) that forms ( rūpā) both formerly ( pubbe) and now are all impermanent ( aniccā), suffering ( dukkhā), and subject to change ( vipariṇāmadhammā), equanimity ( upekkhā) arises. Such equanimity ( upekkhā) transcends the form ( rūpaṃ). Therefore, it is called equanimity based on renunciation ( nekkhammasitā upekkhā).

…of sounds ( saddānaṃ)… of odors ( gandhānaṃ)… of flavors ( rasānaṃ)… of tangibles ( phoṭṭhabbānaṃ)… of mind-objects ( dhammānaṃ), one sees as it actually is ( yathābhūtaṃ) with proper wisdom ( sammappaññāya) that mind-objects ( dhammā) both formerly and now are all impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, equanimity arises. Such equanimity transcends the mind-object. Therefore it is called equanimity based on renunciation. These are the six kinds of equanimity based on renunciation.

‘The thirty-six positions of beings should be understood.’ Thus was it said. And in reference to this was it said.

There is, bhikkhus, equanimity regarding the base of infinite space (ākāsānañcāyatananissitā), the base of infinite consciousness (viññāṇañcāyatananissitā), the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatananissitā), and the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatananissitā). 311. ‘‘‘Three foundations of mindfulness (smṛti-prasthāna) are those that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which, the Teacher is qualified to instruct a group.’ So it was said. And with reference to what was this said? Here, monks, the Teacher teaches the dharma to the disciples, compassionate, seeking their welfare, and taking compassion as his basis, [saying:] ‘This is for your benefit, this is for your happiness.’

His disciples do not wish to hear, do not lend an ear, and do not establish awareness in their minds, turning away from the Teacher's instruction.

There, monks, the Tathāgata is neither dissatisfied ( anāttamano ) nor does he feel dissatisfaction (anattamanataṃ paṭisaṃvedeti); but dwells unperturbed (anavassuto), mindful (sato) and fully aware (sampajāno).

This, monks, is called the first foundation of mindfulness (smṛti-prasthāna) that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which, the Teacher is qualified to instruct a group.

‘‘Furthermore, monks, the Teacher teaches the dharma to the disciples, compassionate, seeking their welfare, and taking compassion as his basis, [saying:] ‘This is for your benefit, this is for your happiness.’

Some of his disciples do not wish to hear, do not lend an ear, and do not establish awareness in their minds, turning away from the Teacher's instruction; some disciples wish to hear, lend an ear, and establish awareness in their minds, not turning away from the Teacher's instruction.

There, monks, the Tathāgata is neither dissatisfied (anāttamano) nor does he feel dissatisfaction (anattamanataṃ paṭisaṃvedeti); and he is neither satisfied (āttamano) nor does he feel satisfaction (āttamanataṃ paṭisaṃvedeti).

Dissatisfaction (anattamanatā) and satisfaction (attamanatā) – having abandoned both, he dwells in equanimity (upekkhako), mindful (sato) and fully aware (sampajāno).

This is called, monks, the second foundation of mindfulness (smṛti-prasthāna) that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which, the Teacher is qualified to instruct a group.

‘‘Furthermore, monks, the Teacher teaches the dharma to the disciples, compassionate, seeking their welfare, and taking compassion as his basis, [saying:] ‘This is for your benefit, this is for your happiness.’

His disciples wish to hear, lend an ear, and establish awareness in their minds, not turning away from the Teacher's instruction.

There, monks, the Tathāgata is satisfied (āttamano) and feels satisfaction (āttamanataṃ paṭisaṃvedeti), and dwells unperturbed (anavassuto), mindful (sato) and fully aware (sampajāno).

This is called, monks, the third foundation of mindfulness (smṛti-prasthāna) that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which, the Teacher is qualified to instruct a group.

‘Three foundations of mindfulness (smṛti-prasthāna) are those that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which, the Teacher is qualified to instruct a group’ – this that was said, was said with reference to this.

312. “‘So it is that he is called the unsurpassed leader of persons to be tamed among the teachers of training.’ This is what was said. But in regard to what was it said? Guided by the elephant tamer, bhikkhus, the elephant to be tamed goes in one direction - east, west, north, or south. Guided by the horse tamer, bhikkhus, the horse to be tamed goes in one direction - east, west, north, or south. Guided by the ox tamer, bhikkhus, the ox to be tamed goes in one direction - east, west, north, or south. “Bhikkhus, guided by the Tathāgata, accomplished and fully enlightened, the person to be tamed goes in eight directions.

“Possessed of material form (rūpī), he sees forms: this is the first direction (disā). Not perceiving forms internally, he sees forms externally: this is the second direction. He is resolved only upon the beautiful: this is the third direction. With the complete surmounting of perceptions of form (rūpasaññā), with the disappearance of perceptions of sensory impact (paṭighasaññā), with non-attention to perceptions of diversity (nānattasaññā), aware that ‘space is infinite,’ he enters upon and abides in the base of infinite space (ākāsānañcāyatanaṃ): this is the fourth direction; With the complete surmounting of the base of infinite space, aware that ‘consciousness is infinite,’ he enters upon and abides in the base of infinite consciousness (viññāṇañcāyatanaṃ): this is the fifth direction; With the complete surmounting of the base of infinite consciousness, aware that ‘there is nothing,’ he enters upon and abides in the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanaṃ): this is the sixth direction; With the complete surmounting of the base of nothingness, he enters upon and abides in the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṃ): this is the seventh direction; With the complete surmounting of the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, he enters upon and abides in the cessation of perception and feeling (saññāvedayitanirodhaṃ): this is the eighth direction.

“Bhikkhus, guided by the Tathāgata, accomplished and fully enlightened, the person to be tamed goes in these eight directions. ‘So it is that he is called the unsurpassed leader of persons to be tamed among the teachers of training.’ This is what was said, and it was with reference to this that it was said.”

This is what the Blessed One said. Gratified, the monks delighted in the Blessed One’s words.
