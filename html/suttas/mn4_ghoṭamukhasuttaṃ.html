THUS HAVE I HEARD. On one occasion the venerable Udena was living at Benares in the Khemiya Mango Grove.

Now on that occasion the brahmin Ghotamukha had arrived in Benares for some business or other.

As he was walking and wandering for exercise, he came to the Khemiya Mango Grove.

At the time the venerable Udena was walking up and down in the open.

Then the brahmin Ghotamukha went up to the venerable Udena and exchanged greetings with him.

When this courteous and amiable talk was finished, still walking up and down with the venerable Udena, he said this: “Worthy recluse, there is no wanderers’ life that accords with the dharma: so it seems to me here, and that may be because I have not seen such venerable ones as yourself or because I have not seen the dharma here.”

When this was said, the venerable Udena stepped down from the walk and went into his dwelling, where he sat down on a seat made ready.

And Ghotamukha too stepped down from the walk and went into the dwelling, where he stood at one side.

Then the venerable Udena said to him: “There are seats, brahmin, sit down if you wish.”

“We did not sit down because we were waiting for Master Udena to speak. How could someone like me presume to sit on a seat without being invited?” Then the brahmin Ghotamukha took a low seat, sat down at one side, and said to the venerable Udena: “Worthy recluse, there is no wanderers’ life that accords with the dharma: so it seems to me here, and that may be because I have not seen such venerable ones as yourself or because I have not seen the dharma here.”

“Brahmin, if you think any statement of mine is to be agreed with, then agree with it; if you think any statement of mine is to be argued against, then argue against it; and if you do not understand the meaning of any statement of mine, ask me to clarify it thus: ‘How is this, Master Udena? What is the meaning of this?’ In that way we could have a conversation here.”

“I will agree with any statement of Master Udena that is to be agreed with, and I will argue against any statement that is to be argued against; and if I do not understand the meaning of any statement of Master Udena, I will ask you to clarify it thus: ‘How is this, Master Udena? What is the meaning of this?’ In that way let us have a conversation here.”

413. “Brāhmaṇa, there are these four types of individuals to be found existing in the world.

What four? Here, brāhmaṇa, a certain individual torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself ( attantapo).

Here, brāhmaṇa, a certain individual torments others and is devoted to the practice of torturing others ( parantapo).

Here, brāhmaṇa, a certain individual torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, and also torments others and is devoted to the practice of torturing others.

Here, brāhmaṇa, a certain individual neither torments himself nor is he devoted to the practice of torturing himself, neither torments others nor is he devoted to the practice of torturing others.

Neither tormenting himself nor tormenting others, he dwells in the here & now free of hunger, unbound, cooled, sensitive to happiness with a Brahma-like mind ( brahmabhūtena attanā viharati).

Of these four individuals, brāhmaṇa, which individual satisfies your mind?”

“That individual, Master Udena, who torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, that individual does not satisfy my mind. That individual, Master Udena, who torments others and is devoted to the practice of torturing others, that individual also does not satisfy my mind. That individual, Master Udena, who torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, and also torments others and is devoted to the practice of torturing others, that individual also does not satisfy my mind. But that individual, Master Udena, who neither torments himself nor is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, neither torments others nor is he devoted to the practice of torturing others; who—neither tormenting himself nor tormenting others—dwells in the here & now free of hunger, unbound, cooled, sensitive to happiness with a Brahmā-like mind.

That very individual satisfies my mind.”

“But why, brāhmaṇa, do these three individuals not satisfy your mind?” “That individual, Master Udena, who torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, torments and tortures himself though he desires pleasure and recoils from pain; that is why this individual does not satisfy my mind.

That individual, Master Udena, who torments others and is devoted to the practice of torturing others, torments and tortures others who desire pleasure and recoil from pain; that is why this individual does not satisfy my mind.

That individual, Master Udena, who torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, and who also torments others and is devoted to the practice of torturing others, torments and tortures himself and others, both of whom desire pleasure and recoil from pain; that is why this individual does not satisfy my mind.

But that individual, Master Udena, who neither torments himself nor is devoted to the practice of torturing himself, neither torments others nor is he devoted to the practice of torturing others; who—neither tormenting himself nor tormenting others—dwells in the here & now free of hunger, unbound, cooled, sensitive to happiness with a Brahmā-like mind; he does not torment and torture either himself or others, both of whom desire pleasure and recoil from pain. That is why this individual satisfies my mind.”

415. Furthermore, brāhmaṇa, what kind of person is one who torments himself and is devoted to the practice of torturing himself? Here, brāhmaṇa, a certain person goes naked, rejecting conventions, licking his hands, not coming when asked, not stopping when asked; he does not accept food brought, food specially made, or an invitation to a meal.

He does not accept food from a pot, from a bowl, across a threshold, across a stick, across a pestle, from two eating together, from a pregnant woman, from a woman giving suck, from a woman living with a man, from where food is advertised to be distributed, from where a dog is waiting, from where flies are buzzing; he accepts no fish or meat, he drinks no liquor, wine, or fermented brew.

He is a one-house man or a one-morsel man, a two-house man or a two-morsel man… he is a seven-house man or a seven-morsel man; he lives on one saucerful a day, on two saucerfuls a day…on seven saucerfuls a day; he eats food once a day, once every two days…once every seven days—thus in such a variety of ways he dwells pursuing the practice of taking food at stated intervals every half month.

He is an eater of greens or millet or wild rice or hide-parings or moss or rice bran or rice-scum or sesamum flour or grass or cow dung. He lives on forest roots and fruits; he feeds on fallen fruits.

He wears coarse hemp or mixed material, shrouds from corpses, rags from the dust-heap, garments of bark-fibre, antelope-skins, grass, bark, shavings, blankets of human hair or horse-hair, the wings of owls. So anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussarati, seyyathidaṃ – ekampi jātiṃ dvepi jātiyo tissopi jātiyo catassopi jātiyo pañcapi jātiyo dasapi jātiyo vīsampi jātiyo tiṃsampi jātiyo cattālīsampi jātiyo paññāsampi jātiyo jātisatampi jātisahassampi jātisatasahassampi, anekepi saṃvaṭṭakappe anekepi vivaṭṭakappe anekepi saṃvaṭṭavivaṭṭakappe – ‘amutrāsiṃ evaṃnāmo evaṃgotto evaṃvaṇṇo evamāhāro evaṃsukhadukkhappaṭisaṃvedī evamāyupariyanto; so tato cuto amutra udapādiṃ; tatrāpāsiṃ evaṃnāmo evaṃgotto evaṃvaṇṇo evamāhāro evaṃsukhadukkhappaṭisaṃvedī evamāyupariyanto; so tato cuto idhūpapanno’ti. Iti sākāraṃ sauddesaṃ anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussarati. ‘‘So evaṃ samāhite citte parisuddhe pariyodāte anaṅgaṇe vigatūpakkilese mudubhūte kammaniye ṭhite āneñjappatte sattānaṃ cutūpapātañāṇāya cittaṃ abhininnāmeti. So dibbena cakkhunā visuddhena atikkantamānusakena satte passati cavamāne upapajjamāne hīne paṇīte suvaṇṇe dubbaṇṇe sugate duggate yathākammūpage satte pajānāti – ‘ime vata bhonto sattā kāyaduccaritena samannāgatā…pe… ariyānaṃ upavādakā micchādiṭṭhikā micchādiṭṭhikammasamādānā , te kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā apāyaṃ duggatiṃ vinipātaṃ nirayaṃ upapannā. Ime vā pana bhonto sattā kāyasucaritena samannāgatā…pe… ariyānaṃ anupavādakā sammādiṭṭhikā sammādiṭṭhikammasamādānā, te kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā sugatiṃ saggaṃ lokaṃ upapannā’ti. Iti dibbena cakkhunā visuddhena atikkantamānusakena satte passati cavamāne upapajjamāne hīne paṇīte suvaṇṇe dubbaṇṇe sugate duggate yathākammūpage satte pajānāti. ‘‘So evaṃ samāhite citte parisuddhe pariyodāte anaṅgaṇe vigatūpakkilese mudubhūte kammaniye ṭhite āneñjappatte āsavānaṃ khayañāṇāya cittaṃ abhininnāmeti. So ‘idaṃ dukkha’nti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhasamudayo’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhanirodho’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti; ‘ime āsavā’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ āsavasamudayo’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ āsavanirodho’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti, ‘ayaṃ āsavanirodhagāminī paṭipadā’ti yathābhūtaṃ pajānāti. Tassa evaṃ jānato evaṃ passato kāmāsavāpi cittaṃ vimuccati, bhavāsavāpi cittaṃ vimuccati, avijjāsavāpi cittaṃ vimuccati. Vimuttasmiṃ vimuttamiti ñāṇaṃ hoti. ‘Khīṇā jāti, vusitaṃ brahmacariyaṃ, kataṃ karaṇīyaṃ, nāparaṃ itthattāyā’ti pajānāti. ‘‘Ayaṃ vuccati, brāhmaṇa, puggalo nevattantapo nāttaparitāpanānuyogamanuyutto, na parantapo na paraparitāpanānuyogamanuyutto. So anattantapo aparantapo diṭṭheva dhamme nicchāto nibbuto sītībhūto sukhappaṭisaṃvedī brahmabhūtena attanā viharatī’’ti.
420. “When his concentrated mind is thus purified, bright, unblemished, rid of imperfection, malleable, wieldy, steady, and attained to imperturbability, he directs it to knowledge of the recollection of past lives (pūbbenivāsānussatiñāṇa). He recollects his numerous past lives, that is, one birth, two births, three, four, or five births; ten, twenty, thirty, forty, or fifty births; a hundred births, a thousand births, a hundred thousand births; many aeons of world contraction, many aeons of world expansion, many aeons of world contraction and expansion, : ‘There I had such a name, belonged to such a clan, had such an appearance; such was my food, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such my span of life. Passing away from that existence, I was reborn there. There too I had such a name, belonged to such a clan, had such an appearance; such was my food, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such my span of life. Passing away from there, I was reborn here.’ Thus he recollects his numerous past lives in their modes and their details.

“When his concentrated mind is thus purified, bright, unblemished, rid of imperfection, malleable, wieldy, steady, and attained to imperturbability, he directs it to knowledge of the passing away and reappearance of beings. With the divine eye (dibba cakkhu), which is purified and surpasses the human, he sees beings passing away and reappearing—inferior and superior, beautiful and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate—and he understands how beings fare according to their kamma, thus: ‘These beings— who were endowed with bad conduct of body, speech, and mind, who reviled the noble ones, held wrong views, and undertook actions governed by wrong views— with the breakup of the body, after death, have reappeared in the plane of misery (apāya), the bad destinations (duggati), the lower realms (vinipāta), in hell (niraya). But these beings—who were endowed with good conduct of body, speech, and mind, who did not revile the noble ones, held right views, and undertook actions governed by right views—with the breakup of the body, after death, have reappeared in the good destinations (sugati), in the heavenly world (sagga).’ Thus with the divine eye, which is purified and surpasses the human, he sees beings passing away and reappearing—inferior and superior, beautiful and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate—and he understands how beings fare according to their actions.

“When his concentrated mind is thus purified, bright, unblemished, rid of imperfection, malleable, wieldy, steady, and attained to imperturbability, he directs it to knowledge of the destruction of the taints (āsava). He understands as it actually is: ‘This is suffering (dukkha).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘This is the origin of suffering (dukkhsamudaya).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘This is the cessation of suffering (dukkhanirodha).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘This is the way leading to the cessation of suffering (dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘These are the taints (āsava).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘This is the origin of the taints (āsavasamudaya).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘This is the cessation of the taints (āsavanirodha).’ He understands as it actually is: ‘This is the way leading to the cessation of the taints (āsavanirodhagāminī paṭipadā).’ When he knows and sees thus, his mind is liberated from the taint of sensual desire (kāmāsava), from the taint of being (bhavāsava), and from taint of ignorance (avijjāsava). When it is liberated, there comes the knowledge: ‘It’s liberated.’ He understands: ‘Destroyed is birth (jāti), the holy life (brahmacariya) has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more for this state of being.’

“This, brāhmaṇa, is called the kind of person who does not torment himself or pursue the practice of torturing himself and who does not torment others or pursue the practice of torturing others. Since he torments neither himself nor others, he dwells in the here-and-now free of hunger, extinguished, cooled, sensitive to pleasure, with a Brahmā-like mind.”

421. Evaṃ vutte, ghoṭamukho brāhmaṇo āyasmantaṃ udenaṃ etadavoca – ‘‘abhikkantaṃ, bho udena, abhikkantaṃ, bho udena! Seyyathāpi, bho udena, nikkujjitaṃ vā ukkujjeyya, paṭicchannaṃ vā vivareyya, mūḷhassa vā maggaṃ ācikkheyya, andhakāre vā telapajjotaṃ dhāreyya – cakkhumanto rūpāni dakkhantīti – evamevaṃ bhotā udenena anekapariyāyena dhammo pakāsito. Esāhaṃ bhavantaṃ udenaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi dhammañca bhikkhusaṅghañca. Upāsakaṃ maṃ bhavaṃ udeno dhāretu ajjatagge pāṇupetaṃ saraṇaṃ gata’’nti. ‘‘Mā kho maṃ tvaṃ, brāhmaṇa, saraṇaṃ agamāsi. Tameva bhagavantaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāhi yamahaṃ saraṇaṃ gato’’ti. ‘‘Kahaṃ pana, bho udena, etarahi so bhavaṃ gotamo viharati arahaṃ sammāsambuddho’’ti? ‘‘Parinibbuto kho, brāhmaṇa, etarahi so bhagavā arahaṃ sammāsambuddho’’ti. ‘‘Sacepi sace hi (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) mayaṃ, bho udena, suṇeyyāma taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ dasasu yojanesu, dasapi mayaṃ yojanāni gaccheyyāma taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ dassanāya arahantaṃ sammāsambuddhaṃ. Sacepi sace (sī. pī.), sace hi (syā. kaṃ.) mayaṃ, bho udena, suṇeyyāma taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ vīsatiyā yojanesu… tiṃsāya yojanesu… cattārīsāya yojanesu… paññāsāya yojanesu, paññāsampi mayaṃ yojanāni gaccheyyāma taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ dassanāya arahantaṃ sammāsambuddhaṃ. Yojanasate cepi yojanasatepi (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) mayaṃ , bho udena, suṇeyyāma taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ, yojanasatampi mayaṃ gaccheyyāma taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ dassanāya arahantaṃ sammāsambuddhaṃ. sace hi (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) sace (sī. pī.), sace hi (syā. kaṃ.) yojanasatepi (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) ‘‘Yato ca kho, bho udena, parinibbuto so bhavaṃ gotamo, parinibbutampi mayaṃ taṃ bhavantaṃ gotamaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāma dhammañca bhikkhusaṅghañca. Upāsakaṃ maṃ bhavaṃ udeno dhāretu ajjatagge pāṇupetaṃ saraṇaṃ gataṃ. Atthi ca me, bho udena, aṅgarājā devasikaṃ niccabhikkhaṃ dadāti , tato ahaṃ bhoto udenassa ekaṃ niccabhikkhaṃ dadāmī’’ti. ‘‘Kiṃ pana te, brāhmaṇa, aṅgarājā devasikaṃ niccabhikkhaṃ dadātī’’ti? ‘‘Pañca, bho udena, kahāpaṇasatānī’’ti. ‘‘Na kho no, brāhmaṇa, kappati jātarūparajataṃ paṭiggahetu’’nti. ‘‘Sace taṃ bhoto udenassa na kappati vihāraṃ bhoto udenassa kārāpessāmī’’ti. ‘‘Sace kho me tvaṃ, brāhmaṇa, vihāraṃ, kārāpetukāmo, pāṭaliputte saṅghassa upaṭṭhānasālaṃ kārāpehī’’ti. ‘‘Imināpāhaṃ bhoto udenassa bhiyyosomattāya attamano abhiraddho yaṃ maṃ bhavaṃ udeno saṅghe dāne samādapeti. Esāhaṃ, bho udena, etissā ca niccabhikkhāya aparāya ca niccabhikkhāya pāṭaliputte saṅghassa upaṭṭhānasālaṃ kārāpessāmī’’ti. Atha kho ghoṭamukho brāhmaṇo etissā ca niccabhikkhāya aparāya ca niccabhikkhāya pāṭaliputte saṅghassa upaṭṭhānasālaṃ kārāpesi. Sā etarahi ‘ghoṭamukhī’ti vuccatīti. Ghoṭamukhasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ catutthaṃ.
421. When this was said, the Brahmin Ghotamukha said to the venerable Udena: “Magnificent, Master Udena! Magnificent, Master Udena! Master Udena has made the dharma clear in many ways, as though he were turning upright what had been overthrown, revealing what was hidden, showing the way to one who was lost, or holding up a lamp in the dark for those with eyesight to see forms.

I go to Master Udena for refuge, and to the dharma, and to the bhikkhu-saṅgha.

From today, may Master Udena remember me as a lay follower (upāsaka) who has gone to him for refuge for life."

"Do not go to me for refuge, Brahmin.

Go for refuge to that same Blessed One (bhagavantam) to whom I have gone for refuge.”

“Where now, Master Udena, does the esteemed Gotama reside, the worthy one (arahaṃ), the perfectly awakened one (sammāsambuddho)?” “The Blessed Lord (bhagavā), the worthy one (arahaṃ), the perfectly awakened one (sammāsambuddho) has attained parinibbāna.”

If we heard that Master Gotama was within ten yojanas, we would go ten yojanas to see that Master Gotama, to see the worthy one (arahantaṃ), the perfectly awakened one (sammāsambuddhaṃ).

If we heard that Master Gotama was within twenty yojanas...thirty yojanas...forty yojanas...fifty yojanas, we would go fifty yojanas to see that Master Gotama, to see the worthy one (arahantaṃ), the perfectly awakened one (sammāsambuddhaṃ).

Even if we heard that Master Gotama was within a hundred yojanas, we would go a hundred yojanas to see that Master Gotama, to see the worthy one (arahantaṃ), the perfectly awakened one (sammāsambuddhaṃ).
But since that Master Gotama (bhavaṃ gotamo) has attained final Nibbana (parinibbuto), we go to that Master Gotama (bhavaṃ gotamaṃ) for refuge (saraṇaṃ gacchāma), and to the Dhamma (dhammañca) and to the Sangha (bhikkhusaṅghañca) of bhikkhus.

May the honored Udena (bhavaṃ udeno) remember me (upāsakaṃ maṃ) as a lay follower (saraṇaṃ gataṃ) who has gone for refuge from this day forth (ajjatagge) as long as life shall last (pāṇupetaṃ).

“Now, Master Udena (bho udena), the king of Aṅga (aṅgarājā) gives me (me) a daily (devasikaṃ) regular donation (niccabhikkhaṃ dadāti). Let me (ahaṃ) give Master Udena (bhoto udenassa) a regular donation (ekaṃ niccabhikkhaṃ dadāmī) from that.”

“What kind of regular donation (kiṃ pana te, brāhmaṇa, aṅgarājā devasikaṃ niccabhikkhaṃ dadātī) does the king of Aṅga give you, brahmin (brāhmaṇa)?” “Five hundred kahāpaṇas, Master Udena (pañca, bho udena, kahāpaṇasatānī)!”

“It is not allowable for us (na kho no, brāhmaṇa, kappati) to accept gold and silver (jātarūparajataṃ paṭiggahetu), brahmin (brāhmaṇa).”

“If a monastery (vihāraṃ) is not allowable (na kappati) for Master Udena (bhoto udenassa), I will have a monastery built (vihāraṃ bhoto udenassa kārāpessāmī) for Master Udena (bhoto udenassa)!”

“If you desire (sace kho me tvaṃ, brāhmaṇa, kārāpetukāmo), brahmin (brāhmaṇa), to have a monastery (vihāraṃ) built, have an assembly hall (upaṭṭhānasālaṃ) built for the Sangha (saṅghassa) at Pāṭaliputta (pāṭaliputte)!”

“I am still more satisfied and pleased (imināpāhaṃ bhoto udenassa bhiyyosomattāya attamano abhiraddho) that Master Udena (bhavaṃ udeno) suggests that I give a gift (dāne) to the Sangha (saṅghe samādapeti).

So with this regular donation (esāhaṃ, bho udena, etissā ca niccabhikkhāya) and another regular donation (aparāya ca niccabhikkhāya), I shall have an assembly hall (upaṭṭhānasālaṃ) built for the Sangha (saṅghassa) at Pāṭaliputta (pāṭaliputte)!”

Then the brahmin Ghoṭamukha (ghoṭamukho brāhmaṇo) had an assembly hall (upaṭṭhānasālaṃ) built for the Sangha (saṅghassa) at Pāṭaliputta (pāṭaliputte) with that regular donation (etissā ca niccabhikkhāya) which he offered to Master Udena and another regular donation (aparāya ca niccabhikkhāya) added to it.

And that (sā) is now known as the Ghoṭamukhī (ghoṭamukhī’ti vuccatīti).

The Ghoṭamukha Sutta, the fourth, is finished (ghoṭamukhasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ catutthaṃ).

Etassa kira jānanasippe mātarampi pitarampi ghātetvā attāva ghātetabboti āgacchati. Etaṃ sippaṃ jānanto ṭhapetvā etaṃ añño sagge nibbatto nāma natthi, esa pana theraṃ upanissāya puññaṃ katvā tattha nibbattitvā ca pana ‘‘kenāhaṃ kammena idha nibbatto’’ti āvajjetvā yathābhūtaṃ ñatvā ekadivasaṃ jiṇṇāya bhojanasālāya paṭisaṅkharaṇatthaṃ saṅghe sannipatite manussavesena āgantvā pucchi – ‘‘kimatthaṃ, bhante, saṅgho sannipatito’’ti? 421. "What about you?"—he asked, to clarify whether a householder should speak of what is permissible and impermissible.

"He had [it] made"—[this means] he caused [it] to be made.

Having caused [it] to be made, and having made time, he was reborn in heaven.

It comes [to mind] that, regarding his skill in knowledge, he killed his mother and father, and he himself was to be killed.

Having established that he knows this skill, there is no other who is reborn in heaven. He, however, having relied on the elder, having done merit, and having been reborn there, reflecting, "By what karma was I reborn here?", having known the truth, one day, when the saṃgha had gathered for the renovation of the dilapidated dining hall, having come in the guise of a human, he asked, "For what purpose, bhante, has the saṃgha gathered?" [The answer is:] "For the purpose of renovating the dining hall."

By whom was this caused to be made? By Ghoṭamukha.

Where is he now? He has made time.

Is there any relative of his? There is one sister.

Have [her] summoned.

The bhikkhus summoned [her].

He approached her and said, "I, your brother, named Ghoṭamukha, having had this hall made, was reborn in heaven. In such and such place, there is wealth placed by me. Having taken that, have this dining hall made, and nourish the children," having said this, having venerated the bhikkhu-saṃgha, having risen up into the sky, he went to the world of the gods.

The rest is clear throughout.
