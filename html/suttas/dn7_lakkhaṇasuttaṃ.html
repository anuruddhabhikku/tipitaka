199. ‘‘Dvattiṃsimāni, bhikkhave, mahāpurisassa mahāpurisalakkhaṇāni, yehi samannāgatassa mahāpurisassa dveva gatiyo bhavanti anaññā. Sace agāraṃ ajjhāvasati, rājā hoti cakkavattī dhammiko dhammarājā cāturanto vijitāvī janapadatthāvariyappatto sattaratanasamannāgato. Tassimāni satta ratanāni bhavanti; seyyathidaṃ, cakkaratanaṃ hatthiratanaṃ assaratanaṃ maṇiratanaṃ itthiratanaṃ gahapatiratanaṃ pariṇāyakaratanameva sattamaṃ. Parosahassaṃ kho panassa puttā bhavanti sūrā vīraṅgarūpā parasenappamaddanā. So imaṃ pathaviṃ sāgarapariyantaṃ adaṇḍena asatthena dhammena abhivijiya ajjhāvasati. Sace kho pana agārasmā anagāriyaṃ pabbajati, arahaṃ hoti sammāsambuddho loke vivaṭṭacchado vivaṭacchado (syā. ka.), vivattacchado (sī. pī.).
199. Monks, a great man (mahāpurisa) who possesses these thirty-two marks of a great man (mahāpurisalakkhaṇāni) has only two possible destinies.

If he lives the household life, he will become a king (rājā), a wheel-turning monarch (cakkavattī), a righteous monarch (dhāmmiko dhammarājā) of the Dharma, a conqueror of the four quarters (cāturanto vijitāvī), who has established the security of his realm (janapadatthāvariyappatto), and is possessed of the seven treasures (sattaratanasamannāgato).

He has these seven treasures (satta ratanāni): the wheel treasure (cakkaratana), the elephant treasure (hatthiratana), the horse treasure (assaratana), the jewel treasure (maṇiratana), the woman treasure (itthiratana), the steward treasure (gahapatiratana), and the counselor treasure (pariṇāyakaratanameva) as the seventh.

He has more than a thousand sons (parosahassaṃ puttā), who are brave (sūrā), of heroic stature (vīraṅgarūpā), and crush the armies of others (parasenappamaddanā).

He dwells having conquered this earth (pathaviṃ) bounded by the ocean (sāgarapariyantaṃ) without stick (adaṇḍena) or sword (asatthena), by the Dharma (dhammena).

But if he goes forth from the household life (agārasmā) into homelessness (anagāriyaṃ), he becomes an Arhat (arahaṃ), a Fully Enlightened Buddha (sammāsambuddho), one who draws back the veil from the world (loke vivaṭṭacchado).

ThusI have heard: the lakṣaṇa-sūtra.
Herein is an explanation of the unelaborated terms.
The thirty-two are these thirty-two.
The marks of a great man (mahāpuruṣa) are the signs of a great man, the indications of a great man, the causes for recognizing "this is a great man (mahāpuruṣa)."
"Possessing these, of a great man (mahāpuruṣa)" and so forth should be understood in the same way as explained in detail in the Great Legend (Mahāpadāna).
"Even non-Buddhists retain [them], but they do not know ‘because of doing this action, one obtains this mark (lakṣaṇa)’" Why [does he] say [this]? Because of conformity with the [actual] origin.
For this sūtra is with [its] origin.
Now, where did its origin arise? Among humans, in the inner village.
Then, at that time, the inhabitants of Śrāvastī, having sat down in their own houses and in the doorways of [their] houses, and in assembly halls, and so forth, how did they bring it about? - "The eighty secondary marks (anubyañjana) of the Fortunate One (Bhagavat), the fathom-wide radiance, the thirty-two marks of a great man (mahāpuruṣa), by which, indeed, the body of the Fortunate One (Bhagavat), is like a fully blossoming pāricchattaka tree, like a blooming lotus grove, like a golden archway adorned with various jewels, like the surface of the sky gleaming with the rays of stars, sending forth excessively beautiful six-colored rays darting and flickering here and there.
And it is said by the Fortunate One (Bhagavat) that this mark (lakṣaṇa) does not arise from this particular action (karma) by name; however, even a mouthful of rice gruel, or a spoonful of food, arises in this way due to a previously given condition."
"What action (karma) did the Teacher (Śāstā) do, by which these marks (lakṣaṇa) arise for him?"
Then the venerable Ānanda, while wandering in the inner village, having heard this conversation, and having finished [his] meal duties, came to the monastery, and having performed the duties of the Teacher (Śāstā), and having paid homage, stood [there]. He said, "Venerable Sir (Bhante), a conversation was heard by me in the inner village."
Then the Fortunate One (Bhagavat) said, "What was heard by you, Ānanda?" [and] he announced everything.
The Teacher (Śāstā), having heard the elder's (sthavira) speech and having surrounded and addressed the monks (bhikṣu) who were sitting [there], showing in order the marks (lakṣaṇa), "These are the thirty-two marks of a great man (mahāpuruṣa) (mahāpurisalakkhaṇāni), O monks (bhikkhave)," in order to show that which arises from which action (karma), he spoke thus.

200. What, monks, are the thirty-two marks of a great man (mahāpuruṣa), with which a great man is endowed, that there are only two possible destinies, and no other? If he lives the household life, he becomes a wheel-turning monarch (cakravartin)… and so on… But if he goes forth from the household life into homelessness, then he becomes an arhat, a fully enlightened Buddha, one who draws back the veil from the world.

Here, monks, a great man has well-established feet. Monks, because a great man has well-established feet, this, monks, is a mark of a great man (mahāpuruṣa-lakṣaṇa).

Again, monks, on the soles of a great man’s feet, wheels are born, with a thousand spokes, complete with rims and hubs, complete in every way. Because of this...

Again, monks, a great man has projecting heels… and so on… he has long fingers… he has soft, tender hands and feet… he has webbed hands and feet… he has high-arched feet… he has antelope-like calves… standing without bending, he strokes and massages his knees with both palms… his genitals are concealed in a sheath… he has a golden complexion, with skin like burnished gold… he has delicate skin, and because of the delicacy of his skin, no dust or dirt clings to his body… he has single hairs in each pore… he has up-turned hairs, with up-turned hairs, dark blue, the color of añjana, curling in rings… He has a straight body like Brahmā… he has the seven ussadas… he has the torso of a lion… he has full shoulders… he is proportioned like a nyagrodha tree, the length of his body is the same as his wingspan, and his wingspan is the same as the length of his body… he has a level chest… he has a superior sense of taste… he has a lion’s jaw… he has forty teeth… he has even teeth… he has unbroken teeth… he has very white canine teeth… he has a large tongue… he has a Brahmā-like voice, with a karavīka-like quality… he has deep blue eyes… he has eyes like a cow… an ūrṇā hair grows between his eyebrows, white and soft like cotton. Because this, monks, is a mark of a great man (mahāpuruṣa-lakṣaṇa).

Again, monks, a great man has a protuberance on his head (uṇhīsa-sīsa). Monks, because a great man has a protuberance on his head (uṇhīsa-sīsa), this, monks, is a mark of a great man (mahāpuruṣa-lakṣaṇa).

These, monks, are the thirty-two marks of a great man (mahāpuruṣa), with which a great man is endowed, that there are only two possible destinies, and no other. If he lives the household life, he becomes a wheel-turning monarch (cakravartin)… and so on… But if he goes forth from the household life into homelessness, then he becomes an arhat, a fully enlightened Buddha, one who draws back the veil from the world.

These thirty-two marks of a great man (mahāpuruṣa-lakṣaṇa) are also known by outside seers, but they do not know that ‘because of doing this action, this mark is obtained.’

"And also, monks, the tathāgata, in a former birth, a former existence, a former abode, having been previously a human being, was resolute in wholesome dharmas, steadfast in resolve, in good conduct of body (kāyasucarita), good conduct of speech (vacīsucarita), good conduct of mind (manosucarita), in the distribution of gifts (dānasaṃvibhāga), in the undertaking of morality (śīlasamādāna), in the observance of the uposatha, in affection for mother (matteyyatā), in affection for father (petteyyatā), in friendliness, in reverence for brahmins, in the family, in honoring elders, and in other superior wholesome dharmas.
