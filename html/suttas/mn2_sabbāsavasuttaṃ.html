14. Thus have I heard…at Sāvatthī, regarding the discourse on the restraint of all āsavas.

Here is an analysis of individual words: Sāvatthī refers to the city that was the dwelling place of the sage Savattha, just as Kākandī, Mākandī, and Kosambī [were dwelling places]. This is according to etymologists.

However, the Teachers of the Commentary say, "Because whatever is of use and profit to man is all here, it is called Sāvatthī."

And when caravans meet and it is asked, “What goods are there?”, then, on account of the invariable reply “There is everything”, it is called Sāvatthī.

"At Sāvatthī, all kinds of equipment are always available; therefore, because it contains everything, it is called Sāvatthī."

"The lovely city of the Kosalas is beautiful and delightful; it is free from the ten noises and filled with food and drink. Having attained growth and abundance, prosperous, thriving, and delightful, the excellent city of Sāvatthī is like Alakamandā, the city of the gods."

In that Sāvatthī. Jetavana: here, Jeta means "one who conquers his enemies," or "one who is born victorious over his enemies by the king," or "Jeta, for that was simply the name given to him because of his auspiciousness"; Jetavana means "Jeta's Grove."

For it was planted by Prince Jeta, tended and guarded by him, and he was its owner. Therefore, it is called Jetavana. In that Jetavana. Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park: this householder was named Sudatta, as to the name given to him by his parents. But because all his desires had been granted and because he was devoid of avarice or stain and endowed with the special qualities of compassion and the rest, he constantly gave food (piṇḍa) to the helpless (anātha), and so he came to be known as Anāthapiṇḍika. Ārāma [Park]: here, because mendicants (pabbajita) especially take delight (rāma) there, it is a park (ārāma).

That is to say, they come from here and there and dwell delighting and rejoicing, free from yearning, because of its beauty with flowers, fruits, etc., and because it is endowed with the five kinds of requisites for a dwelling place, such as not being too far away or too close. Or alternatively, because of its excellence of the kind already stated it draws back again and delights within its confines those who had gone out to wherever it might be; thus it is a 'park'.

For that park was bought by the householder Anāthapiṇḍika from Prince Jeta for eighteen crores of gold coins covering the ground, and having had dwelling places constructed with eighteen crores of gold coins, and having completed the great monastery (vihāra) with eighteen crores of gold coins, he dedicated it to the Saṅgha headed by the Buddha, with such an expenditure of fifty-four crores of gold coins. Therefore, it is called “Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park”. In that Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

Sāvatthī. Jetavana Jetavana. Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

Here, the expression "Jetavana" is a mention of the former owner. The expression "Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park" is a mention of the later owner. What is the purpose of commemorating them? It is to encourage those who desire merit to give effect to right view.

For there, Jeta's generosity was eighteen crores of gold coins obtained from the sale of land for the gatehouse, the surrounding wall, and the mansion, and trees worth many crores. Anāthapiṇḍika’s was fifty-four crores. Thus, by his commemorating these two, the venerable Ānanda showed how those who desired to make merit did so, and thereby he exhorted others desirous of making merit similarly to give effect to right view.

"O monks, the method of restraint of all āsavas:" Why did the Buddha speak this discourse? In order to show those monks the practice for the destruction of the āsavas, beginning with the purification of defilements.

Here, "the method of controlling all āsavas" means the technique which, by itself, acts to control and gets rid of all āsavas. That is to say, the means by which the āsavas are cleared and put away in a manner that they are destroyed, abandoned, and never recur, as implied by the terms such as extinguished, irreversible, etc. What is indicated by a 'method of controlling' is an effective practical device.

Here, āsava means whatever flows out. As it is said, it discharges, it oozes from the sense faculties; that is to say: eye… mind.
Cankers flow from the nature of phenomena (dhammatas) up to the stage of changing lineage (gotrabhu), from a possibility (okasa) up to the peak of existence (bhavagga). That is, having made these phenomena and this possibility internal, they proceed. This is the meaning.

Indeed, this form is the meaning of internal action.

Alternatively, it is canker in the sense of something that has been fermenting for long, like liquor and such other spirits (madirādayo).

In the world, liquor and such other spirits are called cankers.

If cankers [are defined] by means of things that have been fermenting for long, then only these should be.

It was said, "Monks, the earliest limit of ignorance is not discernible, [it cannot be known] that before this, there was no ignorance," and so forth.

Again, whatever extends or prolongs the suffering of saṃsāra, is also canker, in that it perpetuates the process.

Here, those former explanations where defilements come are cankers, there they are fitting, even in the last action.

Not only does canker connote defilement and karma, but it also means varieties of distress or misfortune.

“In the discourses, the reference ‘Cunda, I don’t teach the Dharma only for the riddance of cankers pertaining to the life here and now’ – implying something as the very root-cause of involvements or conflicts, stands for the cankers as defilements .”

Monks, I will teach you the discourse on the restraint of all cankers.

“I have destroyed those taints by which I might have been reborn as a deva, or as a gandhabba or as a bird;

or whereby I might go to the state of a yakṣa, or be reborn among humans.

Those cankers of mine are destroyed, scattered, and made non-existent.”

Here, the threefold world (tebhūmaka) and the remaining unwholesome phenomena ( akusalā dhammā) are [considered].

“for the restraint of corruptions relating to the present life (diṭṭhadhammika), and for the prevention of corruptions relating to future lives (samparāyika)”

Here, blame, remorse, killing, bonds, and other misfortunes, and also suffering of the states of loss, and various types of distress [are considered].

Whatever is the connotation of ‘canker,’ what is meant in a given context has to be identified and understood in the proper perspective.

Indeed, in the Vinaya, "for the purpose of ridding the cankers pertaining to life here and now, and for the purpose of warding off cankers pertaining to life hereafter" is stated as twofold.

In the Saḷāyatana, “there are these three types of cankers, āvuso: the canker of sensual desire (kāmāsava), the canker of desire for the continuity of existence (bhavāsava) and the canker of ignorance (avijjāsava)” is stated as threefold.

In other suttantas and in the Abhidhamma, with the canker of views (diṭṭhāsava) along with these, it is stated as fourfold.

In the Nibbedhika-pariyāya, "Monks, there are cankers which lead to hell (nirayagāmini), there are cankers which lead to the animal realm (tiracchānayonigāmini), there are cankers which lead one to the realm of ghosts (pettivisayagāmini), there are cankers which lead one to the world of human beings (manussalokagāmini), and there are cankers which lead one to the divine sphere (devalokagāmini)" is stated as fivefold.
In the Chakka Nipata, it appears sixfold, with the passage "Monks, there are āsavas (cankers) to be abandoned through restraint (saṃvara)," and so on.

However, in this sutta, these same six appear sevenfold together with those to be abandoned through seeing (darśana).

Here, firstly, concerning the term āsava (canker), [we find] both its definition and its classifications.

Now, regarding the term saṃvara (restraint), saṃvaro means "that which restrains," that is, it prevents and obstructs, not allowing [cankers] to arise.

Thus, as it is said, "I allow, monks, one who is retiring during the day to retire having closed the door" (anujānāmi, bhikkhave, divā paṭisallīyantena dvāraṃ saṃvaritvā paṭisallīyituṃ), and "I speak of restraint (saṃvara) of the currents [of craving]; these are quelled by wisdom (prajñā)" (sotānaṃ saṃvaraṃ brūmi, paññāyete pidhīyare), and in other such instances, saṃvara is spoken of in the sense of obstructing.

This restraint (saṃvara) is fivefold: restraint by virtue (śīla-saṃvara), restraint by mindfulness (smṛti) and wisdom (jñāna), restraint by patience (kṣānti), and restraint by effort (vīrya). (svāyaṃ saṃvaro pañcavidho hoti sīlasaṃvaro satiñāṇa khanti vīriyasaṃvaroti).

Therein, "endowed with restraint by the Prātimokṣa (prātimokṣa-saṃvara)" (iminā pātimokkhasaṃvarena upeto) refers to restraint by virtue (śīla-saṃvara).

For here, the virtue (śīla) of the Prātimokṣa is said to be restraint (saṃvara).

In instances such as "one undertakes restraint (saṃvara) in the eye faculty (cakṣurindriya)" (cakkhundriye saṃvaramāpajjati), it is restraint by mindfulness (smṛti-saṃvara).

Here, mindfulness (smṛti) is said to be restraint (saṃvara).

"I speak of restraint (saṃvara) of the currents [of craving]; these are quelled by wisdom (prajñā)" (sotānaṃ saṃvaraṃ brūmi, paññāyete pidhīyare) refers to restraint by wisdom (jñāna-saṃvara).

Since wisdom (jñāna) here controls (pidhīyare) in the sense of obstructing, it is said to be restraint (saṃvara).

Moreover, through the method of "he is one who bears cold...etc.... he does not endure an arisen thought of sensuality (kāma-vitakka)" (khamo hoti sītassa…pe…, uppannaṃ kāmavitakkaṃ nādhivāseti), restraint by patience (kṣānti) and effort (vīrya) are presented here.

And since all of these are included by the designation "The discourse on the control of all cankers (sarvāśrava-saṃvara-paryāya)," their nature as restraint (saṃvara) should be understood.

Moreover, this fivefold restraint (saṃvara) is indeed present here. Of these, restraint by patience (kṣānti) and effort (vīrya) have already been spoken of.

"He avoids such unsuitable seats and such unsuitable resorts" (so tañca anāsanaṃ tañca agocaraṃ)—this, however, is restraint by virtue (śīla-saṃvara) in this context.

“Wisely reflecting, he dwells restrained with restraint of the faculty of the eye (cakṣurindriya)" (paṭisaṅkhā yoniso cakkhundriyasaṃvarasaṃvuto)—this is restraint by mindfulness (smṛti-saṃvara).

Everywhere, "wisely reflecting (pratisaṃkhā)" is restraint by wisdom (jñāna-saṃvara).

Furthermore, by not grasping at what is grasped, seeing (darśana), judicious use (pratisevanā), and development (bhāvanā) are restraint by wisdom (jñāna-saṃvara).
The term pariyāya means that by which one goes around the dharmas. It has been said that they go to origination or cessation.

Thus far, whatever had to be made explicit concerning the phrase, ‘the method of control of all āsavas,’ has now been stated.

"Monks, I declare the destruction of the effluents (āsava) for one who knows and sees, not for one who does not know and does not see.

Furthermore, monks, what knowing, what seeing, do I declare to be the destruction of the effluents (āsava)? Appropriate attention (yoniso manasikāraṃ) and inappropriate attention (ayoniso manasikāraṃ).

Monks, for one who attends inappropriately (ayoniso manasikaroto), unarisen effluents (āsavā) arise, and arisen effluents (āsavā) increase. But, monks, for one who attends appropriately (yoniso manasikaroto), unarisen effluents (āsavā) do not arise, and arisen effluents (āsavā) are abandoned.

15. Now, in "[knowing, aha]," etc., "knowing" means one who knows.

"Seeing" means one who sees.

These two terms are synonymous, having the same meaning but different expressions.

Thus, even so, "knowing" indicates the individual ( puggalaṃ) by taking understanding as its characteristic, for understanding itself is a characteristic of knowledge (ñāṇa).

"Seeing" indicates by taking the power of wisdom, for the power of seeing is wisdom.

The individual ( puggalaḥ) endowed with wisdom, like one with eyes, sees forms ( rūpāṇi) with the eye, and through wisdom he beholds the dharmas that are laid bare.

Moreover, "one who knows how to arouse wise consideration (yonisomanasikāraṃ), such that unwise consideration (ayonisomanasikāro) does not arise, thus, he sees," this is the essence here.

Some teachers speak elaborately on manifoldness (papañce), but they do not fit into this meaning.

The "destruction of the āsavas" means the abandonment of the āsavas, the complete arising of the destruction of the āsavas, the state of exhausted characteristics, non-existence. Indeed, in this sūtra, "with the destruction of the āsavas (āsavānaṃ khayā), liberation of mind (cetovimutti) is without āsavas (anāsavaṃ)," etc., the meaning is the destruction of the āsavas.

Elsewhere, the path ( magga), the fruits ( phala), and nirvāṇa are also called the destruction of the āsavas.

"Not for one who does not know, not for one who does not see," this means that I do not say [it] for one who does not know, nor for one who does not see.

By this, those who say that even one who does not know or see, purification (śuddhiṃ) comes from restraint (saṃvarādīhiyeva), they are refuted.

Wise consideration and unwise consideration

There, "wise consideration (yoniso manasikāro) " is right attention ( upāyamanasikāro), the first attention ( pathamanasikāro), and in impermanence (aniccādīsu), "impermanent" etc., in this way, or in accordance with truth (saccānulomikena), the turning of the mind (cittassa āvaṭṭanā), the repeated turning ( anvāvaṭṭanā), engagement ( ābhogo), full application ( samannāhāro), attention (manasikāro), this is called wise consideration (yoniso manasikāroti).
Wise consideration. Unwise consideration is inexpedient reflection and reflection on the wrong track.

When a thing is impermanent, one considers it to be permanent; when a thing really means affliction, one considers it to be happiness; when a thing is unsubstantial, one considers it to be substantial; when a thing is repulsive, one considers it to be attractive: this is unwise consideration and reflection on the wrong track.

This kind of wrong consideration or perverted viewing, that is to say, that turning of the mind in contravention of actuality, the repeated dwelling thereon, inclining the mind thereto, the focusing of attention thereon, and thinking to controvert actuality – this is unwise consideration.

Its meaning [is as follows]: that ordinary person by whom, in the manner that has been said, āsavas arise, that ordinary person who has been spoken of in the way beginning with "uninstructed," attends unwisely, without means, in a wrong way. There, "Was I in the past? Indeed?"—relying on the view of permanence and the view of accidental origination, he is doubtful about the existence and non-existence of himself in the past. Thus, having shown the sixteen varieties of doubt ( vicikicchā ), now this teaching ( desanā ) is begun in order to show the view-taint ( diṭṭhāsavaṃ ) with this doubt-dagger ( vicikicchāsīsena ).

Showing that, he says “tassa evaṃ ayoniso manasikaroto channaṃ diṭṭhīna” etc., meaning, to him who thus attends unwisely, of the six views.

There, as this doubt arises for that person, so it is said that when he attends unwisely, due to the force of unwise attention that is associated with doubt, one or another of the six views arises.

There, in all the expressions, the word “vā” is in the sense of विकल्प (= option, alternativity); thus, it is said that either this or that view arises.

"atthi me attā"—here, the eternalist view ( sassatadiṭṭhi ) grasps the existence of self in all times.

"saccato thetato"—[means] from what is true and what is trustworthy, from what is actual ( bhūtato ) and what is steadfast ( thirato ); thus, it is said that “this is true” with a very firm ( daḷhabhāvena ) [understanding] of what is actual ( bhūtato ).

"natthi me attā"—this, however, is the annihilationist view ( ucchedadiṭṭhi ), because it grasps the non-existence of a being ( sato sattassa ) in that place and in that place.

Or, alternatively, the former [view], because it grasps that [the self] exists in the three times, is the eternalist view; the annihilationist view is when one grasps that [the self] exists only in the present.

The latter [view also], because it grasps that [the self] does not exist in the past and future, is the annihilationist view, like those whose view is grasped by the burnt offering of ashes.

If one grasps that [the self] does not exist even in the past, it is the eternalist view only of adventitious origination ( adhiccasamuppattikasseva sassatadiṭṭhi ).

"attanāva attānaṃ sañjānāmī"—this means that by grasping the aggregates as self with the perception-aggregate ( saññākkhandha ) as the head, and understanding, with perception, the remaining aggregates, it becomes, “I cognize this self with this self.”

"attanāva anattāna"—this means by grasping only the perception-aggregate as self, and grasping the other four as not-self, it occurs thus to him who knows them with perception.

"anattanāva attāna"—[means] the perception-aggregate is not-self.

Grasping the other four as self, it occurs thus to him who knows them with perception; all of these are indeed views of eternalism and annihilationism.

"vado vedeyyo " etc. are, however, forms of attachment to the eternalist view only.

There, "vado" means "he speaks," [and thus is] the agent ( kārako ) of verbal action ( vacīkamma ).

"vedeyyo" means "he feels," he knows and experiences.

What does he feel? There, in that place and in that place, he experiences the result ( vipākaṃ ) of good and evil actions.

"tatra tatrā"—[means] in those various abodes of rebirth, courses, stations, dwelling places, and objects.

"nicco"—[means] devoid of arising and disintegration ( uppādavayarahito ).

"dhuvo"—[means] steadfast ( thiro ), having essence ( sārabhūto ).

"sassato"—[means] everlasting ( sabbakālika ).

"avipariṇāmadhammo"—[means] a nature that does not abandon its own essential state, does not undergo various changes, like the kakaṇṭaka thorn.

"sassatisama"—the moon, sun, ocean, great earth, and mountains are called eternal ( sassatiyoti ) by worldly convention ( lokavohārena ).

"sassatisamaṃ"—equal to eternal things.

As long as the eternal things stand, so long will it stand thus—by grasping this, there is such a view.

"idaṃ vuccati, bhikkhave, diṭṭhigata " etc.

"ida"—is a direct indication of what is to be said now.

And "ida" is said in relation to "view-gone," not in relation to "view."

And here, the view itself is "diṭṭhigataṃ," like something gone to excrement.

Or, this view that is gone into views is "diṭṭhigataṃ " because it is included within the sixty-two views.

Or, what is gone by means of view is diṭṭhigataṃ.

For this statement "there is a self to me," etc., is merely going by means of view; it is said that there is no self or anything eternal here.
And that view ( diṭṭhi), because it is difficult to get out of, is a thicket (gahanaṃ).

Because it is difficult to overcome and because it is frightening, it is a wilderness (kantāro), like the wilderness of famine, the wilderness of beasts, and so on.

Because it is pierced or destroyed by right view, it is a dart (visūkaṃ).

Because it grasps sometimes the eternal and sometimes the annihilated, it is a distorted throbbing, a convulsion (vipphanditaṃ).

Because of its nature as a bond, it is a fetter (saṃyojanaṃ).

Therefore, he said, "A thicket of views...and so on... a fetter of views."

Now, showing its very nature as a bond, he said, beginning with "bound by the fetter of views" (diṭṭhisaṃyojanasaṃyutto).

This is its meaning in brief.

The ordinary person, bound by this fetter of views, is not freed from these things, such as birth and so on.

Or what need is there to say much? He is not freed even from all the suffering of the round.

This, monks, is called a fixed view (diṭṭhigata), this is a fixed view, a thicket (gahanaṃ), a wilderness (kantāro), a dart (visūkaṃ), a convulsion (vipphanditaṃ), a fetter (saṃyojanaṃ), "bound by the fetter of views" (diṭṭhisaṃyojanasaṃyutto).

Furthermore, this is the elucidation of the meaning in the passages beginning with "He wisely reflects, 'This is suffering'": The noble disciple who is endowed with the four truths, the craving-related, three-realm aggregates are suffering (duḥkha), craving is the origin of suffering (duḥkhasamudayo), the non-arising of both is cessation (nirodho), the path is the means of attaining cessation (mārga). Thus, having previously grasped the four truths from the teacher, when at a later time he has ascended the path of insight, he wisely reflects on those three-realm aggregates, 'This is suffering,' he connects with the means, with the path, and he contemplates.

Here, as long as it is the path of stream-entry (sotāpattimagga), insight is spoken of as having the mind focused in this way.

That craving which is the cause and origin of that suffering, he wisely reflects, 'This is the origin (samudaya)'.

Since suffering and its origin cease and do not arise again having reached this state, therefore, that which is called nirvāṇa, he wisely reflects, 'This is the cessation of suffering (duḥkhanirodho)'.

The eightfold path that leads to cessation, he wisely reflects, 'This is the practice leading to the cessation of suffering (duḥkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā)', he connects with the means, with the path, and he contemplates.

There is this means: dormant tendencies occur only in this process of becoming (vaṭṭe), and not in cessation thereof (vivaṭṭe).

Therefore, one should determine to make this personality, that is, the aggregates, canker-free, beginning in this manner: ‘In this so-called body there are only the elements of earth (pathavīdhātu), water (āpodhātu) and the rest.’ Having determined one’s own personality in terms of the four primary elements, and the derived corporeality arising therewith, this is the aggregate of corporeality (rūpakkhandha)

One should examine the consciousness and the mental factors which arise making this body their object, and thus comprehend also the four immaterial aggregates (arūpakkhandha).

Thereafter, all these five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) are intuitively penetrated in terms of the truth of suffering (duḥkha).

But, in brief, they are only divided into two parts, name (nāma) and form (rūpa).

And this psycho-physical combination (nāmarūpa) arises with cause and condition (sahetu sappaccayaṃ).

He determines that this is the cause (hetu) and this is the condition (paccaya), dependent on conditions such as ignorance (avijjā), craving for becoming (bhavataṇhā), mental volition (kamma), and psychic nutriment (āhāra).

That is, he penetrates into the underlying characteristics and the functions of both the causative factors and the phenomena arising conditionally therefrom. Then he intuitively understands the characteristic of transitoriness (aniccalakkhaṇa) by seeing through the changefulness of the phenomena under scrutiny. Then he determines the characteristic of suffering (duḥkhalakkhaṇa) by intuiting affliction intrinsic in the oppressive condition of ceaseless rise and fall (udayabbayapīḷitattā dukkha).

Following this experience, he then determines the characteristic of unsubstantiality (anattalakkhaṇa) by intuitively understanding the substancelessness intrinsic in a condition over which one has no hold or control (avasavattanato anattā).

Thus, having grasped the three characteristics, he brings about the arising of the various stages of intuitive insights, one after the other, and thus attains to the supramundane path-insight of Stream-Entrance (sotāpattimagga).

At the moment of gaining the supramundane path-insight, all the Four Noble Truths (cattāri saccāni) are presented at once, as an integral experience (ekābhisamayena abhisameti).

One penetrates into suffering (duḥkhaṃ) by fully and directly comprehending it (pariññāpaṭivedhena), penetrates into the cause (samudayaṃ) by uprooting it (pahānapaṭivedhena), penetrates into the cessation (nirodhaṃ) by realizing it (sacchikiriyāpaṭivedhena), and penetrates into the path (maggaṃ) by developing it (bhāvanāpaṭivedhena).

And one conquers suffering (dukkhaṃ) by means of direct knowledge (pariññābhisamayena abhisameti)…and so on… one conquers the path (maggaṃ) by means of the conquest of development (bhāvanābhisamayena abhisameti), but not by means of separate insights (aññamaññena ñāṇena).

It should be understood that all these victories are not gained by separate insights, but by just one single insight (ekañāṇeneva), which has the truth of cessation, Nirvāṇa (nirodhaṃ), as the object (ārammaṇato), and which penetrates the remaining Truths, both as a function and as a conquest (kiccato paṭivijjhati ceva abhisameti ca).

At that moment of victory a thought such as this never occurs to him: ‘Here I am comprehending suffering (dukkhaṃ parijānāmī)’…and so on… ‘I am developing the path (maggaṃ bhāvemī)’.

But, by making it the object, as he realizes cessation (nirodhaṃ sacchikaroto), that very same insight simultaneously comprehends the actuality of suffering (dukkhapariññākiccampi), uproots the cause thereof (samudayapahānakiccampi), and develops the path leading to the cessation thereof (maggabhāvanākiccampi karotiyeva).

By means of this insight meditation, as he exercises ‘wise reflection,’ these three fetters (saṃyojanāni) are also instantaneously severed: self-illusion (sakkāyadiṭṭhi), comprising the twenty types of wrong views (vīsativatthukā); skeptical doubt (vicikicchā), comprising the eight kinds (aṭṭhavatthukā); and superstitious belief in rules and rituals (sīlabbataparāmāsoti), by way of the blind adherence to dogmas and external practices, saying ‘purification is brought about only by these rules and these rituals.’

Regarding the four cankers (āsavesu): since self-illusion (sakkāyadiṭṭhi) and superstitious belief in rites and rituals (sīlabbataparāmāsā) are already included in the canker of wrong views (diṭṭhāsavena saṅgahitattā), they are both cankers (āsavā ceva) as well as fetters (saṃyojanā ca).

Skeptical doubt (vicikicchā) is only a fetter (saṃyojanameva), not a canker (na āsavo).

But since it has been included within the method of overcoming through supramundane insight in "cankers are to be abandoned through seeing" (dassanā pahātabbā āsavā), it is also to be construed as a canker (pana āsavāti).

The meaning is indicated with the statement "These are called… to be abandoned" (ime vuccanti…pe… pahātabbā), these, such as self-illusion etc. are called cankers to be abandoned through seeing (dassanā pahātabbā nāma āsavāti).

Or, in another way, self-illusion (sakkāyadiṭṭhi) is distinguished by its own nature as whatever kind of view arises from among the six views (channaṃ diṭṭhīnaṃ aññatarā diṭṭhi uppajjati).

Referring to this, [the Buddha] said, "These are called, monks" (ime vuccanti, bhikkhave).

When the canker of views (diṭṭhāsave) is abandoned, craving for sensual pleasures (kāmāsavopi) and the canker of ignorance (avijjāsavopi) which are naturally connected to it are abandoned in the four types of consciousness connected with views (diṭṭhisampayuttacittesu).

24. "Having the ability to endure cold through wise reflection" means reflecting with skillful means and wisdom, one is capable of enduring the cold. The cold is bearable and endurable. Unlike an unmanly person, one is not moved or shaken even by a little cold, and does not abandon their meditation subject ( kammaṭṭhāna).

Moreover, just as the Elder Lomasanāga, though touched by even a slight cold, did not move or tremble, but kept the meditation subject in mind.

It is said that the Elder, while dwelling in the practice chamber in the cave of Piyaṅgu on Cetiyapabbata, having reflected on the hell of Lokantarika during a time of snowfall in the intermediate region, spent his time in the open air without abandoning his meditation subject.

Likewise, the application of the meaning should be understood in relation to heat and other factors also.

Just as a bhikkhu who endures even intense heat is to be regarded as "capable of enduring heat."

It is said that the Elder sat down in the outer cloister after his meal during the hot season. While focusing on his meditation subject, sweat flowed from his armpits. Then his disciple said to him, "Venerable sir, sit here; this is a cool spot." The Elder, reflecting on the Avīci Great Hell, thought, "It is out of fear of heat that I am sitting here, friend," and remained seated.

Here, uṇha (heat) should be understood as the burning torment of fire. This topic is spoken in terms of torment from the sun.

One, who not obtaining alms food or drink at two or three times on the cycle of existence without beginning (anamatagge saṃsāre), reflects on the state of existence in their own ancestral sphere, without wavering, does not abandon the meditation subject.

One, touched by excessive contact with gnats, mosquitoes, wind, sun, or insects, and reflecting on the possibility of rebirth in the animal realm, without wavering, does not abandon the meditation subject.

One, touched by contact with snakes, reflecting on the previous states of existence transmigrated many times in the cycle of existence without beginning (anamatagge saṃsāre) in the mouths of lions, tigers, and other animals, without wavering, does not abandon the meditation subject, like the Elder Padhāniya.

This one is to be understood as “capable of hunger…pe… capable of contact with snakes.”

25. 'Having reflected, he avoids a wild elephant' means: Thinking, "I am a renunciate," one should not stand near a wild elephant. For from that cause, death might occur, or even a death-like suffering. Thus, having investigated with skill, means, reason, and cause, he avoids and retreats from a wild elephant. This method applies to all the other cases.

'Wild' (caṇḍa) means fierce, or as it has been said, 'violent' (vāḷa).

'Stump' (khāṇu) means a khadira stump and so on.

'Thorn-thicket' (kaṇṭakaṭṭhāna) means a place of thorns, a location where thorns are found.

'Pit' (sobbha) means a depression with an even rim all around.

'Precipice' (papāta) means a cliff with a broken edge on one side.

'Cesspool' (candanika) means a place for discarding leftover water, refuse, and so on.

'Puddle' (oḷigalla) means a place where mud and other such things collect. It might be filled knee-deep with filth. Both of these places are haunted by non-human beings. Therefore, they should be avoided.

Here, 'in an unsuitable seat' (anāsane) means an improper seat (anāsana), which is to be understood as an isolated, concealed seat consisting of unstable materials.

Similarly, 'in an improper resort' (agocare) means an improper resort (agocara), which is fivefold in terms of prostitutes and so on.

'Evil friends' (pāpake mitte) means base, immoral individuals who are only imitations of friends, or even enemies.

'Consorting with' (bhajanta) means associating with.

'Wise spiritual companions' (viññū sabrahmacārī) means learned, intelligent spiritual companions. This is a designation for monks, for they live the same spiritual life, with a single purpose, a single instruction, and equal training. Therefore, they are called 'spiritual companions' (sabrahmacārī).

'In evil places' (pāpakesu ṭhānesu) means in base places.

'Would impute' (okappeyyu) means they would believe and be convinced, "Indeed, this venerable one has done it or will do it."

'Whatever of it' (yañhissā) means any one of the elephants and so on.

The rest is as already stated.

Here, the arising of defilements (āsava) should be understood in this way:

Lust (kāmāsava) arises in one who has been afflicted by suffering due to elephants and so on, and who longs for pleasure. Craving for existence (bhavāsava) arises in one who desires continued existence, thinking, "May there not be such suffering for us in a fortunate future existence!" Grasping at the idea that "an elephant crushes me, a horse crushes me" is the defilement of views (diṭṭhāsava). The defilement of ignorance (avijjāsava) is associated with all of these.

'Whatever of it…these are called…to be avoided' means that these many defilements, based on each of the elephants and so on, four at a time, are said to be abandoned by this avoidance, which is known as moral restraint.

The explanation of defilements to be abandoned by avoidance is completed.

" paṭisaṅkhā yoniso satisambojjhaṅgaṃ bhāvetīti": by wisely considering, he cultivates the mindfulness enlightenment factor. That is, reflecting with skillful means and the right path on the disadvantage of non-cultivation and the advantage of cultivation, he cultivates the mindfulness enlightenment factor. This method applies in each instance.

Here, although these enlightenment factors are intended to be supramundane, having arisen at the time of the three higher paths, nevertheless, in order to prevent confusion for the beginning practitioner regarding the enlightenment factors, I will make an explanation of the meaning according to their method, with a mixture of mundane and supramundane [elements].

However, in this case, the supramundane method should be adopted, abandoning the mundane method.

First of all, regarding just the initial terms of the seven [enlightenment factors] that were mentioned in the way beginning with " satisambojjhaṅganti":

A discerning person should understand the ascertainment by way of the meaning, characteristics, etc., and by way of the sequence, and by way of being neither deficient nor excessive.

Therein, first, sati (mindfulness) in satisambojjhaṅga (mindfulness enlightenment factor) [means] sati due to the meaning of remembering.

And this sati, indeed, has the characteristic of attending [to something], or the characteristic of not wobbling.

For it was said, "Just as, great king, a treasurer of the king does not lose the king's property, [saying,] 'So much, great king, is silver; so much is gold; so much is property,' just so, great king, sati (mindfulness), when arising, does not wobble regarding phenomena that are skillful, unskillful, blameworthy, blameless, inferior, superior, dark, bright, or similar. These are the four foundations of mindfulness" (Majjhima Nikāya, Paṇṇāsa 2.1.13) in detail. It has the function of not wobbling. For the Elder spoke of this characteristic only in terms of its function. Or, it has the essence of non-confusion. It is manifested as the state of confronting an objective field. Sati (mindfulness) itself is satisambojjhaṅga (mindfulness enlightenment factor). There, bojjhaṅga (enlightenment factor) [means] an aṅga (factor) of bodhi (enlightenment) or for enlightenment.

What is meant by sati satisambojjhaṅgo (mindfulness is the mindfulness-enlightenment-factor)? The community of qualities through which the noble disciple awakens at the moment of the arising of a mundane or supramundane path is called "enlightenment" (bodhi), because it is the opponent of many disturbances such as a foundation for being torpid or agitated, application, addiction to pleasure, exhaustion, eternalism or annihilationism, and because of the community of qualities consisting of mindfulness, investigation of phenomena, effort, joy, tranquility, concentration, and equanimity.

"Bujjhatī (awakens)" means he arises from the sleep of the continuum of defilements, or he penetrates the four noble truths, or it is said that he realizes nirvāṇa itself. As it was said, "Having cultivated the seven enlightenment factors, he has awakened to unsurpassed complete enlightenment." The bojjhaṅga (enlightenment factor) is a factor of that bodhi (enlightenment) consisting of a community of qualities, just like the factors of jhāna, the factors of the path, and so forth.

Because the noble disciple awakens through this community of qualities of the type that was mentioned, he is called "bodhi" (enlightenment), and the bojjhaṅga (enlightenment factor) is a factor of that enlightenment, just like the parts of an army, the parts of a chariot, and so forth. Therefore, the teachers of the commentaries said, "Bojjhaṅga (enlightenment factor) is a factor of a person who awakens." Moreover, the meaning should also be understood according to the method of discrimination (Paṭisambhidāmagga 3.17) [in the following way]: "In what sense is it a bojjhaṅga (enlightenment factor)? Because they lead to enlightenment, they are bojjhaṅgas; because they awaken, they are bojjhaṅgas; because they awaken accordingly, they are bojjhaṅgas; because they awaken completely, they are bojjhaṅgas," and so forth. Because the bojjhaṅga (enlightenment factor) is excellent or beautiful, it is sambojjhaṅga (perfect enlightenment factor). Thus, sati (mindfulness) itself is satisambojjhaṅga (mindfulness-enlightenment-factor). That is satisambojjhaṅgaṃ (mindfulness-enlightenment-factor). In this way, first, the ascertainment of just one initial term by way of the meaning, the characteristics, and so forth, should be understood.
Satisambojjhaṅgaṃ: Furthermore, in the case of the four noble truths, dhammavicayo is discriminating the truths.

Moreover, it has the characteristic of investigation, the taste of illumination, and the manifestation of non-confusion.

Vīriyaṃ: Because of the nature of heroism, and because it should be exerted methodically.

That has the characteristic of striving, the taste of support, and the manifestation of non-collapse.

Pīti: It pleases.

It has the characteristic of pervading, or the characteristic of satisfaction, the taste of pleasing body and mind, and the manifestation of the elation of those same [body and mind].

Passaddhi: Because of the calming of the agitation of body and mind.

It has the characteristic of tranquility, the taste of suppressing the agitation of body and mind, and the manifestation of the coolness that is the state of non-trembling of the mind.

Samādhi: Because of concentration.

It has the characteristic of non-distraction, or the characteristic of non-dispersion, the taste of unification of consciousness and mental factors, and the manifestation of the stability of mind.

Upekkhā: Because of equanimity.

It has the characteristic of reflection, or the characteristic of even-handedness, the taste of preventing deficiency and excess, or the taste of cutting off partiality, and the manifestation of the state of equilibrium.

The rest is as already stated.

In this way, the exposition of the remaining terms should be known in terms of their meaning, characteristics, and so on.

Dhammavicayo, vīriyaṃ, pīti, passaddhi, samādhi, upekkhā. Moreover, concerning the order (kamato), it is said, "Mindfulness, monks, I declare is always useful" (satiñca khvāhaṃ, bhikkhave, sabbatthikaṃ vadāmīti). Because mindfulness (satisambojjhaṅga) is helpful to all the remaining awakening factors, it is mentioned first.

After that, in accordance with the principle of "Thus, while living mindfully, he investigates that Dhamma with wisdom" (so tathā sato viharanto taṃ dhammaṃ paññāya pavicinatītiādinā), the purpose of the order of the remaining awakening factors has already been stated in the Sutta.

In this way, the explanation should also be understood in terms of its order.

Why, when the Blessed One stated the seven awakening factors, [did he say they were] neither deficient nor excessive (anūnādhikato ti kasmā pana bhagavatā satteva bojjhaṅgā vuttā anūnā anadhikāti)?

Because of being opposed to lethargy and agitation, and because of being universally applicable.

Here, three awakening factors are opposed to lethargy.

As it is said: "On an occasion, monks, when the mind becomes sluggish (yasmiñca kho, bhikkhave, samaye līnaṃ cittaṃ hoti), it is the right time to develop the awakening factor of discrimination of states, the awakening factor of energy, and the awakening factor of joy (dhammavicayasambojjhaṅgassa bhāvanāya, kālo vīriyasambojjhaṅgassa bhāvanāya, kālo pītisambojjhaṅgassa bhāvanāyā)."

Three are opposed to agitation.

As it is said: "On an occasion, monks, when the mind becomes agitated (yasmiñca kho, bhikkhave, samaye uddhataṃ cittaṃ hoti), it is the right time to develop the awakening factor of tranquility, the awakening factor of concentration, and the awakening factor of equanimity (passaddhisambojjhaṅgassa bhāvanāya, kālo samādhisambojjhaṅgassa bhāvanāya, kālo upekkhāsambojjhaṅgassa bhāvanāyā)."

But one here is universally applicable.

As it is said: "Mindfulness, monks, I declare is always useful" (satiñca khvāhaṃ, bhikkhave, sabbatthikaṃ vadāmīti).
"‘‘Sabbatthaka’’ntipi pāṭho," is also a reading, meaning that in both cases, "sabbattha" is intended.

Thus, because of being opposed to dullness and distraction, and because of being "sabbatthika" (applicable in all cases), only seven factors of enlightenment are mentioned, not less and not more. In this regard, the distinction between "anūnādhikato" (not less and not more) should be understood.

Having thus understood the meaning of only the first words of the seven factors mentioned in the method beginning with "‘‘satisambojjhaṅga’," now in "bhāveti vivekanissita," etc., it should be understood thus:

"Bhāveti" means develops, increases, repeatedly generates, brings into being in one's own mind-stream. This is the meaning.

"Vivekanissita" means dependent on detachment.

"Viveko" means separateness, distinction.

Now, this is fivefold: momentary detachment, detachment by suppression, detachment by eradication, detachment by tranquility, and detachment by escape.

Its variety should be understood by the method mentioned in "ariyadhamme avinīto."

This itself is what is stated there as discipline (vinaya).

Thus, in this fivefold detachment,

"Vivekanissita" means that the mindfulness enlightenment factor is developed dependent on momentary detachment, dependent on detachment by eradication, and dependent on detachment by escape. This meaning should be understood.

Thus, this yogi, engaged in developing the enlightenment factors, at the moment of insight, develops the mindfulness enlightenment factor dependent on momentary detachment in terms of function, dependent on detachment by escape in terms of intention, at the time of the path, dependent on detachment by eradication in terms of function, and dependent on detachment by escape in terms of object.

Some say "dependent on fivefold detachment." They highlight the enlightenment factors not only at the moment of powerful insight, path, and fruition, but also in the kasina jhānas that are the basis of insight, in mindfulness of breathing, in the unattractive, and in the brahma-vihāra jhānas.

And it is not refuted by the Aṭṭhakathā teachers.

Therefore, in their opinion, at the moment of the arising of these jhānas, it is dependent on detachment by suppression in terms of function.

And just as it is said "at the moment of insight, dependent on detachment by escape in terms of intention," so it is fitting to say that it also develops dependent on detachment by tranquility.

This method also applies to "virāganissitā" (dependent on detachment), and so on.

For detachment, etc., are in the sense of detachment.

Here, however, "vossaggo" (relinquishment) is twofold: relinquishment by abandonment (pariccāgavossaggo) and relinquishment by inclination (pakkhandanavossaggo).

There, "pariccāgavossaggo" means the abandoning of defilements at the moment of insight in terms of momentariness, and at the moment of the path in terms of eradication.

"Pakkhandanavossaggo" means inclining towards it at the moment of insight, and inclining towards nirvāṇa by making it the object at the moment of the path.

Both of these are suitable in this method of explaining the meaning mixed with the mundane and supramundane.

Thus, this mindfulness enlightenment factor, in the manner stated, abandons defilements and inclines towards nirvāṇa.

"Vossaggapariṇāmi" (maturing in relinquishment): by this entire statement, it means transforming into relinquishment, transformed, ripening, and ripened.

This is what is said: "Indeed, this bhikkhu, engaged in developing the enlightenment factors, just as the mindfulness enlightenment factor ripens into relinquishment by abandoning defilements and into relinquishment by inclining towards nirvāṇa, and just as it is ripened, so he develops it."

This method applies to the remaining enlightenment factors.

Here, however, only nirvāṇa is called "viveko" (detachment) because it is separate from all conditioned things, "virāgo" (dispassion) because of the state of dispassion of all, and "nirodho" (cessation) because of the state of cessation.

And only the path matures in relinquishment; therefore, the mindfulness enlightenment factor, having made detachment the object, is "vivekanissita" (dependent on detachment) because of its arising.

Similarly, "virāganissitaṃ nirodhanissitaṃ" (dependent on dispassion, dependent on cessation).
And that, with the arising of the moment of the noble path, due to the destruction of the defilements, and by way of relinquishment, and by way of inclining towards nirvāṇa, is transformed and matures; this meaning should be seen.

And so with the rest of the enlightenment factors (bojjhaṅga).

Discernment (viveko), dispassion (virāgo), cessation (nirodho), dependent on dispassion (virāganissitaṃ), dependent on cessation (nirodhanissitaṃ), whatever of these factors of enlightenment (bojjhaṅga) there may be.

The rest is as already stated.

Here, however, with the arising of the pollutants (āsava), because these enlightenment factors (bojjhaṅga) connected with the three higher paths have not been developed, whatever pollutants (āsava) might arise—the pollutant of sensual desire (kāmāsava), the pollutant of existence (bhavāsava), and the pollutant of ignorance (avijjāsava)—these three pollutants (āsava) do not exist for one who cultivates [the path] in this way; this method should be understood.

These are called... cultivating is to be abandoned (bhāvanā pahātabbā)—these three pollutants (āsava) are to be abandoned by this cultivation of the enlightenment factors (bojjhaṅga) connected with the three paths; thus it is said and should be understood.

These are called... cultivating is to be abandoned (bhāvanā pahātabbā).
