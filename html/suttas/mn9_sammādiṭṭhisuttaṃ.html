Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was staying at Savatthi in the Jetavana in Anathapindika’s park.

There the venerable Sāriputta addressed the bhikkhus thus: “Friends, bhikkhus.”

“Yes, friend,” the monks responded to venerable Sāriputta.

The venerable Sāriputta said this:

“‘Right view, right view’ is said, friends. In what way, friend, is a noble disciple one of right view, whose view is straight, who has perfect confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma?”

“Indeed, friend, we would come from far away to learn from the Venerable Sariputta the meaning of this statement. It would be good if the venerable Sariputta would explain the meaning of this statement. Having heard it from him the bhikkhus will remember it.”

“Then, friends, listen and attend closely to what I shall say.”

“Yes, friend,” the bhikkhus replied to the venerable Sāriputta.

The venerable Sāriputta said this:

“When, friends, a noble disciple understands the unwholesome (akuśala), the root of the unwholesome (akuśalamūla), the wholesome (kuśala), and the root of the wholesome (kuśalamūla)—in that way he is one of right view, whose view is straight, who has perfect confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma.

“And what, friend, is the unwholesome? What is the root of the unwholesome? What is the wholesome? What is the root of the wholesome? Killing living beings is unwholesome, taking what is not given is unwholesome, misconduct in sensual pleasures is unwholesome, false speech is unwholesome, malicious speech is unwholesome, harsh speech is unwholesome, gossip is unwholesome, covetousness (abhijjhā) is unwholesome, ill will (byāpādo) is unwholesome, wrong view (micchādiṭṭhi) is unwholesome. This is called the unwholesome.

“And what, friend, is the root of the unwholesome? Greed (lobho) is a root of the unwholesome, hate (doso) is a root of the unwholesome, delusion (moho) is a root of the unwholesome. This is called the root of the unwholesome.

“And what, friend, is the wholesome? Abstention from killing living beings is wholesome, abstention from taking what is not given is wholesome, abstention from misconduct in sensual pleasures is wholesome, abstention from false speech is wholesome, abstention from malicious speech is wholesome, abstention from harsh speech is wholesome, abstention from gossip is wholesome, non-covetousness (anabhijjhā) is wholesome, non-ill will (abyāpādo) is wholesome, right view (sammādiṭṭhi) is wholesome. This is called the wholesome.

“And what, friend, is the root of the wholesome? Non-greed (alobho) is a root of the wholesome, non-hate (adoso) is a root of the wholesome, non-delusion (amoho) is a root of the wholesome. This is called the root of the wholesome.

“When a noble disciple has thus understood the unwholesome, the root of the unwholesome, the wholesome, and the root of the wholesome, he entirely abandons the underlying tendency to lust, he abolishes the underlying tendency to aversion, he extirpates the underlying tendency to the view and conceit ‘I am,’ and by abandoning ignorance and arousing true knowledge he here and now makes an end of suffering. In that way too a noble disciple is one of right view, whose view is straight, who has perfect confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma.”

89. Thus I have heard: The Right View Discourse.

Therein, all such questions spoken by the Elder as “‘One of right view, one of right view’ is said, friends. In what way is a noble disciple one of right view. .. ?” or “And what, friends, is the unwholesome. .. ?” — these are questions showing a desire to expound.

Herein, since those who know, those who do not know, those outside the Dispensation, those within it, those who speak by hearsay, etc., and those who speak by personal knowledge, say “one of right view,” therefore, taking it as an expression to the many, he touched upon it twice, saying “One of right view, one of right view” is said, friends .

The intention here is this: “Others say ‘one of right view,’ and still others say ‘one of right view. ‘ Since that is said, in what way, friends, is a noble disciple one of right view in respect of meaning and characteristic?”

There, sammādiṭṭhi (samyagdṛṣṭi) means one possessing a beautiful and excellent view.

But when this word sammādiṭṭhi (samyagdṛṣṭi) applies to the Dhamma itself, then “beautiful and excellent view is sammādiṭṭhi (samyagdṛṣṭi)”—thus the meaning should be understood.

sammādiṭṭhi (samyagdṛṣṭi) sammādiṭṭhi (samyagdṛṣṭi)

And this right view (sammādiṭṭhi) is of two kinds: mundane (lokiyā) and transcendental (lokuttarā).

Herein, the knowledge of kamma as one’s own and knowledge which is in conformity with the Truths are mundane right view; or, in brief, all understanding that is accompanied by the taints.

Understanding connected with the noble paths and fruits is supramundane right view.

The person possessing right view is of three kinds: the worldling, the disciple in higher training, and the one beyond training.

Herein, the worldling is of two kinds: one outside the Dispensation and one within the Dispensation.

Herein, one outside the Dispensation who believes in kamma is one of right view on account of the view of kamma as one’s own, but not on account of that which is in conformity with the truths, because he holds to the view of self.

One within the Dispensation is of right view on account of both.

The disciple in higher training is one of right view on account of fixed right view.

the one beyond training on account of that is beyond training.

But here “one of right view” is intended as one possessing supramundane wholesome right view, which is fixed in destiny and emancipating.

Hence he said: whose view is straight, who has perfect confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma . Because of its going straight without deviating to either extreme, or because of its going straight by removing all crookedness such as bodily crookedness, etc., supramundane right view is “straight. “ One possessing that view also possesses perfect confidence, unshakable confidence, in the ninefold supramundane Dhamma. And by becoming disentangled from all the thickets of views, abandoning all defilements, emerging from the round of rebirths, completing the practice, he has arrived by the Noble Path at this true Dhamma (saddhamma), proclaimed by the Fully Enlightened One, which is an immersion in the Deathless, which is reckoned as Nibbāna.

Vādo is the communication (viññāpanaṃ) of that which is true (tacchato) from that which has come into being (bhūtato).
According to characteristic, musāvādo denotes the volition (cetanā) occasioning such an act of intimation (tathāviññattisamuṭṭhāpikā cetanā) in one who wishes to make another believe that an untrue situation (atathaṃ vatthuṃ) is true (tathato paraṃ viññāpetukāmassa).
This is less blameworthy (appasāvajjo) when the welfare that it destroys is slight (tassa appatāya), and more blameworthy (mahantatāya mahāsāvajjo) when the welfare is great.
Further, when it occurs on the part of householders (gahaṭṭhānaṃ) who, not wishing to give away some belonging of theirs (attano santakaṃ adātukāmatāya), say “I do not have it (natthītiādinayappavatto),” it is less blameworthy (appasāvajjo); when one who is a witness (sakkhinā hutvā) speaks for the purpose of destroying another’s welfare (atthabhañjanatthaṃ vutto), it is more blameworthy (mahāsāvajjo).
In the case of those gone forth (pabbajitānaṃ), when it occurs by their saying as a joke (hasādhippāyena), after they have obtained just a little oil or ghee (appakampi telaṃ vā sappiṃ vā labhitvā), in the manner of the Purāṇas, “Today the oil is flowing in the village just like a river (ajja gāme telaṃ nadīmaññe sandatī),” then it is less blameworthy (appasāvajjo); but for those who speak saying that they have seen what they have not seen (adiṭṭhaṃyeva pana diṭṭhantiādinā nayena vadantānaṃ), it is more blameworthy (mahāsāvajjo).
There are four constituents of this act (tassa cattāro sambhārā): an untrue situation (atathaṃ vatthu), the thought of deceiving (visaṃvādanacittaṃ), the corresponding effort (tajjo vāyāmo), and the communicating of that meaning to another (parassa tadatthavijānananti).
The means is single: one’s own person only (eko payogo sāhatthiko eva).
That is to be regarded as the performing of the action of deceiving another (para visaṃvādakakiriyākaraṇe daṭṭhabbo) by means of the body (kāyena vā) or by means of something attached to the body (kāyappaṭibaddhena vā) or by means of speech (vācāya vā).
If, through that action (tāya ce kiriyāya), the other understands that meaning (paro tamatthaṃ jānāti), one is bound by the kamma of false speech (musāvādakammunā bajjhati) at the very moment of the volition initiating the action (ayaṃ kiriyāsamuṭṭhāpikacetanākkhaṇeyeva).

In malicious speech (pisuṇā vācā) etc. (pisuṇā vācātiādīsu): The kind of speech (yāya vācāya) that creates in the heart (hadaye) of the person to whom it is spoken (yassa taṃ vācaṃ bhāsati) affection for oneself (attano piyabhāvaṃ) and voidness for another (parassa ca suññabhāvaṃ karoti) is malicious speech (sā pisuṇā vācā).
The kind of speech by which one makes both oneself and another harsh (attānampi parampi pharusampi karoti), the kind of speech which is also itself harsh (sā vācā sayampi pharusā), being pleasant neither to the ear nor to the heart (neva kaṇṇasukhā na hadayasukhā vā) — that is harsh speech (ayaṃ pharusā vācā).
That by which one babbles on senselessly, that is frivolous speech (samphappalāpo).
Also, the volition (cetanāpi) that is the root cause (mūlabhūtā) of these gains the name “malicious speech (pisuṇāvācādi-nāmaṃ eva labhati),” etc. And that only is intended here (sā eva ca idha adhippetāti).
Therein, malicious speech (pisuṇā vācā) is the volition (cetanā) of one with a defiled mind (saṃkiliṭṭhacittassa), which initiates an effort (kāyavacīpayogasamuṭṭhāpikā) by body or by speech either to cause division among others (paresaṃ vā bhedāya) or to endear oneself (attano piyakamyatāya vā).
The act is less blameworthy (appasāvajjā) when the person one divides is of inferior moral qualities (yassa bhedaṃ karoti, tassa appaguṇatāya), more blameworthy (mahāguṇatāya mahāsāvajjo) when he or she is of superior moral qualities.
Its constituents (tassā cattāro sambhārā) are four: another person to be divided (bhinditabbo paro), the intention to divide, “Thus these will be separated and split (iti ime nānā bhavissanti vinā bhavissantī),” or the desire to endear oneself, “Thus I shall become loved and intimate (ahaṃ piyo bhavissāmi vissāsiko),” the appropriate effort (tajjo vāyāmo), the communicating of that meaning to that person (tassa tadatthavijānananti).

Harsh speech (pharusā vācā) is the volition (cetanā) that is extremely harsh (ekantapharusā), occasioning bodily or vocal effort (kāyavacīpayogasamuṭṭhāpikā) that cuts another’s quick (parassa mammacchedaka).
This is a story to illustrate a point: It is said that a boy went to the forest without listening to his mother's words. Unable to make him turn back, she angrily scolded him, saying, "May a fierce buffalo chase you!"

Then, right there in the forest, a buffalo appeared before him.

The boy made a declaration of truth, saying, "Let it not be as my mother said with her mouth, but as she thought in her mind!"

The buffalo stood there as though tied.

Thus, even speech that wounds to the heart is not harsh speech if it arises from gentleness of mind.

For sometimes parents say to their children, "May robbers cut you to pieces!" yet they do not even want a lotus leaf to fall upon them.

And teachers and preceptors sometimes say to their pupils, "Why do these shameless and reckless ones wander about? Drive them out!"

Yet they desire their success in learning and attainment.

Just as, through gentleness of mind, speech is not harsh, so through gentleness of speech, speech does not become unharsh.

For the words "Let him sleep in peace" spoken by one wishing to kill are not unharsh speech.

But harsh speech is such on account of harshness of mind only.

It is less blameworthy when the person to whom it is spoken has few good qualities, and more blameworthy when such a one has great qualities.

Its constituents are three: another to be abused, an angry mind, the abusing.


An anattha-viññāpaka-kāya-vacī-payoga-samuṭṭhāpikā akusala-cetanā is gossip (samphappalāpo).

It is less blameworthy when indulged in mildly, and more blameworthy when indulged in strongly.

Its constituents are two: the being intent on purposeless stories such as the Bhārata war or the abduction of Sītā, etc., and the telling of such stories.


Gossip (samphappalāpo) Because it craves, it is covetousness (abhijjhā), turning towards another's goods and inclining towards them.

It has the characteristic of coveting others’ goods thus: “Oh, that this were mine!”

It is less blameworthy and more blameworthy as in the case of taking what is not given (adinnādāna).

Its constituents are two: another’s goods, and the inclination for them to be one’s own.

For even though greed has arisen based on another’s goods, it is not classed as a course of karma so long as one does not incline to them as one’s own, “Oh, that this were mine!”


Covetousness (abhijjhā), because it destroys the welfare and happiness of others, is ill will (byāpāda).

Its characteristic is the mental defect of wishing the destruction of others. It is less blameworthy and more blameworthy as in the case of harsh speech (pharusā vācā).

Its constituents are two: another being, and the wish for that being’s destruction.

For even though anger has arisen based on another being, there is no breach of a course of karma so long as one does not wish, “Oh, that this being might be cut off and destroyed!”


Ill will (byāpāda) Wrong view (micchādiṭṭhi) sees wrongly due to the absence of a correct grasp of things.

Its characteristic is the mistaken view that “there is no giving,” etc.

It is less blameworthy and more blameworthy as in the case of gossip (samphappalāpo).

Moreover, it is less blameworthy when not fixed in destiny, and more blameworthy when fixed.

Its constituents are two: a mistaken manner of grasping the basis , and the appearance of that in accordance with the manner in which it has been grasped.


Wrong view (micchādiṭṭhi) However, it should be understood that the non-virtuous karmic paths are defined in terms of these five aspects: from the perspective of the dharma, from the perspective of its class, from the perspective of its object, from the perspective of feeling, and from the perspective of its root.


There, From the perspective of the dharma (dhammato), of these, seven are volitional states (cetanā-dhammā), and three, beginning with covetousness (abhijjhā), are associated with volition (cetanā-sampayuttā).
In terms of classification (dhammato):

According to category (koṭṭhāsato): In order, seven, and wrong view - these eight are only courses of action (kammapatha), not roots. Covetousness and ill will are courses of action and also roots. For covetousness, having arrived at a root, is the unwholesome root greed. And ill will is the unwholesome root hate.

According to object (ārammaṇato): Killing living beings has formations as object with regard to the object of the life faculty. Taking what is not given has beings as object or formations as object. Misconduct in sensual pleasures has formations as object by way of tangible object. But some say it also has beings as object. False speech has beings or formations as object; likewise malicious speech. Harsh speech has only beings as object. Gossip has beings or formations as object by way of what is seen, heard, sensed, and cognized; likewise covetousness. Ill will has only beings as object. Wrong view has formations as object by way of the states belonging to the three planes.

According to feeling (vedanāto): Killing living beings has painful feeling. For although kings, seeing a robber, say laughingly, “Go and execute him,” their volition consummating the action is associated only with pain. Taking what is not given has three feelings. Misconduct has two feelings, pleasant and neutral, but in the mind which consummates the action there is no neutral feeling. False speech has three feelings; likewise malicious speech. Harsh speech has painful feeling only. Gossip has three feelings. Covetousness has two feelings, pleasant and neutral; likewise wrong view. Ill will has painful feeling only.

According to root (mūlato): Killing living beings has two roots, by way of hate and delusion. Taking what is not given, by way of hate and delusion or by way of greed and delusion. Misconduct [has two roots,] by way of greed and delusion. False speech, by way of hate and delusion or by way of greed and delusion; likewise malicious speech and gossip. Harsh speech, by way of hate and delusion. Covetousness has one root, by way of delusion; likewise ill will. Wrong view has two roots, by way of greed and delusion.

In greed is an unwholesome root, etc.:

Greed (lobho) is so called because it clings (lubbhati). Hate (doso) is so called because it is hostile (dussati). Delusion (moho) is so called because it is confusing (muyhati). Among these, greed is itself unwholesome because of being blameworthy and resulting in painful suffering, and because it is a root of these unwholesome actions, such as killing living beings, in the sense of being the associated cause for some, and in the sense of being the condition of dependence for others, [it is an] unwholesome root (akusalamūlaṃ).

This too is said: “One who is lustful, friends, overwhelmed and with mind obsessed by lust, kills a living being,” etc.
The same principle applies to the states of being unwholesome roots, namely, hatred (dosa) and delusion (moha).
Because of its despicable nature, it cuts off immoral behavior that has acquired such a designation; thus, it is kusala ("wholesome").

But since the question, "And what, friend, is kusala?" is not appropriate here, kusala is not stated.

Samuccheda-viratī is kusala.

Just as with unwholesome courses of action, the analysis of these wholesome courses of action should be understood in five ways: according to dharma, according to category, according to object, according to feeling, and according to root.

There, according to dharma, seven mental factors occur serially in these, and also abstinences.

The last three are only associated with mental factors.

According to dharma.

According to category, seven courses of action occur serially, but not roots.

The last three are courses of action and also roots.

For non-covetousness, having arrived at a root, is the wholesome root, non-greed (alobha).

Non-ill will (abyāpāda) is the wholesome root, non-hatred (adosa).

Right view (sammādiṭṭhi) is the wholesome root, non-delusion (amoha).

According to object, the objects of these are the same as the objects of killing living beings, etc. For abstention is spoken of in relation to something which can be transgressed.

But just as the noble path, which has Nibbāna as object, abandons the defilements, so too should these courses of kamma, which have the life faculty, etc., as object, be understood to abandon the kinds of evil conduct beginning with killing living beings.

According to feeling: all have pleasant feeling or neutral feeling.

For there is no painful feeling which arrives at the wholesome (kusala).

According to root: seven courses of action occur serially; for one who abstains by means of consciousness associated with knowledge, they have three roots by way of non-greed (alobha), non-hatred (adosa), and non-delusion (amoha).

They have two roots in one who abstains by means of consciousness dissociated from knowledge.

Non-covetousness has two roots in one who abstains by means of consciousness associated with knowledge.

[It] has one root by means of consciousness dissociated from knowledge.

However, non-greed is not its own root; the same method applies in the case of non-ill will.

Right view always has two roots, by way of non-greed and non-hatred.

In the expressions "alobho kusalamūla," etc., alobho [means] "non-greed," this is a designation for the dharma which is the opposite of greed.

The same method applies in the cases of non-hatred and non-delusion.

Among these, non-greed is itself wholesome (kusala); and it is a root of these wholesome qualities beginning with abstention from killing living beings, for some in the sense that it is an associated originative cause and for some in the sense that it is a decisive support condition. Thus it is a wholesome root (kusalamūla).

The same method applies to the state of being wholesome roots in the cases of non-hatred and non-delusion.

Alobho kusalamūla.

Now, to sum up what has all been taught concisely and at length, he says, "Because, friend," etc., beginning the application.

There, "evaṃ akusalaṃ pajānāti," thus, he knows the unwholesome in accordance with the ten unwholesome courses of action that have been described.

The same method applies in "evaṃ akusalamūla," etc.

Up to this point, by a single method, emancipation as far as arahantship has been expounded for one who has the Four Noble Truths as his meditation subject.

How? Here, the ten courses of unwholesome action, with the exception of covetousness, and the courses of wholesome action, are the truth of suffering (dukkha).

These two states — covetousness (abhijjhā) and the greed (lobha) which is a root of the unwholesome (akusalamūla) — are, literally speaking, the truth of the origin (samudaya).

Speaking figuratively, however, all the courses of kamma are the truth of suffering.

All the wholesome (kusala) and unwholesome roots are the truth of the origin.

The non-occurrence of both is the truth of cessation (nirodha).
Understanding suffering, abandoning its origin, understanding its cessation, the noble path is the truth of the path. Thus, two truths are stated in their own nature, and two are to be understood by way of the guideline of conversion.

"Good, friends," those monks, having delighted in and approved the words spoken by the venerable Śāriputra, asked the venerable Śāriputra a further question: "But, friends, might there be another way in which a noble disciple is of right view, his view is straight, he is endowed with unwavering confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma?"

"There might be, friends. When, friends, a noble disciple understands nutriment, the origin of nutriment, the cessation of nutriment, and the way leading to the cessation of nutriment, in that way he is one of right view, his view is straight, he is endowed with unwavering confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma. But what, friends, is nutriment, what is the origin of nutriment, what is the cessation of nutriment, and what is the way leading to the cessation of nutriment? There are these four nutriments, friends, for the maintenance of beings that already exist or for the support of those seeking to be born. What are the four? They are: physical food (kabaḷīkāro āhāro), gross or subtle; contact (phasso) as the second; mental volition (manosañcetanā) as the third; and consciousness (viññāṇaṃ) as the fourth. With the arising of craving (taṇhā) there is the arising of nutriment. With the cessation of craving there is the cessation of nutriment. The way leading to the cessation of nutriment is just this Noble Eightfold Path (ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo); that is, right view (sammādiṭṭhi), right intention (sammāsaṅkappo), right speech (sammāvācā), right action (sammākammanto), right livelihood (sammāājīvo), right effort (sammāvāyāmo), right mindfulness (sammāsati), and right concentration (sammāsamādhi)."

"When a noble disciple has thus understood nutriment, thus understood the origin of nutriment, thus understood the cessation of nutriment, and thus understood the way leading to the cessation of nutriment, he entirely abandons the underlying tendency to greed (rāgānusayaṃ), he eliminates the underlying tendency to aversion (paṭighānusayaṃ), he eradicates the underlying tendency to the view and conceit ‘I am’ (diṭṭhimānānusayaṃ), and by abandoning ignorance (avijjaṃ) and arousing true knowledge (vijjaṃ) he here and now makes an end of suffering (dukkhassantakaro hoti). In that way too a noble disciple is one of right view, whose view is straight, who has perfect confidence in the Dhamma, and has arrived at this true Dhamma."

Saccavāravaṇṇanā
Saying, “Good, friend, and has arrived at this true Dhamma”: Thus, having heard the Venerable Sāriputta’s exposition of the four truths under the heading of the wholesome and the unwholesome, the bhikkhus delighted in his words with the statement, “Good, friend,” and rejoiced with the mind that aroused that statement; what is meant is that they agreed by word and approved by mind.

Now, since the elder was capable of expounding the four truths in various ways, as he said, “Sāriputta, monks, is able to explain and expound the four noble truths in detail,” and since he was willing to explain further, he said, “Even to this extent.” Therefore, desiring to hear the exposition of the truth in another way, those bhikkhus asked the Venerable Sāriputta a further question.

Here it is said, if the meaning of condition (paccayaṭṭho) is the meaning of nutriment (āhāraṭṭho), then why, when conditions exist for other beings too, are just these four stated? It is said, because of the internal continuum…
Because of its specific conditionality.

For physical nutriment is the specific condition for the material body of beings that eat physical nutriment; as regards the group of mental constituents, contact is for feeling, mental volition for consciousness, and consciousness for mentality-materiality.

As it was said:

“Just as, bhikkhus, this body has nutriment for its maintenance, is maintained in dependence on nutriment, and without nutriment is not maintained.”

and likewise: “With contact as condition, feeling;… with formations as condition, consciousness;. .. with consciousness as condition, mentality-materiality” .

What here is nutriment, what does it nourish? Physical nutriment nourishes the materiality with nutritive essence as eighth. Contact as nutriment nourishes the three feelings; mental volition as nutriment nourishes the three kinds of being; consciousness as nutriment nourishes the mentality-materiality of rebirth-linking.

How? Physical nutriment, as soon as it is placed in the mouth, brings into being eight kinds of materiality. Then each lump of cooked rice ground by the teeth, on being swallowed, brings into being unit after unit of the eight kinds of materiality. Thus it nourishes the materiality with nutritive essence as eighth.

But with contact as nutriment, when contact productive of pleasant feeling arises it nourishes pleasant feeling; contact productive of painful feeling nourishes painful feeling; contact productive of neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling nourishes neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. Thus in all ways contact as nutriment nourishes the three kinds of feeling.

In the case of mental volition as nutriment, kamma leading to sense-sphere being nourishes sense-sphere being; kamma leading to fine-material and immaterial being nourishes its respective kind of being. Thus in all ways mental volition as nutriment nourishes the three kinds of being.

But with consciousness as nutriment, it is said that it nourishes, by way of conascence condition, etc., the three aggregates associated with itself at the moment of rebirth-linking and the thirty kinds of materiality that arise by way of triple continuity. Thus consciousness nourishes the mentality-materiality of rebirth-linking.

And here, by the words “mental volition as nutriment nourishes the three kinds of being,” only the wholesome and unwholesome volition accompanied by taints is meant. By the words "Consciousness nourishes the mentality-materiality of rebirth-linking," only rebirth-linking consciousness is meant. However, because of nourishing the associated and originated phenomena without distinction, those should be understood as nutriments.

As regards these four kinds of nutriment, physical food as nutriment accomplishes the function of nutriment by sustaining, contact by contacting , mental volition by accumulating, consciousness by cognizing.

How? Physical nutriment, by merely sustaining, is “for the maintenance of beings” by establishing the body. For this body, though generated by kamma, is sustained by physical food and stands for ten years or a hundred years up to the end of the life-span. Like what? Like a child which, though given birth by the mother, is nurtured by the milk, etc., given to him to drink by the wet-nurse and thus lives long. Also, as a house is supported by a prop. And this was said:

“Great king, just as, when a house is collapsing, they prop it up with other timber, and that house, being propped up by other timber, does not collapse, so too this body is supported by nutriment, persists in dependence upon nutriment.”

Thus physical nutriment accomplishes the function of nutriment by sustaining. Accomplishing it thus, physical food as nutriment becomes a condition for two material continuities, namely, for that originated by nutriment and that kammically acquired. It is a condition for the kamma-born materiality by becoming its preserver. It is a condition for that originated by nutriment by becoming its producer.

Then contact, by contacting the object which is the basis for pleasure, etc., is “for the maintenance of beings” by causing the occurrence of pleasant feeling, etc. Mental volition, accumulating by way of wholesome and unwholesome kamma, is “for the maintenance of beings” because it provides the root of existence. Consciousness, by cognizing, is “for the maintenance of beings” by causing the occurrence of mentality-materiality.
Thus, when these are accomplishing the function of nutriment by way of supporting, etc., four dangers should be seen.

That is to say: in the case of physical food as nutriment, desire (nikanti) itself is the danger; in the case of contact, approach (upagamana) itself; in the case of mental volition, accumulation (āyūhana) itself; and in the case of consciousness, launching (abhinipāta) itself is the danger.

What is the reason? Because, having aroused desire for physical food, beings, facing cold, etc., to undertake such work as checking, accounting, etc., for the sake of nutriment, incur not a little suffering.

And some who have gone forth in this dispensation seek nutriment through such improper means as the practice of medicine, etc., and they are to be censured here and now.

And hereafter they become “recluse ghosts” in the manner described thus in the Lakkhaṇa Saṃyutta: “And his outer robe was burning, blazing,” etc.

For this reason alone, desire itself is to be understood as the danger in physical food as nutriment.

Those who approach contact, who find gratification in contact, commit crimes in respect of others’ guarded and protected belongings, such as their wives, etc.

When the owners of the goods catch them with their belongings, they cut them into pieces or throw them onto a rubbish heap.

Or hand them over to the king;

and then the king has various tortures inflicted upon them.

And with the breakup of the body, after death, a bad destination is to be expected for them.

Thus this entire danger—that pertaining to the here and now and that pertaining to the afterlife—has come about rooted in contact.

For this reason, approach is to be understood as the danger in the nutriment contact.

But through the accumulation of wholesome and unwholesome action alone, all the danger rooted in that in the three realms has come about.

For this reason, accumulation is to be understood as the danger in the nutriment mental volition.

And in whatever place rebirth-linking consciousness launches, in that same place it is reborn by seizing the rebirth-linking mentality-materiality. When it is produced, all dangers are produced, for they are all rooted in it. For this reason, launching is to be understood as the danger in the nutriment consciousness.

The boy died from going back and forth between the father and the mother.

Seeing him dead, they wept, and having taken the flesh as described above, they departed.

Because that flesh of their son was repulsive to them for nine reasons, it was not eaten for enjoyment nor for intoxication nor for making strong and beautiful, but only for the purpose of crossing out of the desert.

If one should ask, “What are the nine reasons for it being repulsive?” [They are:] Because it was flesh of their own kind, flesh of a relative, flesh of a son, flesh of a beloved son, tender flesh, raw flesh, unseasoned flesh, unsalted flesh, and unsmoked flesh.

Therefore the bhikkhu who sees the nutriment physical food thus, as similar to son’s flesh, eliminates the desire for it.

This, in the first place, is the interpretation of the meaning of the simile of son’s flesh (puttamaṃsūpamāyaṃ).

Then, as regards the simile of the flayed cow (niccammagāvūpamāyaṃ): If a cow were stripped of its skin from the neck to the hooves and then set free, whatever it would rest upon would become a basis of pain for it, since it would be bitten by the small creatures living there. So too, whatever physical basis or object contact stands upon as its support becomes a basis for the felt pain originating from that basis or object.

Therefore the bhikkhu who sees the nutriment contact thus, as similar to a flayed cow, eliminates the desire for it. This is the interpretation of the meaning of the simile of the flayed cow (niccammagāvūpamāyaṃ).

Then, as regards the simile of the charcoal pit (aṅgārakāsūpamāyaṃ): Just as that charcoal pit [is full of burning embers], so are the three realms of existence full of great burning. Like the two men who grab hold by both his arms and drag him towards it, is mental volition in the sense that it drags one towards the realms of being.

Therefore a bhikkhu who sees the nutriment mental volition thus, as similar to a charcoal pit and men dragging [one to it], eliminates the desire for it. This is the interpretation of the meaning of the simile of the charcoal pit (aṅgārakāsūpamāyaṃ).

Then, as regards the simile of one struck by three hundred spears (sattisatāhatūpamāyaṃ): If a man is struck by three hundred spears in the early morning, making a hundred wound openings in his body, and those spears neither remain inside nor pass right through, but fall to the ground at once, and in the same way another two hundred spears [strike him]; and thus, in the place where [the first spears] fell, since they did not remain inside but passed through, the whole body becomes riddled with holes, there is no measure for the suffering which arises from even one wound opening, what then to say of three hundred wound openings? The time of the spears falling is like the time of the arising of rebirth-linking consciousness.

The production of the aggregates is like the production of the wound openings.

The arising of the various kinds of suffering rooted in the round once the aggregates have been born is like the arising of suffering on account of the wound openings.

Another method of interpretation: The rebirth-linking consciousness is like the thief.

His mentality-materiality conditioned by consciousness is like the wound openings created by the striking of the spears.

The arising of severe pain for that man conditioned by the wound openings is like the arising of the various kinds of suffering by way of the thirty-two types of torture and the eighty-nine types of diseases in regard to consciousness conditioned by mentality-materiality.

Therefore a bhikkhu who sees the nutriment consciousness thus, as similar to one struck by three hundred spears, eliminates the desire for it. This is the interpretation of the meaning of the simile of one struck by three hundred spears (sattisatāhatūpamāyaṃ).

Thus by eliminating desire in regard to these nutriments, he also fully understands these four nutriments. When these have been fully understood, the entire basis has also been fully understood.

The arising of nutriment is the arising of craving (taṇhāsamudayā āhārasamudayo): The meaning is that the arising of the nutriments of rebirth-linking (paṭisandhi) is produced by the arising of craving (taṇhā) in the previous existence.

How [is this so]? Because at the moment of rebirth-linking (paṭisandhikkhaṇe) there is the nutritive essence (ojā) produced among the thirty types of materiality (rūpa) that have arisen by way of triple continuity.

This is the kammically acquired physical food as nutriment (upādinnakakabaḷīkārāhāra) produced by craving (taṇhā) as its condition (paccaya).

But the contact (phassa) and volition (cetanā) associated with the rebirth-linking consciousness (paṭisandhicitta), and that mind (citta) or consciousness (viññāṇa) itself — these are the kammically acquired nutriments (upādinnaka) of contact (phassa), mental volition (manosañcetanā) and consciousness (viññāṇāhāra) produced by craving (taṇhā) as their condition (paccaya).

Thus, in the first place, the arising of the nutriments at rebirth-linking (paṭisandhi) should be understood (veditabbo) as occurring with the arising of craving (taṇhā) in the previous existence.

But because the nutriments that are kammically acquired (upādinnaka) and those that are not kammically acquired (anupādinnaka) have been discussed here combined, the arising of nutriment with the arising of craving (taṇhāsamudayā āhārasamudayo) should be understood (veditabbo) to apply also to those that are not kammically acquired (anupādinnaka).

For there is nutritive essence (ojā) in the kinds of materiality (rūpa) that are aroused by the eight types of consciousness (citta) accompanied by greed (lobha); this is the nutriment physical food (kabaḷīkārāhāra) that is not kammically acquired (anupādinnaka) yet is produced by conascent craving (sahajātataṇhā) as its condition (paccaya).

But the contact (phassa) and volition (cetanā) associated with the consciousness (citta) accompanied by greed (lobha), and that mind (citta) or consciousness (viññāṇa) itself — these are the nutriments of contact (phassa), mental volition (manosañcetanā) and consciousness (viññāṇāhāra) that are not kammically acquired (anupādinnaka) yet are produced by craving (taṇhā) as their condition (paccaya).

The arising of nutriment is the arising of craving (taṇhāsamudayā āhārasamudayo).

The cessation of nutriment is the cessation of craving (taṇhānirodhā āhārasamudayo): Through the cessation (nirodha) of craving (taṇhā) that is the condition (paccaya) for the nutriments that are kammically acquired (upādinnaka) and those that are not kammically acquired (anupādinnaka), the cessation of nutriment (āhāra-nirodho) is known (paññāyati).

92. From here onwards the teaching is given by way of dependent arising.

In that context, concerning aging and death, “tesaṃ tesaṃ” should be understood as a collective designation in brief for the many kinds of beings.

For if one were to state the aging of Devadatta, the aging of Somadatta, etc., one would never come to an end of beings.

But there is no being not included by these two terms.

Therefore it was said above: “This should be understood as a collective designation in brief for the many kinds of beings.”

“tamhi tamhī” is a collective designation for the many nikāyas by way of their kinds of existence.

"sattanikāye" is an illustration of the form of what is designated by the collective designation.

Among "jarā jīraṇatā," "jarā" is a description of the nature.

"jīraṇatā" is a description of the aspect.

"khaṇḍicca" and the like are descriptions of the function in the course of time.

The last two are descriptions of the normal.

This "jarā" is indicated as to its nature by this term, hence this is a description of its nature.

"jīraṇatā" is in terms of its appearance.

Hence this is a description of its aspect.

"khaṇḍicca" is by means of the function of causing the state of brokenness of teeth and nails in the course of time.

"pālicca" is by means of the function of causing the state of greyness of the hairs of the head and body.

"valittacatā" is explained by means of the function of causing the state of wrinkling of the skin by softening the flesh.

Hence these three, "khaṇḍicca," etc., are descriptions of the function in the course of time.

By these, evident aging is shown, which becomes evident by the showing of these alterations.

For just as the course taken by water or wind or fire is evident from the damaged and broken state, or the burnt state, of the grass and trees, etc., and yet the course that has been taken is not the water, etc., itself, so too the course taken by aging is evident through brokenness of teeth, etc., and it is apprehended by opening the eyes.

but the brokenness, etc., themselves are not aging, nor is aging cognizable by the eye.

"tamhi tamhī sattanikāye jarā jīraṇatā jarā jīraṇatā khaṇḍicca jarā jīraṇatā khaṇḍicca pālicca valittacatā khaṇḍicca"

"āyuno saṃhāni indriyānaṃ paripāko" are explained by the natural characteristic designated by the expressions of the decay of life and the decay of the eye and other faculties, in the course of time.

Hence these last two are to be understood as descriptions of its normal.

"āyuno saṃhāni indriyānaṃ paripāko"

Therein, since the life of one who has reached aging diminishes, therefore aging is called “decay of life” metaphorically.

And because the eye faculty, etc. — which at the time of youth were quite clear and could easily grasp even subtle objects — become deficient, obscure, unable to grasp even gross objects when one has reached old age, therefore it is called “weakness of faculties” also as a metaphor of its effect.

This aging, thus described, is all of two kinds, evident and concealed.

Therein, the aging of material phenomena, shown by brokenness, etc., is called evident aging.

But the aging of immaterial phenomena, due to the invisibility of such an alteration, is called concealed aging.

Therein, the brokenness that is seen is simply color because of the ease of comprehending such things as the teeth, etc. Having seen this with the eye and reflected on it with the mind door, one knows aging thus: “These teeth have been afflicted by aging,” just as one knows the existence of water below when one has noticed the heads of cows, etc., bound to the place where the water is located.

Again, aging is twofold thus: as continuous and as discrete.

Therein, continuous aging is the aging of such things as gems, gold, silver, coral, the sun and moon, etc.; it is so called because of the difficulty of perceiving in such things distinct changes in color, etc., at regular intervals, as we can in the case of living beings passing through the decade of childhood, etc., and in the case of vegetation such as flowers, fruits, buds, etc. The meaning is: aging that progresses without interval.
Furthermore, discrete aging (savīcijarā) should be understood as the aging of the things other than those, i. e., of the aforesaid things; it is so called because it is easy to perceive in them distinct changes in color, etc., at regular intervals.

Manifest aging (pākaṭajarā), concealed aging (paṭicchannajarā), uninterrupted aging (avīcijarā), discrete aging (savīcijarā).

Hereafter, "of those, of those," etc., should be understood in the manner already stated.

Then, in the expression decay, disintegration, etc., decay (cuti) is said by way of what has the nature to decay; this is a collective designation to one-, four-, and five-aggregate.

Disintegration (cavanatā) indicates the characteristic by way of the designation as a state.

Dissolution (bhedo) is the exposition of the breaking and arising of the decaying aggregates.

Disappearance (antaradhāna) is the exposition, by whatever way, of the non-existence of a place for the decaying aggregates that are broken, just like a broken pot.

Dying (maccu), death (maraṇa) is death which is called dying (maccusaṅkhātaṃ maraṇaṃ).

By this he rejects the idea of death as complete annihilation (samucchedamaraṇa), etc.

Time (kālo) is the end-maker; the action of that is the action of time (kālakiriyā).

By this he explains death in conventional terminology.

102. In the section on formations, saṅkhāra is defined by the characteristic of forming (abhisaṅkharaṇalakkhaṇo saṅkhāro).

However, in the detailed section, kāyasaṅkhāro ("body formation") means a formation that proceeds from the body. This is a designation (adhivacanaṃ) for the twenty body-volitions (kāyasañcetanānam) that occur by way of activating the bodily door: eight wholesome volitions related to the desire realm (kāmāvacarakusalato aṭṭhannaṃ), and twelve unwholesome ones (akusalato dvādasannanti).

Vacīsaṅkhāro ("verbal formation") means a formation that proceeds from speech. This is a designation for the same twenty verbal volitions (vacīsañcetanānam) that occur by way of distinctions of speech through the verbal door.

Cittasaṅkhāro ("mental formation") means a formation that proceeds from the mind. This is a designation for the twenty-nine mental volitions (manosañcetanānam) that occur when one sits alone in thought, without activating the bodily and verbal doors, in terms of mundane wholesome and unwholesome states (lokiyakusalākusalavasena).

Furthermore, regarding avijjāsamudayā ("the arising of ignorance"), it should be understood that ignorance is a condition (avijjāpaccayo hoti) for wholesome states by way of being a proximate condition (upanissayavasena), and also for unwholesome states by way of being a co-arisen condition (sahajātādivasena).

103. In the section on ignorance ( avijjā ): “dukkhe aññāṇaṃ”—aññāṇaṃ in the truth of suffering (duḥkhasatya), is an epithet for delusion (moha).

This is the method for the samudaye aññāṇaṃ, and so forth.

Herein, not knowing about suffering (duḥkha) should be understood in four ways: as to containment (antogadha), as to physical basis (vatthu), as to object (ārammaṇa), and as to concealment (paṭicchādana).

Thus, because of being included in the truth of suffering (duḥkhasatya), it is contained in suffering; and the truth of suffering is its physical basis by being its support condition (nissayapaccayabhāva); and is its object by being its object condition (ārammaṇapaccayabhāva); and it conceals the truth of suffering by preventing the penetration of its real characteristic (yāthāvalakkhaṇa) and by not allowing knowledge (ñāṇa) to occur in regard to it.

Not knowing about the origin (samudaya) should be understood in three ways: as to physical basis (vatthu), as to object (ārammaṇa), and as to concealment (paṭicchādana).

And not knowing about cessation (nirodha) and the way (paṭipadā) should be understood in one way only: as to concealment (paṭicchādana).

For non-knowledge only conceals cessation and the way by preventing the penetration of their real characteristics (yāthāvalakkhaṇa) and by not allowing knowledge (ñāṇa) to occur in regard to them.

But it is not contained in them because it is not included in this pair of truths (saccadvaya).

And these two truths (saccadvaya) are not its physical basis (vatthu) because they are not conascent (asahajāta).

Nor are they its object (ārammaṇa) because of its non-occurrence on account of them.

For the last pair of truths (saccadvaya) are difficult to see because of their profundity (gambhīra), and non-knowledge, which is blind (andhabhūtaṃ aññāṇaṃ), does not occur there.

But the first is profound (gambhīraṃ) in the sense of opposition because of the difficulty in seeing the characteristic of their intrinsic nature (sabhāvalakkhaṇassa); it occurs there by way of obsession by the perversions (vipallāsa).

Moreover, by "dukkhe," ignorance (avijjā) is shown in brief, by way of physical basis (vatthu), by way of object (ārammaṇa), and by way of function (kicca).

By "dukkhasamudaye," by way of physical basis (vatthu), by way of object (ārammaṇa), and by way of function (kicca).

By "dukkhanirodhe dukkhanirodhagāminiyā paṭipadāyā," by way of function (kicca).

But it should be known that, without differentiation (avisesato), by "aññāṇa," it is not designated by way of intrinsic nature (sabhāvato).

By "āsavasamudayā," here, the taints (āsava) of sensual desire (kāma) and existence (bhava) are conditions (paccaya) for ignorance (avijjā) by way of being co-arisen (sahajāta), and so forth.

The taint (āsava) of ignorance (avijjā), only by way of decisive support (upanissayavasena).

Here, the previously arisen ignorance (avijjā) and taint of ignorance (avijjāsava) should be understood.

That is a decisive support condition (upanissayapaccayo) for the ignorance that arises subsequently (aparāparuppannāya avijjāya).

In the section on the taints, regarding "with the arising of ignorance ( avijjāsamudayā)," here, ignorance is a condition for the taint of sense-desire ( kāmāsava) and the taint of existence ( bhavāsava) by way of being co-nascent, etc. For the taint of ignorance ( avijjāsavassa) only by way of decisive support. And here, the ignorance that arises subsequently should be understood as the taint of ignorance ( avijjāsavo). The previously arisen ignorance itself becomes a decisive support condition for the subsequently arisen taint of ignorance ( avijjāsavassa upanissayapaccayo). The rest is as already stated. This section is stated by way of showing the condition for the ignorance which heads the factors of dependent arising. Stated thus, the undiscoverability of any beginning of saṃsāra is established. How? Because with the arising of the taints there is the arising of ignorance ( āsavasamudayena hi avijjāsamudayo), and with the arising of ignorance there is the arising of the taints (avijjāsamudayenāpi āsavasamudayo). Having shown this, no first point of ignorance is manifest, and because none is manifest the undiscoverability of any beginning of saṃsāra is proven.

Thus in all this sutta sixteen sections (soḷasavārā) have been stated: the section on the courses of kamma, the section on nutriment, the section on suffering, and the sections on aging and death, birth, being, clinging, craving, feeling, contact, the sixfold base, mentality-materiality, consciousness, formations, ignorance and the taints.

As to these sixteen sections (soḷasavārā), in each individual section there is a twofold analysis — in brief and in detail — amounting to thirty-two cases. Thus in this sutta, in these thirty-two cases, the Four Truths (cattāri saccāni) are expounded. Among these, in the sixteen cases stated in detail, arahattaṃ (arahantship) is expounded. But according to the opinion of the Elder, the four truths and the four paths are expounded in the thirty-two cases. Thus in the entire Word of the Buddha comprised in the five great Nikayas, there is no sutta except for this Discourse on Right View (sammādiṭṭhisutta) where the Four Truths are explained thirty-two times and where arahattaṃ (arahantship) is explained thirty-two times.

That is what the Venerable (āyasmā) Sāriputta (sāriputto) said : The Venerable (āyasmā) Sāriputta (sāriputto) spoke this Discourse on Right View (sammādiṭṭhisutta), having adorned it with sixty-four divisions — thirty-two expositions of the four truths (catusaccapariyāyehi) and thirty-two expositions of arahantship (arahattapariyāyehi). The bhikkhus were satisfied and delighted in the Venerable Sāriputta’s words.

The commentary on the section on the taints is finished.

The commentary on the Sammādiṭṭhisutta in the Papañcasūdanī, commentary to the Majjhimanikāya, is finished.
