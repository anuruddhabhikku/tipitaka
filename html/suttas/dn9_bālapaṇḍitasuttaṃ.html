246. Thus have I heard. Once the Blessed One (Bhagavā) was dwelling at Sāvatthī (Śrāvastī) in Jeta's Grove (Jetavana), Anāthapindika's Park (Anāthapiṇḍikassa ārāme).

There the Blessed One addressed the monks (bhikkhus), saying: "Monks (bhikkhavo)!"

"Venerable Sir (bhadante)," those monks (bhikkhus) replied to the Blessed One (Bhagavato).

The Blessed One (Bhagavā) said this:

"These three, monks (bhikkhave), are the characteristics of a fool (bālassa bālalakkhaṇāni), the signs of a fool (bālanimittāni), the actions of a fool (bālāpadānāni).

Which three? Here, monks (bhikkhave), a fool is one who thinks bad thoughts (duccintitacintī), speaks bad words (dubbhāsitabhāsī), and does bad deeds (dukkaṭakammakārī).

If, monks (bhikkhave), a fool were not one who thinks bad thoughts (duccintitacintī), speaks bad words (dubbhāsitabhāsī), and does bad deeds (dukkaṭakammakārī), then how would the wise (paṇḍitā) know him: 'This person is a fool (bālo), an untrue man (asappuriso)'? But because, monks (bhikkhave), a fool is one who thinks bad thoughts (duccintitacintī), speaks bad words (dubbhāsitabhāsī), and does bad deeds (dukkaṭakammakārī), therefore the wise (paṇḍitā) know him: 'This person is a fool (bālo), an untrue man (asappuriso)'.

That fool (bālo), monks (bhikkhave), experiences threefold suffering (dukkhaṃ) and grief (domanassaṃ) here and now (diṭṭheva dhamme).

If, monks (bhikkhave), a fool (bālo) is seated in an assembly (sabhāyaṃ) or along a street (rathikāya) or in a square (siṅghāṭake), and people there are discussing certain pertinent and relevant matters (tajjaṃ tassāruppaṃ kathaṃ manteti).

If, monks (bhikkhave), a fool (bālo) is one who kills living beings (pāṇātipātī), takes what is not given (adinnādāyī), misconducts himself in sensual pleasures (kāmesumicchācārī), speaks falsehood (musāvādī), and indulges in wine, liquor, and intoxicants, which are the basis of negligence (surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhāyī), then, monks (bhikkhave), the fool (bālassa) thinks: 'These people are discussing certain pertinent and relevant matters (tajjaṃ tassāruppaṃ kathaṃ manteti); these things are found in me (dhammā mayi), and I am seen engaging in those things (tesu dhammesu sandissāmī)'.

This, monks (bhikkhave), is the first kind of suffering (dukkhaṃ) and grief (domanassaṃ) that a fool (bālo) experiences here and now (diṭṭheva dhamme)."

“Again, monks, when a fool is sitting on a chair or a bed, or resting on the ground, the evil deeds done by him in the past – misconduct of body (kāyena duccaritaṃ), speech (vācāya duccaritaṃ), and mind (manasā duccaritaṃ) – cover him, overwhelm him, and engulf him.

Just as, monks, the shadow of a great mountain peak in the evening covers, overwhelms, and engulfs the earth, so too, monks, when a fool is sitting on a chair or a bed, or resting on the ground, the evil deeds done by him in the past – misconduct of body, speech, and mind – cover him, overwhelm him, and engulf him.

There, monks, the fool thinks: ‘I have not done what is good (akataṃ kalyāṇaṃ), I have not done what is wholesome (akataṃ kusalaṃ), I have not made myself a shelter from fear (akataṃ bhīruttāṇaṃ); I have done what is evil (kataṃ pāpaṃ), I have done what is cruel (kataṃ luddaṃ), I have done what is wicked (kataṃ kibbisaṃ).’

‘When I pass away, I shall go to the destination of those who have not done what is good, have not done what is wholesome, have not made themselves a shelter from fear, have done what is evil, have done what is cruel, have done what is wicked.’

He sorrows, grieves, and laments, he weeps beating his breast and becomes distraught.

This is the third kind of pain and grief that a fool feels here and now.

“Furthermore, monks, a fool, having practiced misconduct with the body (kāyena duccaritaṃ caritvā), having practiced misconduct with speech (vācāya duccaritaṃ caritvā), having practiced misconduct with the mind (manasā duccaritaṃ caritvā), upon the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (maraṇā), is reborn in the plane of misery (apāyaṃ), in a bad destination (duggatiṃ), in a lower world (vinipātaṃ), in hell (nirayaṃ upapajjati).

Monks, if one were rightly to say of anything, 'It is utterly unwished for, utterly undesired, utterly disagreeable,' it is of hell that one would rightly say, 'It is utterly unwished for, utterly undesired, utterly disagreeable.'

Monks, it is not easy to find a simile for the suffering in hell."

When this was said, a certain bhikkhu said this to the Blessed One: “Is it possible, bhante, to make a simile?”

“It is possible, bhikkhus,” the Blessed One said.

“Suppose, bhikkhu, men caught a robber culprit and presented him to the king, saying: ‘Sire, here is a robber culprit. Order what punishment you will for him.’

The king says to them: ‘Go, men, in the morning strike this man with a hundred spears.’

And they struck him in the morning with a hundred spears.

Then the king says at noon: ‘Sirs, how is that man?’ ‘Sire, he is still alive.’

Then the king said: ‘Go and strike that man at noon with a hundred spears.’

And they struck him at noon with a hundred spears.

Then the king says in the evening: ‘Sirs, how is that man?’ ‘Sire, he is still alive.’

Then the king said: ‘Go and strike that man in the evening with a hundred spears.’

In the evening they strike him with a hundred spears.

What do you think, bhikkhus, if that man were struck with three hundred spears, would he experience pain and grief because of that?”

“Venerable sir, that man would experience pain and grief because of being struck with even one spear, let alone three hundred.”

Atha kho taṃ, bhikkhave, gahapatiratanaṃ ubhohi hatthehi udake omasitvā pūraṃ hiraññasuvaṇṇassa kumbhiṃ uddharitvā rājānaṃ cakkavattiṃ etadavoca – ‘alamettāvatā, mahārāja! Katamettāvatā, mahārāja! Pūjitamettāvatā, mahārājā’ti. Rājā cakkavattī evamāha – ‘alamettāvatā, gahapati! Katamettāvatā, gahapati! Pūjitamettāvatā, gahapatī’ti <pb ed="M" n="3.0214"/>. Rañño, bhikkhave, cakkavattissa evarūpaṃ gahapatiratanaṃ pātubhavati.

<note>dhanena karaṇīyaṃ (ka.)</note> <note>ogahetvā (sī. pī.)</note> <pb ed="M" n="3.0214"/> <p rend="bodytext">‘‘Puna caparaṃ, bhikkhave, rañño cakkavattissa pariṇāyakaratanaṃ <pb ed="P" n="3.0176"/> pātubhavati – paṇḍito byatto medhāvī paṭibalo rājānaṃ cakkavattiṃ upayāpetabbaṃ upayāpetuṃ <note>upaṭṭhapetabbaṃ upaṭṭhapetuṃ (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.)</note> apayāpetabbaṃ apayāpetuṃ ṭhapetabbaṃ ṭhapetuṃ. So rājānaṃ cakkavattiṃ upasaṅkamitvā evamāha – ‘appossukko tvaṃ <pb ed="T" n="3.0330"/>, deva, hohi. Ahamanusāsissāmī’ti. Rañño, bhikkhave, cakkavattissa evarūpaṃ pariṇāyakaratanaṃ pātubhavati. Rājā, bhikkhave, cakkavattī imehi sattahi ratanehi samannāgato hoti.

<pb ed="P" n="3.0176"/> <note>upaṭṭhapetabbaṃ upaṭṭhapetuṃ (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.)</note> <pb ed="T" n="3.0330"/>
"Furthermore, monks, the elephant-treasure appears to the Wheel-turning Monarch: a perfectly white, firm-footed, powerful, sky-faring royal nāga named Uposatha. Seeing him, the mind of the Wheel-turning Monarch is pleased: 'It would be wonderful to ride the elephant if he would undergo taming!' Then, monks, that elephant-treasure undergoes taming just like a fine thoroughbred elephant well tamed for a long time. It so happens that the Wheel-turning Monarch, when testing the elephant-treasure, mounts him in the morning, and after traversing the whole earth to the edge of the ocean, he returns to the royal capital to take his morning meal. Such is the elephant-treasure that appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch.

"Furthermore, monks, the horse-treasure appears to the Wheel-turning Monarch: a perfectly white, raven-headed, muñja-maned, powerful, sky-faring king of horses named Valāhaka ('Thundercloud'). Seeing him, the mind of the Wheel-turning Monarch is pleased: 'It would be wonderful to ride the horse if he would undergo taming!' Then, monks, that horse-treasure undergoes taming just like a fine thoroughbred horse well tamed for a long time. It so happens that the Wheel-turning Monarch, when testing the horse-treasure, mounts him in the morning, and after traversing the whole earth to the edge of the ocean, he returns to the royal capital to take his morning meal. Such is the horse-treasure that appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch.

"Furthermore, monks, the jewel-treasure appears to the Wheel-turning Monarch. This jewel is a beryl gem, beautiful, of good origin, eight-faceted, and well-worked. Now the radiance of the jewel-treasure spreads around for a whole league. It so happens that when the Wheel-turning Monarch is testing the jewel-treasure, he draws up his four-constituent army in array, and mounting the jewel on top of his banner, he sets forth in the darkness and gloom of the night. Then all the inhabitants of the villages nearby begin their work by its light, thinking that it is day. Such is the jewel-treasure that appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch.

"Furthermore, monks, the woman-treasure appears to the Wheel-turning Monarch. She is beautiful, comely and graceful, possessing the supreme beauty of complexion, neither too tall nor too short, neither too slim nor too stout, neither too dark nor too fair, surpassing human beauty without reaching divine beauty. The touch of the woman-treasure is such that it is like a tuft of cotton or a tuft of cotton-wool. When it is cool, her limbs are warm; when it is warm, her limbs are cool. From her body, the scent of sandalwood comes forth, and from her mouth the scent of lotuses. She rises before the Wheel-turning Monarch and retires after him. She is eager to serve, agreeable in conduct, and sweet in speech. That woman-treasure does not transgress the Wheel-turning Monarch even in thought, how much less in deed? Such is the woman-treasure that appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch.
Again, bhikkhus, the steward-treasure ( gahapatiratana ) appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch ( cakkavatti ).

The divine eye born of past action (kammavipākajaṃ dibbacakkhu) is manifested in him whereby he sees hidden stores of treasure both with owners and ownerless.

He approaches the Wheel-turning Monarch and says: ‘Sire, you remain at ease.

I shall take care of your monetary affairs.’

And it so happens that when the Wheel-turning Monarch is testing the steward-treasure, he boards a boat, and putting out into the river Ganges, in midstream he tells the steward-treasure: ‘I need gold and bullion, steward (hiranya-suvarna ).’

‘Then, sire, let the boat be steered towards one bank.’

‘Steward (gahapati ), it is actually here that I need gold and bullion.’

Then the steward-treasure plunges both hands into the water and draws up a pot full of gold and bullion, and he tells the Wheel-turning Monarch: ‘Is this enough, sire? Is this sufficient, sire? Is this pleasing to you, sire?’

The Wheel-turning Monarch says: ‘It is enough, steward! It is sufficient, steward! It is pleasing, steward!’

Such is the steward-treasure that appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch.

Again, bhikkhus, the counsellor-treasure (pariṇāyakaratana) appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch: he is wise, clever, intelligent and capable of advising the Wheel-turning Monarch, keeping him on the right path, preventing him from straying, and establishing him in goodness.

He approaches the Wheel-turning Monarch and says: ‘Sire, you remain at ease. I shall govern.’

Such is the counsellor-treasure that appears to a Wheel-turning Monarch.

These are the seven treasures that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

<pb ed="M" n="3.0215"/> <pb ed="P" n="3.0177"/> <p rend="bodytext">‘‘Taṃ kiṃ maññatha, bhikkhave, api nu kho rājā cakkavattī imehi sattahi ratanehi samannāgato imāhi catūhi ca iddhīhi tatonidānaṃ sukhaṃ somanassaṃ paṭisaṃvediyethā’’ti? ‘‘Ekamekenapi, bhante, ratanena <note>tena ratanena (sī.)</note> samannāgato rājā cakkavattī tatonidānaṃ sukhaṃ somanassaṃ paṭisaṃvediyetha, ko pana vādo sattahi ratanehi catūhi ca iddhīhī’’ti?

259. “What are the four kinds of success? Here, bhikkhus, a Wheel-turning Monarch is handsome, comely, and graceful, possessing the supreme beauty of complexion, and he surpasses other human beings in that respect. This is the first kind of success that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“Again, bhikkhus, a Wheel-turning Monarch is long-lived and endures long, and he surpasses other human beings in that respect. This is the second kind of success that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“Again, bhikkhus, a Wheel-turning Monarch is free from illness and affliction, possessing a good digestion that is neither too cool nor too warm, and he surpasses other human beings in that respect. This is the third kind of success that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“Again, bhikkhus, a Wheel-turning Monarch is dear and agreeable to brāhmaṇas (brahmins) and householders. Just as a father is dear and agreeable to his children, so too a Wheel-turning Monarch is dear and agreeable to brāhmaṇas (brahmins) and householders. Brāhmaṇas (Brahmins) and house-holders, too, are dear and agreeable to a Wheel-turning Monarch. Just as children are dear and agreeable to a father, so too brāhmaṇas (brahmins) and householders are dear and agreeable to a Wheel-turning Monarch.

“Once a Wheel-turning Monarch was driving in a pleasure park with his four-constituent army. Then brāhmaṇas (brahmins) and householders went to the Wheel-turning Monarch and spoke thus: ‘Sire, drive slowly that we may see you longer.’ And so the Wheel-turning Monarch told his charioteer: ‘Charioteer, drive slowly that I may see the brāhmaṇas (brahmins) and householders longer.’ This is the fourth kind of success that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses. These are the four kinds of success that a Wheel-turning Monarch possesses.

“What do you think, bhikkhus? Would a Wheel-turning Monarch experience pleasure and joy because of possessing these seven treasures and these four kinds of success?” “Venerable sir, a Wheel-turning Monarch would experience pleasure and joy because of possessing even one treasure, let alone seven treasures and four kinds of success.”

<p n="260" rend="bodytext"><hi rend="paranum">260</hi><hi rend="dot">.</hi> Atha kho bhagavā parittaṃ pāṇimattaṃ pāsāṇaṃ gahetvā bhikkhū āmantesi – ‘‘taṃ kiṃ maññatha, bhikkhave, katamo nu kho mahantataro – yo cāyaṃ mayā paritto pāṇimatto pāsāṇo <pb ed="V" n="3.0216"/> gahito yo ca himavā pabbatarājā’’ti? ‘‘Appamattako ayaṃ, bhante, bhagavatā paritto pāṇimatto pāsāṇo gahito; himavantaṃ pabbatarājānaṃ upanidhāya saṅkhampi na upeti; kalabhāgampi na upeti; upanidhampi na upetī’’ti. ‘‘Evameva kho, bhikkhave, yaṃ rājā cakkavattī sattahi ratanehi samannāgato <pb ed="T" n="3.0332"/> catūhi ca iddhīhi tatonidānaṃ sukhaṃ somanassaṃ paṭisaṃvedeti taṃ dibbassa sukhassa upanidhāya saṅkhampi na upeti; kalabhāgampi na upeti; upanidhampi na upeti’’.

<p rend="bodytext">‘‘Sa kho so, bhikkhave, paṇḍito sace kadāci karahaci dīghassa addhuno accayena manussattaṃ āgacchati, yāni tāni uccākulāni – khattiyamahāsālakulaṃ vā brāhmaṇamahāsālakulaṃ vā gahapatimahāsālakulaṃ vā tathārūpe kule paccājāyati aḍḍhe mahaddhane mahābhoge pahūtajātarūparajate pahūtavittūpakaraṇe pahūtadhanadhaññe. So ca hoti abhirūpo dassanīyo pāsādiko paramāya <pb ed="M" n="3.0216"/> vaṇṇapokkharatāya samannāgato, lābhī annassa pānassa vatthassa yānassa mālāgandhavilepanassa seyyāvasathapadīpeyyassa. So kāyena sucaritaṃ carati, vācāya sucaritaṃ carati, manasā sucaritaṃ carati. So kāyena <pb ed="P" n="3.0178"/> sucaritaṃ caritvā, vācāya sucaritaṃ caritvā, manasā sucaritaṃ caritvā, kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā sugatiṃ saggaṃ lokaṃ upapajjati. Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, akkhadhutto paṭhameneva kaṭaggahena mahantaṃ bhogakkhandhaṃ adhigaccheyya; appamattako so, bhikkhave, kaṭaggaho yaṃ so akkhadhutto paṭhameneva kaṭaggahena mahantaṃ bhogakkhandhaṃ adhigaccheyya. Atha kho ayameva tato mahantataro kaṭaggaho yaṃ so paṇḍito kāyena sucaritaṃ caritvā, vācāya sucaritaṃ caritvā, manasā sucaritaṃ caritvā kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā sugatiṃ saggaṃ <pb ed="T" n="3.0333"/> lokaṃ upapajjati. 260. Then the Blessed One, taking a small stone the size of his hand (parittaṃ pāṇimattaṃ pāsāṇaṃ), addressed the monks (bhikkhū) thus: “What do you think, monks (bhikkhave)? Which is the greater, this small stone (paritto pāṇimatto pāsāṇaṃ) that I have taken, or Himalaya (himavā), the king of mountains (pabbatarājā)?”

“Venerable sir (bhante), the small stone (paritto pāṇimatto pāsāṇaṃ) that the Blessed One (bhagavatā) has taken, is insignificant compared to Himalaya (himavantaṃ), the king of mountains (pabbatarājānaṃ); it is not even a fraction (saṅkhampi na upeti), there is no comparison (kalabhāgampi na upeti; upanidhampi na upeti).”

“So too, monks (bhikkhave), the pleasure (sukhaṃ) and joy (somanassaṃ) that a Wheel-turning Monarch (rājā cakkavattī), possessing the seven treasures (sattahi ratanehi samannāgato) and the four kinds of success (catūhi ca iddhīhi), would experience because of that, does not count beside the happiness of heaven (dibbassa sukhassa); it is not even a fraction (saṅkhampi na upeti), there is no comparison (kalabhāgampi na upeti; upanidhampi na upeti).

“If, some time or other, monks (bhikkhave), at the end of a long period (dīghassa addhuno accayena), the wise man (paṇḍito) comes back to the human state (manussattaṃ āgacchati), it is into a high family (uccākulāni) that he is reborn (paccājāyati) - into a family of well-to-do nobles (khattiyamahāsālakulaṃ) , or well-to-do brahmins (brāhmaṇamahāsālakulaṃ) , or well-to-do householders (gahapatimahāsālakulaṃ) - one that is rich (aḍḍhe), of great wealth (mahaddhane), of great possessions (mahābhoge), with abundant gold and silver (pahūtajātarūparajate), with abundant assets and means (pahūtavittūpakaraṇe), and with abundant money and grain (pahūtadhanadhaññe).

He is handsome (abhirūpo), comely (dassanīyo) and graceful (pāsādiko), possessing the supreme beauty of complexion (paramāya vaṇṇapokkharatāya samannāgato). He obtains food (annassa) and drink (pānassa), clothes (vatthassa), vehicles (yānassa), garlands (mālā), scents (gandha) and unguents (vilepanassa), bed (seyyā), lodging (avasatha), and light (padīpeyyassa).

He conducts himself well in body (kāyena sucaritaṃ carati), speech (vācāya sucaritaṃ carati), and mind (manasā sucaritaṃ carati).

Having conducted himself well in body (kāyena sucaritaṃ caritvā), speech (vācāya sucaritaṃ caritvā), and mind (manasā sucaritaṃ caritvā), on the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (paraṃ maraṇā), he reappears in a happy destination (sugatiṃ), even in the heavenly world (saggaṃ lokaṃ upapajjati).

Suppose, monks (bhikkhave), a gambler (akkhadhutto) at the very first lucky throw (paṭhameneva kaṭaggahena) won a great fortune (mahantaṃ bhogakkhandhaṃ adhigaccheyya); yet a lucky throw such as that is negligible (appamattako so, bhikkhave, kaṭaggaho) compared to the wise man (paṇḍito) who, at the very first lucky throw (paṭhameneva kaṭaggahena), wins a great fortune (mahantaṃ bhogakkhandhaṃ adhigaccheyya).

It is a far more lucky throw when a wise man (paṇḍito) who conducts himself well in body (kāyena sucaritaṃ caritvā), speech (vācāya sucaritaṃ caritvā), and mind (manasā sucaritaṃ caritvā), on the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (paraṃ maraṇā), reappears in a happy destination (sugatiṃ), even in the heavenly world (saggaṃ lokaṃ upapajjati).

This, monks (bhikkhave), is the complete perfection of the wise man’s grade (kevalā paripūrā paṇḍitabhūmī).”

Thus spoke the Lord (idamavoca bhagavā).

Elated (attamanā), those monks (te bhikkhū) delighted in the Blessed One’s (bhagavato) statement (bhāsitaṃ abhinandunti).
