64. Thus, "me suta*nti ākaṅkheyyasuttaṃ."

There, sampannasīlā means threefold completeness, perfect complete with qualities of delightful sweetness.

There—

"Complete with rice fields, the parrots eat the kosiya;

I report to you, Brahmā, I am not able to prevent them."

(jā. 1.14.1);

This is called paripuṇṇasampannaṃ.

"Endowed with this pātimokkhasaṃvara, he is approaching, meeting, arriving, fully approaching, attaining, complete, endowed" (vibha. 511). This is called samaṅgisampannaṃ.

"The undersurface of this great earth, O venerable one, is complete, tasting just like pure honey" (pārā. 17). This is called madhurasampannaṃ.

Here, both paripuṇṇasampannaṃ and samaṅgisampannaṃ are applicable.

Therefore, sampannasīlāti should be understood to mean becoming perfect in morality, and also becoming complete with moral qualities.

Sīlanti, what is sīla by way of meaning? Sīla is sīla by way of its nature.

Its detailed discussion is stated in the Visuddhimagga.

paripuṇṇasampannaṃ samaṅgisampannaṃ madhurasampannaṃ sīla sīlaṃ Visuddhimagga

There, "paripuṇṇasīlā"—by this meaning, just as completeness of a field is through the removal of defects of the field, likewise completeness of morality is said to be through the removal of defects of morality.

Just as a field, being complete with four defects—seed defect, sowing defect, water defect, alkali defect—is incomplete.

There, bījakhaṇḍaṃ means that wherever seeds are scattered intermittently, or are rotten, there the crop does not grow, the field is defective.

vappakhaṇḍaṃ means that wherever an unskillful person, scattering seeds, intermittently causes them to fall.

Thus, indeed, the crop does not grow everywhere, the field is defective.

udakakhaṇḍaṃ means that wherever water is sometimes too much or not present, there also the crops do not grow, the field is defective.

ūsakhaṇḍaṃ means that wherever a farmer, plowing the land in some area four or five times, makes it excessively deep, then alkali arises, there also the crop does not grow, the field is defective, and such a field is not of great fruit, nor of great benefit, there also, having sown much, one obtains little.

However, with the removal of these four defects, the field is complete.

And such a field is of great fruit, of great benefit.

In the same way, morality, being complete with four defects—broken, perforated, spotted, mixed with action—is incomplete.

And such morality is not of great fruit, nor of great benefit.

However, with the removal of these four defects, the field of morality is complete, and such morality is of great fruit, of great benefit.

bījakhaṇḍaṃ vappakhaṇḍaṃ udakakhaṇḍaṃ ūsakhaṇḍa

"sīlasamaṅgino "—by this meaning, being complete with morality, having gone to harmony, being endowed, live, thus indeed it is said.

There, completeness of morality is by two causes: by seeing the danger in the failure of morality, and by seeing the benefit in the success of morality.

Both of these are explained in detail in the Visuddhimagga.

There, Sumanatthera, a resident of Dīpavihāra, said, "By 'endowed with virtue' (sampannasīlā), the Blessed One, it seems, having mentioned the fourfold purity of virtue (catupārisuddhisīlaṃ), by 'restrained by the restraint of the Pātimokkha' (pātimokkhasaṃvarasaṃvutā), showed the chief virtue there by expanding it."
His pupil, Tepiṭakacūḷanāgatthera, said, "The restraint of the Pātimokkha (pātimokkhasaṃvaro) was spoken of by the Blessed One in both places, for virtue is simply the restraint of the Pātimokkha (pātimokkhasaṃvaroyeva hi sīlaṃ)."
Not approving that the remaining three are called virtues, he said, " – 'The restraint of the senses (indriyasaṃvaro) is merely the measure of guarding the six doors; purity of livelihood (ājīvapārisuddhi) is merely the measure of the arising of requisites with evenness in accordance with the Dharma, merely the measure of using having reflected, 'This is for such and such a purpose' (idam atthanti), on requisites that are relied on and obtained.
Virtue is the restraint of the Pātimokkha (pātimokkhasaṃvarova sīlaṃ) without alternative.
He whose [virtue] is broken, should not be spoken of as protecting the remaining hands and feet like a man whose head is cut off (chinnasīso viya puriso hatthapāde sesāni rakkhissatīti na vattabbo).
But he whose [virtue] is not diseased, is able to protect the remaining life, having made [them] natural again, like a man whose head is not cut off (acchinnasīso viya puriso jīvitaṃ sesāni puna pākatikāni katvā rakkhituṃ sakkoti).
Therefore, having mentioned the restraint of the Pātimokkha (pātimokkhasaṃvaraṃ) by 'endowed with virtue' (sampannasīlā), saying 'endowed with the Pātimokkha' (sampannapātimokkhā) is a synonym of that, showing that expanding that, he said, 'restrained by the restraint of the Pātimokkha' (pātimokkhasaṃvarasaṃvutā) etc."

65. "If he should wish" - why is this begun? For the purpose of showing the advantages of morality.

Even if new renunciates or unwise ones were to [ask], "The Blessed One says, 'Fulfill morality,' what is the advantage in fulfilling morality, what is the distinction, what is the increase?" To show them the seventeen advantages, he said this:

"Perhaps these fellow ব্রহ্মচারিনs, having heard the advantage that culminates in the destruction of the influxes beginning with the beloved and pleasing, might fulfill morality."

Like a merchant of thornless goods.

"Merchant of thornless goods" is what a merchant of guḷa is called.

That [man], it is said, having taken guḷa, phāṇita, khaṇḍa, sakkharā, and other things in a cart, went to a border village and exclaimed, "Take thornless [goods], take thornless [goods]!"

Having heard that, the villagers [thought], "Poison is indeed harsh; whoever eats it dies. A thorn also kills by piercing. Both of these are harsh. What is the advantage here?" They shut the doors of their houses and chased away the children.

Having seen that, the merchant [thought], "These villagers are unskilled in business. Come, let me persuade them by a trick." He exclaimed, "Take very sweet [goods], take very delicious [goods]. Guḷa, phāṇita, and sakkharā are obtained cheaply. [They] can be obtained even with counterfeit māsakas and counterfeit kahāpaṇas!"

Having heard that, the villagers, delighted and pleased, came and, having given much wealth, took [them].

There, the exclamation of the merchant, "Take thornless [goods]!" is like the Blessed One's words, "Endowed with morality, monks, abide...train yourselves by undertaking the training rules."

"Both of these are harsh. What is the advantage here?" The villagers' thought is like the Blessed One's saying, "Abide endowed with morality." "Morality is indeed harsh, rough, and opposed to playfulness, etc. What is the advantage for those endowed with morality?" [This is] the monks' thought.

Then, the words of that merchant, "Take very sweet [goods]!" etc., are like the Blessed One's words, "If he should wish," etc., which are for the purpose of declaring the seventeen advantages culminating in the destruction of the influxes beginning with the beloved and pleasing. This should be understood.

There, "ākaṅkheyya ce (If he should wish)" means if he should wish, if he should desire.

"piyo ca assa (And he is dear)" means he should be seen with loving eyes; it is said that he would be the basis for the arising of affection.

"manāpo (Pleasing)" means he is one who increases their minds; or he should be attained by their minds; it is said that he should be pervaded with a loving mind.

"garū (Revered)" means he is worthy of their reverence, like a stone parasol.

"bhāvanīyo (Cultivatable)" means he is to be esteemed thus: "Indeed, this venerable one knows knowing, sees seeing."

"sīlesvevassa paripūrakārī (He is a fulfiller of morality)" means he is a fulfiller of morality only in the fourfold purity of morality; it is said that he would be endowed with a complete and unexcelled form.

"ajjhattaṃ cetosamathamanuyutto (Devoted to inner tranquility of mind)" means devoted to tranquility of his own mind. Here, "inner" or "own" - this is the same meaning; only the expression is different.

In the sphere of the earth, however, tranquility is a word of usage.

Anu is accomplished in connection with this preposition.

"anirākatajjhāno (Not rejecting absorption)" means not driving out absorption externally, or not destroying absorption. Rejection is indeed a term for carrying out and destroying.

His usage should be seen in Thambhaṃ niraṃkatvā nivātavuttītiādi (Having rejected stiffness, his conduct is without wind), etc.

ākaṅkheyya ce (If he should wish) piyo ca assa (And he is dear) manāpo (Pleasing) garū (Revered) bhāvanīyo (Cultivatable) sīlesvevassa paripūrakārī (He is a fulfiller of morality)

"vipassanāya samannāgato (Endowed with insight)" means endowed with sevenfold contemplation. The sevenfold anupassanā (contemplation) are: contemplation of impermanence, contemplation of suffering, contemplation of selflessness, contemplation of disgust, contemplation of dispassion, contemplation of cessation, [and] contemplation of relinquishment.

These are expanded in the Visuddhimagga.

"brūhetā suññāgārāna (One who develops empty dwellings)" means one who increases empty dwellings. Here, having taken a meditation subject by way of tranquility and insight, a monk who enters an empty dwelling day and night and sits down is to be understood as "brūhetā suññāgārāna".

However, one who is making a one-storied palace, etc., is not a developer of empty dwellings; this should be seen.

vipassanāya samannāgato (Endowed with insight) anupassanā (Contemplation) visuddhimagge (Visuddhimagga) brūhetā suññāgārāna (One who develops empty dwellings)

And so, just as the teaching concerning craving-wandering, although begun first by way of craving, because craving is the foundation, having shifted the pride-views to the pride-views, gradually became a teaching of the threefold proliferation, so this teaching, although begun first by way of the training in higher morality, because morality is the foundation, having shifted the tranquility and insight to the tranquility and insight, gradually became a teaching of the threefold training; this should be understood.

Here, indeed, with "sīlesvevassa paripūrakārī (He is a fulfiller of morality)," the training in higher morality has been spoken of.
“Being devoted to inner tranquility of mind, without rejecting meditation” – this much is the training in higher mind. “Being endowed with insight” – this much is the training in higher wisdom. But by “frequenting empty dwellings,” the training in higher mind is [achieved] by increasing empty dwellings through tranquility, and the training in higher wisdom is [achieved] through insight; thus, both trainings are mentioned by including [them].

And here, by these words, "Being devoted to inner tranquility of mind, without rejecting meditation," is spoken of the single-pointedness of mind that protects morality.

By the word "insight," [is meant] the examination of formations that protects morality.

How does single-pointedness of mind protect morality? For one who does not have single-pointedness of mind, he is disturbed when illness arises; being afflicted by illness, with a distracted mind, he might even destroy morality in order to alleviate the illness.

But for one who has single-pointedness of mind, he suppresses that suffering of illness and attains meditative absorption; at the moment of attainment, suffering is far removed, and a stronger happiness arises. Thus, single-pointedness of mind protects morality.

How does the examination of formations protect morality? For one who does not have the examination of formations, he has a strong sense of self in the self-existence, [thinking], "My form, my consciousness"; when such misfortunes as famine, illness, and fear arise, he might even nourish the self-existence by destroying morality.

But for one who has the examination of formations, he does not have a strong sense of self or affection in the self-existence; when such misfortunes as famine, illness, and fear arise, even if his intestines come out, even if he withers and dries up, or even if he is broken into pieces, a hundred times or a thousand times, he would never nourish the self-existence by destroying morality. Thus, the examination of formations protects morality.

But by “frequenting empty dwellings,” the continuous action of developing and increasing both of these [qualities] is shown.

Thus, the Blessed One, since the Blessed One said, "For one who desires these four qualities: 'May I be dear, agreeable, respected, and esteemed by my fellow ব্রহ্মচারীs,' nothing else needs to be done; rather, one should be endowed with morality and other virtues; for such a one is dear, agreeable, respected, and esteemed by his fellow ব্রহ্মচারীs."

It was also said:

"A person who is accomplished in morality and vision,
Just in the Dhamma, truthful in speech,
Doing his own work,
That person is made dear by the people."

Therefore, "If a monk should desire, O monks, to be dear, agreeable, respected, and esteemed by his fellow ব্রহ্মচারীs, he should be a perfecter in morality…frequenting empty dwellings," having said this, now, since even one who desires gain and other requisites should do this same thing, and nothing else, therefore, [the Blessed One] said, "If a monk should desire, O monks, to be a gainer," and so on.

And here, the Blessed One should be understood not to be speaking of the perfection of morality and other virtues for the sake of gain.

The Blessed One, who has cut off the talk of seeking food, does not utter speech that is connected [with it]; thus he advises his disciples; how could he speak of the perfection of morality and other virtues for the sake of gain? But this was said according to the inclination of the individual. For those who might have such an inclination, "If we were not troubled by the four requisites, we would be able to perfect morality and other virtues," according to their inclination, the Blessed One spoke thus.

Moreover, this is the taste-related benefit of morality, namely, these four requisites.

Thus, wise people, having taken out what is placed in storehouses and other places, even without giving it to their sons and others, or even without using it themselves, give it to the virtuous; therefore, this was said for the purpose of showing the benefit of morality with its pleasant taste.

In the third saying, "Whom I" means whom I. "Their deeds" means those acts of giving requisites done to me by those gods or humans. For even gods give requisites to those endowed with morality and other virtues, not only humans, like Sakka to the venerable Mahākassapa.

"Of great fruit, of great benefit" – both of these are the same in meaning, only the expressions are different. "Of great fruit" means producing great worldly happiness. "Of great benefit" means being a condition for great transcendental happiness. For even a spoonful of alms given to one endowed with morality and other virtues, or a leaf hut built and given on land the size of five jewels, protects from falling into bad destinies for many thousands of eons, and in the end, it is a condition for the deathless element of nirvāṇa.

68. “If, monks, a bhikkhu should wish: ‘May I experience the various kinds of supernormal powers: having been one, may I become many; having been many, may I become one; may I appear and vanish; may I go unhindered through a wall, through an enclosure, through a mountain as though through space; may I dive in and out of the earth as though it were water; may I walk on water without sinking as though it were earth; seated cross-legged, may I travel in space like a bird; with my hand may I touch and stroke the moon and sun, so powerful and mighty; may I exert influence with my body even as far as the Brahma world’—let him be one who perfectly fulfills the precepts…and let him develop secluded dwellings.

“If, monks, a bhikkhu should wish: ‘May I, with the divine ear element (dibbāya sotadhātuyā), which is purified and surpasses the human, hear both kinds of sounds, the divine and the human, those that are far as well as near’—let him be one who perfectly fulfills the precepts…and let him develop secluded dwellings.

“If, monks, a bhikkhu should wish: ‘May I understand the minds of other beings, of other persons, having encompassed them with my own mind. May I understand a mind affected by lust (sarāgaṃ cittaṃ) as affected by lust and a mind unaffected by lust (vītarāgaṃ cittaṃ) as unaffected by lust; may I understand a mind affected by hate (sadosaṃ cittaṃ) as affected by hate and a mind unaffected by hate (vītadosaṃ cittaṃ) as unaffected by hate; may I understand a mind affected by delusion (samohaṃ cittaṃ) as affected by delusion and a mind unaffected by delusion (vītamohaṃ cittaṃ) as unaffected by delusion; may I understand a contracted mind (saṃkhittaṃ cittaṃ) as contracted and a distracted mind (vikkhittaṃ cittaṃ) as distracted; may I understand an exalted mind (mahaggataṃ cittaṃ) as exalted and an unexalted mind (amahaggataṃ cittaṃ) as unexalted; may I understand a surpassable mind (sauttaraṃ cittaṃ) as surpassable and an unsurpassed mind (anuttaraṃ cittaṃ) as unsurpassed; may I understand a concentrated mind (samāhitaṃ cittaṃ) as concentrated and an unconcentrated mind (asamāhitaṃ cittaṃ) as unconcentrated; may I understand a liberated mind (vimuttaṃ cittaṃ) as liberated and an unliberated mind (avimuttaṃ cittaṃ) as unliberated’—let him be one who perfectly fulfills the precepts…and let him develop secluded dwellings.

“If, monks, a bhikkhu should wish: ‘May I recollect my manifold past lives (anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ), that is, one birth, two births, three births, four births, five births, ten births, twenty births, thirty births, forty births, fifty births, a hundred births, a thousand births, a hundred thousand births, many aeons of world-contraction, many aeons of world-expansion, many aeons of world-contraction and expansion: There I was so named, of such a clan, with such an appearance, such was my nutriment, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such my life-term. Passing away from there, I was reborn in such-and-such other circumstances. There too I was so named, of such a clan, with such an appearance, such was my nutriment, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such my life-term. Passing away from there, I was reborn here.’ Thus with their aspects and their particulars may I recollect my manifold past lives (anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ)’—let him be one who perfectly fulfills the precepts…and let him develop secluded dwellings.

“If, monks, a bhikkhu should wish: ‘May I, with the divine eye (dibbena cakkhunā), which is purified and surpasses the human, see beings passing away and reappearing, inferior and superior, fair and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate; may I understand how beings pass on according to their actions thus: “These worthy beings who were ill-conducted in body, speech, and mind, revilers of noble ones, wrong in their views, giving effect to wrong view in their actions, on the dissolution of the body, after death, have reappeared in a state of deprivation, in a bad destination, in the abyss, in hell; but these worthy beings who were well-conducted in body, speech, and mind, not revilers of noble ones, right in their views, giving effect to right view in their actions, on the dissolution of the body, after death, have reappeared in a good destination, in a heavenly world”’—thus may I, with the divine eye (dibbena cakkhunā), which is purified and surpasses the human, see beings passing away and reappearing, inferior and superior, fair and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate; may I understand how beings pass on according to their actions’—let him be one who perfectly fulfills the precepts, devoted to internal serenity of mind, not rejecting meditative absorption, possessed of insight, and let him develop secluded dwellings.

Thus, it has been said regarding the path of the non-returner (anāgāmin). Although the turn of the fourth path has come, the Blessed One did not take it up. Because it is not only the benefit of morality that is the knowledge of the destruction of the influxes (āsavakkhaya-abhiññā), but also the worldly five knowledges (lokiyapañcābhiññā), therefore, in order to show these as well, since the teaching is completed when the destruction of the influxes is spoken of, and thus the story would be called a "truncated knowledge story" because these qualities are not spoken of, and therefore, in order to show the completion of the knowledges, and because the supernormal transformations (iddhi-vikuppanā) of one established in the path of the non-returner easily succeed, since the obstructions to concentration, desire, passion, and ill-will are eradicated, the non-returner is a fulfiller in morality and in concentration. Therefore, it is fitting and appropriate to show the worldly knowledges. "If he should desire...pe...manifold," thus, and so on, he says. This is the connection.

There, the Pāḷi commentary on the five worldly knowledges that have come in the manner beginning with "manifold supernormal power" (anekavihitaṃ iddhividha) is spoken of in the Visuddhimagga together with the method of cultivation.

69. āsavānaṃ khayā ("the destruction of the āsavas") means the destruction of all defilements by the path of Arhatship.

anāsava ("taintless") means devoid of āsavas.

cetovimuttiṃ paññāvimutti ("liberation of mind, liberation by wisdom"): here, ceto ("mind") refers to the samādhi associated with the fruition of Arhatship, and paññā ("wisdom") refers to the wisdom associated with that.

And there, it should be understood that samādhi is cetovimutti ("liberation of mind") because of being liberated from passion, and paññā is paññāvimutti ("liberation by wisdom") because of being liberated from ignorance.

This was said by the Blessed One: "That which is his, monks, is samādhi, that is his faculty of samādhi.

That which is his, monks, is wisdom, that is his faculty of wisdom.

Thus, monks, , liberation of mind comes from the absence of passion, liberation by wisdom comes from the absence of ignorance," also, it should be understood that the fruit of serenity is cetovimutti ("liberation of mind"), and the fruit of insight (vipassanā) is paññāvimutti ("liberation by wisdom").

diṭṭheva dhamme ("in the visible here and now") means in this very existence.

sayaṃ abhiññā sacchikatvā ("having realized it oneself through super-knowledge") means having directly made it, having known it through one's own wisdom, not through another's agency; that is the meaning.

upasampajja vihareyya ("having attained, he dwells") means having reached and accomplished, he dwells.

sīlesvevā ("only in virtue") means that even desiring to attain liberation of mind and liberation by wisdom, after having completely driven away all the taints in this way, he is a perfecter only in virtue.

diṭṭheva dhamme sayaṃ abhiññā sacchikatvā upasampajja vihareyya sīlesvevā Thus, the Blessed One, having spoken of the benefits of virtue up to Arhatship, now summing up all those benefits of virtue, and showing [them], speaks the conclusion: "Accomplished in virtue, monks…pe… this was said dependent on this."

This is the condensed meaning: "Accomplished in virtue, monks, dwell…pe… undertake the training in the trainings," thus, that which was previously said by me in this way, all of this: the monk accomplished in virtue is dear and pleasing to his fellow ব্রহ্মচারীs, he is respected and worthy of reverence, he obtains requisites, he is of great fruit to those who give requisites, he is of great fruit through the thought of remembrance to former relatives, he is able to endure both discontent and content, he is able to endure fear and terror, and he gains the rūpāvacara-jhānas and the arūpāvacara-jhānas, the three lower fruits of asceticism, the five mundane super-knowledges, and the knowledge of the destruction of the āsavas, and he himself is the direct experiencer of these qualities through super-knowledge , this was said dependent on this, intending this.

This is what the Blessed One said, the monks were delighted and rejoiced in the Blessed One's words.
