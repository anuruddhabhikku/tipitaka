Thus have I heard. Once the Blessed One was staying at Sāavatthī in the Jetavana, Anāthapindika’s monastery.

There the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying: “Monks!”

“Venerable sir,” those monks replied to the Blessed One. The Blessed One said this:

“Monks, a monk intent on heightened mind should attend to five themes from time to time. What are the five? Here, monks, if evil, unskillful thoughts, imbued with desire ( chandūpasaṃhitā ), aversion ( dosūpasaṃhitā ), or delusion ( mohūpasaṃhitā ), arise in a monk while he is referring to and attending to a particular theme ( nimittaṃ ), he should attend to another theme, apart from that one, connected with what is skillful ( kusalūpasaṃhitaṃ ). When he is attending to this other theme, apart from that one, connected with what is skillful, then those evil, unskillful thoughts are abandoned and subside. With their abandoning, his mind becomes steadied internally, quieted, brought to singleness, and concentrated ( samādhiyati ).

Just as a skilled carpenter or his apprentice would use a small peg to knock out, drive out, and pull out a large one; in the same way, if evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — arise in a monk while he is referring to and attending to a particular theme, he should attend to another theme, apart from that one, connected with what is skillful. When he is attending to this other theme, apart from that one, connected with what is skillful, then those evil, unskillful thoughts are abandoned and subside. With their abandoning, his mind becomes steadied internally, quieted, brought to singleness, and concentrated.

216. Thus have I heard: The vitakka-saṇṭhāna-sutta.

Therein, "adhicittamanuyuttenā" means the mind (citta) that has arisen through the ten wholesome courses of action (dasakusalakammapatha), [is] just mind. The mind of the eight attainments that are the basis of insight (vipassanāpādakaaṭṭhasamāpatti), that mind is more than mind, thus "adhicitta." "anuyuttenā" means that adhicitta by means of being engaged (anuyuttena), there the meaning is "by means of being yoked and applied" (yuttappayuttenāti attho).

Therein, this bhikkhu, having wandered for alms before the meal (purebhattaṃ piṇḍāya caritvā), having returned from the alms-round after the meal (pacchābhattaṃ piṇḍapātappaṭikkanto), taking his sitting cloth (nisīdanaṃ ādāya), even as he goes forth saying, "At such-and-such a tree-root (asukasmiṃ rukkhamūle vā), or in a forest thicket (vanasaṇḍe vā), or at the foot of a mountain (pabbatapāde vā), or in a mountain cave (pabbhāre vā) I will do the ascetic's duty (samaṇadhammaṃ karissāmīti nikkhamantopi)", even as he goes there and removes grass and leaves from the sitting place with his hands or feet (hatthehi vā pādehi vā nisajjaṭṭhānato tiṇapaṇṇāni apanentopi) is engaged in the higher mind (adhicittaṃ anuyuttoyeva).

Moreover, having sat down, having washed his hands and feet, having crossed his legs in the lotus position, having taken a root meditation subject (mūlakammaṭṭhānaṃ gahetvā), even as he dwells (viharantopi) is engaged in the higher mind (adhicittaṃ anuyuttoyeva).

"nimittānī": causes (kāraṇāni).

"kālena kāla": at the right time (samaye samaye).

But surely, a meditation subject (kammaṭṭhānaṃ) by name should be attended to uninterruptedly, without abandoning it even for a moment (muhuttampi achaḍḍetvā nirantaraṃ manasikātabbaṃ), why did the Blessed One say "kālena kāla"?

In the Pali, thirty-eight meditation subjects (aṭṭhatiṃsa kammaṭṭhānāni) are distinguished (vibhattāni), of those, by the bhikkhu who has taken a meditation subject agreeable to his own mind (attano cittarucitaṃ kammaṭṭhānaṃ gahetvā) and is sitting (nisinnena) as long as any defilement (upakkileso) does not arise (nuppajjati), there is no task of attending to these causes (imesaṃ nimittānaṃ manasikārakiccaṃ natthi).

But when it arises (yadā pana uppajjati), then showing that "having taken these, the boil that has arisen in the mind (citte uppannaṃ abbudaṃ) should be extracted (nīharitabbanti dassento)", thus he said (evamāha).

Nimittāni, Kālena kāla.

"chandūpasaṃhitā": accompanied by desire (chandasahagatā), connected with passion (rāgasampayuttā).

Moreover, the field (khettaṃ) and object (ārammaṇañca) of these three thoughts (tiṇṇaṃ vitakkānaṃ) should be known (jānitabbaṃ).

Therein, the eight minds accompanied by greed (lobhasahagatacittāni) are the field (khettaṃ) of those connected with desire (chandūpasañhitānaṃ), the two minds accompanied by sadness (domanassasahagatāni) of those connected with aversion (dosūpasañhitānaṃ), the twelve unwholesome minds (akusalacittāni) as well of those connected with delusion (mohūpasañhitānaṃ).

But the two minds connected with doubt and agitation (vicikicchāuddhaccasampayuttacittāni) are the individual field (pāṭipuggalikaṃ khettaṃ) of these (etesaṃ).

All beings and formations (sabbesampi sattā ceva saṅkhārā ca) are the object (ārammaṇaṃ), for they arise in beings and formations that are indifferent to what is desired and not desired (iṭṭhāniṭṭhaasamapekkhitesu hi sattesu ca saṅkhāresu ca te uppajjanti).

"aññampi nimittaṃ manasikātabbaṃ kusalūpasaṃhita": moreover, another cause (aññaṃ nimittaṃ) that is connected with the wholesome (kusalūpasaṃhita) should be attended to (manasikātabbaṃ), that is, another cause (aññaṃ nimittaṃ) that is dependent on the wholesome (kusalanissitaṃ) should be attended to (manasikātabbaṃ).

Therein, "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name is, when thoughts connected with desire (chandūpasañhite vitakke) arise in beings (sattesu uppanne), the contemplation of the unattractive (asubhabhāvanā) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name.

When formations (saṅkhāresu) have arisen, the attention to impermanence (anicca-manasikāro) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name.

When aversion (dose) has arisen in beings (sattesu uppanne), the cultivation of loving-kindness (mettābhāvanā) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name.

When formations (saṅkhāresu) have arisen, attention to the elements (dhātumanasikāro) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name.

When delusion (moha) has arisen anywhere (yattha katthaci uppanne), the support of the five factors (pañcadhammūpanissayo) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name.

chandūpasaṃhitā, aññampi nimittaṃ manasikātabbaṃ kusalūpasaṃhita, aññaṃ nimittaṃ

"These hands or feet are beautiful (imassa hatthā vā sobhanā pādā vātiādinā nayena)": for in this way, when greed has arisen in beings (sattesu lobhe uppanne), one should connect it to the unattractive (asubhato upasaṃharitabbaṃ).

"In what are you passionate (kimhi sārattosi)? In the hair are you passionate (kesesu sārattosi)."

"In the body hairs...pe...in the urine are you passionate (lomesu…pe… muttesu sārattosi)."

This self (ayaṃ attabhāvo) by name is propped up by three hundred bones (tīhi aṭṭhisatehi ussāpito), bound by nine hundred tendons (navahi nhārusatehi ābaddho), smeared with nine hundred masses of flesh (navahi maṃsapesisatehi anulitto), covered with moist skin (allacammena pariyonaddho), concealed by the color of the skin (chavirāgena paṭicchanno), from nine wounds (navahi vaṇamukhehi) and ninety-nine thousand pores (navanavutilomakūpasahassehi ca) impurity flows (asuci paggharati), filled with a corpse (kuṇapapūrito), foul-smelling (duggandho), disgusting (jeguccho), repulsive (paṭikūlo), a collection of thirty-two corpses (dvattiṃsakuṇapasañcayo), there is no essence or excellence in it (natthettha sāraṃ vā varaṃ vāti)": thus, for one who connects to the unattractive (evaṃ asubhato upasaṃharantassa) the greed that has arisen in beings (sattesu uppanno lobho) is abandoned (pahīyati), therefore for him this connecting to the unattractive (tenassa asubhato upasaṃharaṇaṃ) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name (nāma hoti).

When greed has arisen in formations (saṅkhāresu) such as robes and bowls (pattacīvarādīsu), by establishing the middleness of formations through two aspects (dvīhākārehi saṅkhāramajjhattataṃ samuṭṭhāpetīti), according to the method spoken of in the commentary on the Foundations of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhānavaṇṇanāyaṃ vuttanayena), through the power of thinking according to the nature of being ownerless and temporary (assāmikatāvakālikabhāvavasena manasikaroto) that (so) is abandoned (pahīyati).

Therefore for him, attention to impermanence (tenassa aniccato manasikāro) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name (nāma hoti).

But when aversion (dose) has arisen in beings (sattesu dose uppanne), loving-kindness (mettā) should be cultivated (bhāvetabbā) through the power of the removal of hatred and the advice of the saw simile (āghātavinayakakacopamovādādīnaṃ vasena), as one cultivates that (taṃ bhāvayato) aversion (doso) is abandoned (pahīyati), therefore for him the cultivation of loving-kindness (tenassa mettābhāvanā) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name (nāma hoti).

When aversion (dose) has arisen in stumps, thorns, grass, leaves, etc. (khāṇukaṇṭakatiṇapaṇṇādīsu), "to whom do you get angry (tvaṃ kassa kuppasi)? To the earth element (kiṃ pathavīdhātuyā), or to the water element (udāhu āpodhātuyā)? Who is it that gets angry (ko vā panāyaṃ kuppati nāma)? Is it the earth element (kiṃ pathavīdhātu) or the water element (udāhu āpodhātūtiādinā nayena)": thus, for one who attends to the elements (dhātumanasikāraṃ karontassa) aversion (doso) is abandoned (pahīyati).

Therefore for him, attention to the elements (tenassa dhātumanasikāro) is "aññaṃ nimittaṃ" by name (nāma hoti).

221. “There is the case where, monks, evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire (chandūpasaṃhitāpi), aversion (dosūpasaṃhitāpi), or delusion (mohūpasaṃhitāpi) — arise in a monk while he is referring to and attending to a particular theme (nimittaṃ āgamma yaṃ nimittaṃ manasikaroto).

He should attend to another theme (aññaṃ nimittaṃ), apart from that one, connected with what is skillful (kusalūpasaṃhitaṃ). When he is attending to this other theme, apart from that one, connected with what is skillful, then those evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — are abandoned (pahīyanti) and subside (abbatthaṃ gacchanti). With their abandoning (tesaṃ pahānā), he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it (eko-di), and concentrates it (samādhiyati).

As he is scrutinizing the drawbacks (ādīnavaṃ upaparikkhato) of those thoughts (tesampi vitakkānaṃ), those evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — are abandoned and subside. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it.

As he is paying no mind and paying no attention (asatiamanasikāraṃ āpajjato) to those thoughts, those evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — are abandoned and subside. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it.

As he is attending to the relaxing of thought-fabrication (vitakkasaṅkhārasaṇṭhānaṃ manasikaroto) with regard to those thoughts, those evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — are abandoned and subside. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it.

As — with his teeth clenched (dantebhi dantaṃ ādhāya) and his tongue pressed against the roof of his mouth (jivhāya tāluṃ āhacca) — he is beating down (abhiniggaṇhato), constraining (abhinippīḷayato), and crushing (abhisantāpayato) his mind with his awareness (cetasā cittaṃ), those evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — are abandoned and subside. With their abandoning, he steadies his mind right within, settles it, unifies it, and concentrates it.

He is then called a monk with mastery (vasī) over the ways of thought sequences (vitakkapariyāyapathesu). He thinks whatever thought he wants to (yaṃ vitakkaṃ ākaṅkhissati taṃ vitakkaṃ vitakkessati), and doesn’t think whatever thought he doesn’t want to (yaṃ vitakkaṃ nākaṅkhissati na taṃ vitakkaṃ vitakkessati). He has severed craving (taṇhaṃ), thrown off the fetters (saṃyojanaṃ), and — through the right penetration of conceit (sammā mānābhisamayā) — has made an end to suffering and stress (dukkhassā’’ti)."

That is what the Blessed One (bhagavā) said. Gratified (attamanā), the monks delighted at the Blessed One’s words (bhagavato bhāsitaṃ abhinandunti).

221. "Because, monks," this is called analysis of the complete exhaustion [of defilements], and its meaning is clear.

Just as a teacher of skills, having taught the five weapons skills to a prince who came from a foreign country, sends him forth, saying, "Go, take the kingdom in your own country. If thieves arise against you on the way, go making use of the bow. If your bow is destroyed or broken, [use] the spear or the sword," thus, having shown what is to be done with each of the five weapons, he sends him off. Having done so, he goes to his own country, takes the kingdom, and experiences the glory of kingship.

In the same way, the Blessed One, sending forth a monk devoted to higher consciousness for the sake of attaining arahantship, [says:] "If unwholesome thoughts arise in him, by staying in the attention-to-another-object section, having warded them off, and having developed insight, he will attain arahantship. Not being able to [do that] there, by staying in the danger section; not being able to [do that] even there, by staying in the inattention section; not being able to [do that] even there, by staying in the thought-root-severance section; not being able to [do that] even there, by staying in the forceful-warding-off section, having warded off thoughts, and having developed insight, he will attain arahantship," thus he taught these five sections.

"Mastered in the avenues of thought analysis" means he is called one who is practiced in mastery, skilled in mastery, in the avenues of thought-wandering.

"He will think whatever thought he wants" - this was said to show the mode of his being mastered. For formerly, whatever thought he wanted to think, that he did not think; whatever he did not want to think, that he thought. But now, because he is mastered, whatever thought he wants to think, that very thought he thinks; whatever he does not want to think, that he does not think. Therefore, it was said, "He will think whatever thought he wants, that thought he will think; whatever thought he does not want, that thought he will not think."

"He has cut off craving," etc., was already said in the Sabbāsava Sutta.
