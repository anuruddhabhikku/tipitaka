304. Evaṃ me sutaṃ – ekaṃ samayaṃ bhagavā sāvatthiyaṃ viharati jetavane anāthapiṇḍikassa ārāme. Tatra kho bhagavā bhikkhū āmantesi – ‘‘bhikkhavo’’ti. ‘‘Bhadante’’ti te bhikkhū bhagavato paccassosuṃ. Bhagavā etadavoca – ‘‘saḷāyatanavibhaṅgaṃ vo, bhikkhave, desessāmi. Taṃ suṇātha, sādhukaṃ manasi karotha; bhāsissāmī’’ti. ‘‘Evaṃ, bhante’’ti kho te bhikkhū bhagavato paccassosuṃ. Bhagavā etadavoca – ‘‘‘Cha ajjhattikāni āyatanāni veditabbāni, cha bāhirāni āyatanāni veditabbāni, cha viññāṇakāyā veditabbā, cha phassakāyā veditabbā, aṭṭhārasa manopavicārā veditabbā, chattiṃsa sattapadā veditabbā, tatra idaṃ nissāya idaṃ pajahatha, tayo satipaṭṭhānā yadariyo sevati yadariyo sevamāno satthā gaṇamanusāsitumarahati, so vuccati yoggācariyānaṃ yogācariyānaṃ (ka.) anuttaro purisadammasārathī’ti – ayamuddeso saḷāyatanavibhaṅgassa. yogācariyānaṃ (ka.)
Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was dwelling at Anāthapindika’s monastery, in the Jeta Grove, near Sāvatthi.

There the Fortunate One addressed the monks (saying): “Monks!”

“Venerable sir,” they replied.

The Blessed One said this: “Bhikkhus, I shall teach you an exposition of the sixfold base (ṣaḷāyatanavibhaṅgaṃ).

Listen & pay close attention. I will speak.”

“Yes, venerable sir,” the bhikkhus replied.

The Blessed One said this:

“The six internal bases (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) should be understood. The six external bases (bāhirāni āyatanāni) should be understood. The six classes of consciousness (viññāṇakāyā) should be understood. The six classes of contact (phassakāyā) should be understood. The eighteen kinds of mental exploration (manopavicārā) should be understood. The thirty-six positions of beings (sattapadā) should be understood. Therein, by depending on this, abandon that. There are three foundations of mindfulness (satipaṭṭhānā) that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which the Noble One is a teacher (satthā) fit to instruct a group (gaṇa), and is called the unsurpassed guide (anuttaro purisadammasārathī) of those who practice yoga (yoggācariyānaṃ)” – this is a summary (uddeso) of the exposition of the sixfold base (saḷāyatanavibhaṅgassa).
304.Evaṃme sutanti saḷāyatanavibhaṅgasuttaṃ. Tattha veditabbānīti sahavipassanena maggena jānitabbāni. Manopavicārāti vitakkavicārā. Vitakkuppādakañhi mano idha manoti adhippetaṃ, manassa upavicārāti manopavicārā. Sattapadāti vaṭṭavivaṭṭanissitānaṃ sattānaṃ padā. Ettha hi aṭṭhārasa vaṭṭapadā nāma, aṭṭhārasa vivaṭṭapadā nāma, tepi sahavipassanena maggeneva veditabbā. Yoggācariyānanti hatthiyoggādiācārasikkhāpakānaṃ, dametabbadamakānanti attho. Sesaṃ vibhaṅgeyeva āvibhavissati. Ayamuddesoti idaṃ mātikāṭṭhapanaṃ.
Thus it is said, "Thus I have heard," the Saḷāyatanavibhaṅgasutta.

There, veditabbānī means they should be known by the path together with insight.

Manopavicārā means vitakkavicārā.

For here, mind is intended as that which produces thought; manassa upavicārāti manopavicārā.

Sattapadā means the paths of beings dependent on the round and the reversal [of rebirth].

Here, there are eighteen paths of the round, and eighteen paths of the reversal; those, too, should be known by the path together with insight.

Yoggācariyāna means those who train in the practices of elephant training and so forth; the meaning is those who tame what should be tamed.

The rest will become clear in the analysis itself.

Ayamuddeso means this is the establishment of the matrix.

305. ‘“The six internal bases (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) should be understood.”’ So it was said.

And in reference to what was it said? ‘The eye-base (cakkhāyatana), the ear-base (sotāyatana), the nose-base (ghānāyatana), the tongue-base (jivhāyatana), the body-base (kāyāyatana), and the mind-base (manāyatana) – the six internal bases (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘“The six external bases (bāhirāni āyatanāni) should be understood.”’ So it was said.

And in reference to what was it said? ‘The form-base (rūpāyatana), the sound-base (saddāyatana), the odour-base (gandhāyatana), the flavour-base (rasāyatana), the tangible-base (phoṭṭhabbāyatana), and the mind-object-base (dhammāyatana) – the six external bases (bāhirāni āyatanāni) should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘“The six classes of consciousness (viññāṇakāyā) should be understood.”’ So it was said.

And in reference to what was it said? ‘Eye-consciousness (cakkhuviññāṇa), ear-consciousness (sotaviññāṇa), nose-consciousness (ghānaviññāṇa), tongue-consciousness (jivhāviññāṇa), body-consciousness (kāyaviññāṇa), and mind-consciousness (manoviññāṇa) – the six classes of consciousness (viññāṇakāyā) should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘“The six classes of contact (phassakāyā) should be understood.”’ So it was said.

And in reference to what was it said? ‘Eye-contact (cakkhusamphasso), ear-contact (sotasamphasso), nose-contact (ghānasamphasso), tongue-contact (jivhāsamphasso), body-contact (kāyasamphasso), and mind-contact (manosamphasso) – the six classes of contact (phassakāyā) should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

‘“The eighteen kinds of mental exploration (manopavicārā) should be understood.”’ So it was said.

And in reference to what was it said? ‘On seeing a form (rūpaṃ) with the eye (cakkhunā), one explores a form productive of joy (somanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ rūpaṃ upavicarati), one explores a form productive of grief (domanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ rūpaṃ upavicarati), one explores a form productive of equanimity (upekkhāṭṭhānīyaṃ rūpaṃ upavicarati).

On hearing a sound (saddaṃ) with the ear (sotena)… On smelling an odour (gandhaṃ) with the nose (ghānena)… On tasting a flavour (rasaṃ) with the tongue (jivhāya)… On touching a tangible (phoṭṭhabbaṃ) with the body (kāyena)… On cognizing a mind-object (dhammaṃ) with the mind (manasā), one explores a mind-object productive of joy (somanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ dhammaṃ upavicarati), one explores a mind-object productive of grief (domanassaṭṭhānīyaṃ dhammaṃ upavicarati), one explores a mind-object productive of equanimity (upekkhāṭṭhānīyaṃ dhammaṃ upavicarati).

Thus there are six kinds of exploration with joy (somanassūpavicārā), six kinds of exploration with grief (domanassūpavicārā), and six kinds of exploration with equanimity (upekkhūpavicārā). The eighteen kinds of mental exploration (manopavicārā) should be understood.’ So it was with reference to this that it was said.

306. “‘The thirty-six positions of beings should be understood.’ So it was said.

What was said with reference to what? Six kinds of joy based on the household life, six kinds of joy based on renunciation, six kinds of grief based on the household life, six kinds of grief based on renunciation, six kinds of equanimity based on the household life, six kinds of equanimity based on renunciation.

What are the six kinds of joy based on the household life? When one regards as an acquisition the acquisition of forms cognizable by the eye that are wished for, desired, agreeable, gratifying, and associated with worldliness—or when one recalls what was formerly acquired that has passed, ceased, and changed—joy (somanassaṃ) arises. Such joy (somanassaṃ) as this is called joy based on the household life (gehasitaṃ somanassaṃ).

When one regards as an acquisition the acquisition of sounds cognizable by the ear…the acquisition of odours cognizable by the nose…the acquisition of flavours cognizable by the tongue…the acquisition of tangibles cognizable by the body…the acquisition of mind-objects cognizable by the mind that are wished for, desired, agreeable, gratifying…joy (somanassaṃ) arises. Such joy (somanassaṃ) as this is called joy based on the household life (gehasitaṃ somanassaṃ). These are the six kinds of joy based on the household life (gehasitāni somanassāni).

What are the six kinds of joy based on renunciation (nekkhammasitāni somanassāni)? When, by knowing the impermanence (aniccatā), change, fading away (virāga), and cessation (nirodha) of forms, one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom (sammappaññā) that forms both formerly and now are all impermanent (aniccā), suffering (dukkhā), and subject to change, joy (somanassaṃ) arises. Such joy (somanassaṃ) as this is called joy based on renunciation (nekkhammasitaṃ somanassaṃ).

When, by knowing the impermanence (aniccatā), change, fading away (virāga), and cessation (nirodha) of sounds…of odours…of flavours…of tangibles…of mind-objects, one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom (sammappaññā) that mind-objects both formerly and now are all impermanent (aniccā), suffering (dukkhā), and subject to change, joy (somanassaṃ) arises. Such joy (somanassaṃ) as this is called joy based on renunciation (nekkhammasitaṃ somanassaṃ). These are the six kinds of joy based on renunciation (nekkhammasitāni somanassāni).

310. Nānattā means various, many, of diverse kinds.

Nānattasitā means based on diverse objects.

Ekattā means one.

Ekattasitā means based on a single object.

But what is this equanimity? A lower ignorance-equanimity was stated earlier; above, the six-membered equanimity will be stated; here, two kinds of equanimity are taken: serenity-equanimity and insight-equanimity.

There, since one equanimity is in forms, and another in sounds, etc., for the equanimity that is in form does not exist in sounds, etc.

And equanimity in form makes form itself the object, not sounds, etc.

And, other than equanimity in form, one is serenity-equanimity, having made the earth kasina the object, arises; another, the water kasina, etc.

Therefore, distinguishing diversity and what is based on diversity, he said, "There is, monks, equanimity in forms," etc.

Since, however, there are not two or three or the sphere of infinite space, or the sphere of infinite consciousness, etc., therefore, distinguishing oneness and what is based on oneness, he said, "There is, monks, equanimity based on the sphere of infinite space," etc.

There is, monks, equanimity in forms. There is, monks, equanimity based on the sphere of infinite space.

There, the equanimity of the sphere of infinite space is based on the sphere of infinite space in the sense of association; the insight-equanimity of one contemplating the aggregate of the sphere of infinite space is based on the sphere of infinite space in the sense of object.

The same rule applies to the others as well.

"Abandon that" here causes the abandonment of the fine-material-sphere equanimity by the immaterial-sphere-absorption equanimity, and the abandonment of the fine-material-sphere insight-equanimity by the immaterial-sphere insight-equanimity.

Abandon that.

Atammayata—here, tammayata means craving; atammayatā is said to be the insight leading to relinquishment because of its completion.

"Abandon that" here causes the abandonment of both immaterial-sphere-absorption equanimity and insight-equanimity by the insight leading to relinquishment.

311. ‘‘‘There are three satipaṭṭhānas (foundations of mindfulness) that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which the Teacher is fit to instruct a group’—so it was said. And with reference to what was this said? Here, bhikkhus, the Teacher teaches the dharma to the disciples, compassionate, seeking their welfare, undertaking compassion, saying: ‘This is for your welfare; this is for your happiness.’

His disciples do not wish to hear, they do not lend an ear, they do not establish their minds with understanding, and they behave turning away from the Teacher's instruction.

With that, bhikkhus, the Tathāgata is neither unsatisfied, nor does he feel dissatisfaction; yet he dwells detached, mindful, and fully aware. This, bhikkhus, is the first satipaṭṭhāna that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which the Teacher is fit to instruct a group.

‘‘Furthermore, bhikkhus, the Teacher teaches the dharma to the disciples, compassionate, seeking their welfare, undertaking compassion, saying: ‘This is for your welfare; this is for your happiness.’

Some of his disciples do not wish to hear, they do not lend an ear, they do not establish their minds with understanding, and they behave turning away from the Teacher's instruction; some of his disciples wish to hear, they lend an ear, they establish their minds with understanding, and they do not behave turning away from the Teacher's instruction.

With that, bhikkhus, the Tathāgata is neither unsatisfied, nor does he feel dissatisfaction; and he is neither satisfied, nor does he feel satisfaction. Remaining free from both dissatisfaction and satisfaction, he dwells in equanimity, mindful, and fully aware. This is called, bhikkhus, the second satipaṭṭhāna that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which the Teacher is fit to instruct a group.

‘‘Furthermore, bhikkhus, the Teacher teaches the dharma to the disciples, compassionate, seeking their welfare, undertaking compassion, saying: ‘This is for your welfare; this is for your happiness.’

His disciples wish to hear, they lend an ear, they establish their minds with understanding, and they do not behave turning away from the Teacher's instruction.

With that, bhikkhus, the Tathāgata is satisfied and feels satisfaction; yet he dwells detached, mindful, and fully aware. This is called, bhikkhus, the third satipaṭṭhāna that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which the Teacher is fit to instruct a group.

‘There are three satipaṭṭhānas that the Noble One cultivates, cultivating which the Teacher is fit to instruct a group’—so it was said; this was said with reference to this.

“So it is said: ‘Among the trainers of those who are fit to be trained, he is the unsurpassed charioteer of persons to be tamed.’ It is because of this that this was said.

But in regard to what was it said? “Guided by the elephant tamer, bhikkhus, the elephant to be tamed goes in one direction only—east, west, north, or south. Guided by the horse tamer, bhikkhus, the horse to be tamed goes in one direction only—east, west, north, or south. Guided by the ox tamer, bhikkhus, the ox to be tamed goes in one direction only—east, west, north, or south. Bhikkhus, guided by the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened one, the person to be tamed courses in eight directions.

Possessed of material form, he sees forms: this is the first direction (disā). Not perceiving forms internally, he sees forms externally: this is the second direction. He is resolved only upon the beautiful: this is the third direction. With the complete surmounting of perceptions of form (rūpa-saṃjñā), with the disappearance of perceptions of sensory impact (paṭigha-saṃjñā), with non-attention to perceptions of diversity (nānāttasaṃjñā), aware that ‘space is infinite’ (ananto ākāso), he enters upon and abides in the base of infinite space (ākāsānañcāyatana): this is the fourth direction. With the complete surmounting of the base of infinite space, aware that ‘consciousness is infinite’ (anantaṃ viññāṇaṃ), he enters upon and abides in the base of infinite consciousness (viññāṇañcāyatana): this is the fifth direction. With the complete surmounting of the base of infinite consciousness, aware that ‘there is nothing’ (natthi kiñcī), he enters upon and abides in the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana): this is the sixth direction. With the complete surmounting of the base of nothingness, he enters upon and abides in the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana): this is the seventh direction. With the complete surmounting of the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, he enters upon and abides in the cessation of perception and feeling (saññāvedayitanirodha): this is the eighth direction. Bhikkhus, guided by the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened one, the person to be tamed courses in these eight directions. So it was with reference to this that it was said: ‘Among the trainers of those who are fit to be trained, he is the unsurpassed charioteer of persons to be tamed.’”
