Thus have I heard.

On one occasion the Blessed One was living at Vesālī in the Great Wood in the Hall with the Peaked Roof. Now on that occasion a number of bhikkhus had declared final knowledge in the presence of the Blessed One thus: “'Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of being,' thus we understand.”

Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, heard: “A number of bhikkhus, it seems, have declared final knowledge in the presence of the Blessed One thus: 'Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of being,' thus we understand.”

Then Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, went to the Blessed One, and after paying homage to him, he sat down at one side.

Seated to one side, Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, said to the Blessed One: “I have heard, venerable sir, that a number of bhikkhus have declared final knowledge in the presence of the Blessed One, saying: 'Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of being,' thus we understand.”

“Those bhikkhus, venerable sir, who in the presence of the Blessed One declared final knowledge, saying: 'Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of being,' thus we understand—did those bhikkhus rightly declare final knowledge, or are there some bhikkhus here who declared final knowledge out of conceit?”

56. “When those bhikkhus, Sunakkhatta, declared final knowledge in my presence, saying, ‘Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more for this state of being,’

there were some bhikkhus who declared final knowledge rightly, and there were some bhikkhus who declared final knowledge because they overestimated themselves.

Therein, when bhikkhus declare final knowledge rightly, their declaration is true. But when bhikkhus declare final knowledge because they overestimate themselves, the Tathāgata thinks: ‘Let me teach them the Dharma.’

Thus it is in this case, Sunakkhatta, that the Tathāgata thinks: ‘Let me teach them the Dharma.’

But some misguided men here formulate a question, come to the Tathāgata, and ask it.

In that case, Sunakkhatta, though the Tathāgata has thought: ‘Let me teach them the Dharma,’ he changes his mind.”

“This is the time, Bhagavā, this is the time, Sugata, for the Bhagavā to teach the Dharma.

Having heard it from the Bhagavā, the bhikkhus will remember it.”

“Then listen, Sunakkhatta, and attend closely to what I shall say; I will speak.”

“Yes, venerable sir,” Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, replied to the Bhagavā.

The Bhagavā said this:

<p n="58" rend="bodytext"><hi rend="paranum">58</hi><hi rend="dot">.</hi> ‘‘Ṭhānaṃ kho panetaṃ, sunakkhatta, vijjati yaṃ idhekacco purisapuggalo <pb ed="T" n="3.0063"/> lokāmisādhimutto assa. Lokāmisādhimuttassa kho, sunakkhatta, purisapuggalassa tappatirūpī ceva kathā saṇṭhāti, tadanudhammañca anuvitakketi, anuvicāreti, tañca purisaṃ bhajati, tena ca vittiṃ āpajjati; āneñjapaṭisaṃyuttāya ca pana kathāya kacchamānāya na sussūsati, na sotaṃ odahati, na aññā cittaṃ upaṭṭhāpeti <note>upaṭṭhapeti (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.)</note>, na ca taṃ purisaṃ bhajati, na ca tena vittiṃ āpajjati. 58. “It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that some person here may be intent on worldly material things (lokāmisādhimutto).

When a person is intent on worldly material things, only talk concerning that interests him, and his thinking and pondering are in line with that, and he associates with that kind of person, and he finds satisfaction through him. But when talk about the imperturbable is going on, he will not listen to it or give it ear or exert his mind to understand it. He does not associate with that kind of person, and he does not find satisfaction through him.

“Suppose, Sunakkhatta, a man had left his own village or town a long time ago.

and he were to see another man who had only recently left that village or town.

He would ask that man whether the people of that village or town were safe, prosperous, and healthy, and that man would tell him whether the people of that village or town were safe, prosperous, and healthy.

What do you think, Sunakkhatta? Would that first man listen to him, give him ear, and exert his mind to understand?” “Yes, venerable sir.”

“So too, Sunakkhatta, it is possible that some person here may be intent on worldly material things (lokāmisādhimutto).

When a person is intent on worldly material things, only talk concerning that interests him, and his thinking and pondering are in line with that, and he associates with that kind of person, and he finds satisfaction through him. But when talk about the imperturbable is going on, he will not listen to it or give it ear or exert his mind to understand it. He does not associate with that kind of person, and he does not find satisfaction through him.

He should be understood thus: ‘The person who is intent on worldly material things is disconnected from the bond of the imperturbable.’

59. Moreover, Sunakkhatta, it is possible that here some person is intent on the imperturbable.

When a person is intent on the imperturbable, only talk concerning that interests him, and his thinking and pondering are in line with that, and he associates with that kind of person, and he finds satisfaction through him. But when talk about worldly material things is going on, he will not listen to it or give it ear or exert his mind to understand it. He does not associate with that kind of person, and he does not find satisfaction through him.

“Just as a yellow leaf that has fallen from its stalk is incapable of becoming green again, so too, Sunakkhatta, when a person is intent on the imperturbable he has shed the fetter of worldly material things.

He should be understood as a person not bound by the fetter of worldly material things who is intent on the imperturbable.

“It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that some person here may be intent on the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanādhimutto).

When a person is intent on the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanādhimuttassa), only talk concerning that interests him, and his thinking and pondering are in line with that, and he associates with that kind of person, and he finds satisfaction through him. But when talk about the imperturbable (āneñjapaṭisaṃyuttāya) is going on, he will not listen to it or give it ear or exert his mind to understand it, he does not associate with that kind of person, and he does not find satisfaction through him.

“Just as a thick stone that has split in two cannot be joined together again, so too, Sunakkhatta, when a person is intent on the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanādhimuttassa) his fetter of the imperturbable (āneñjasaṃyojane) has been split.

He should be understood as a person not bound by the fetter of the imperturbable (āneñjasaṃyojanena) who is intent on the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanādhimutto).

61. It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that some person here may be intent on the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana).

When a person is intent on the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, Sunakkhatta, only talk concerning that interests him, and his thinking and pondering are in line with that, and he associates with that kind of person, and he finds satisfaction through him. But when talk about the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana) is going on, he will not listen to it or give it ear or exert his mind to understand it. He does not associate with that kind of person, and he does not find satisfaction through him.

Suppose a person has eaten some delicious food and thrown it up.

What do you think, Sunakkhatta, would that person still have any desire for that food he threw up?

"No, venerable sir."

"What is the reason for that?"

"Because, venerable sir, that food is considered repulsive."

"In the same way, Sunakkhatta, whatever connections to the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana) a person intent on the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana) has, are like vomit to him.

He should be understood thus: 'This person is detached from the connections to the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatana), and is intent on the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana).'"

When a person is completely intent on Nirvāṇa (nibbāna), only talk concerning that interests him, and his thinking and pondering are in line with that, and he associates with that kind of person, and he finds satisfaction through him. He should be understood as a person not bound by the fetter of the base of neither-perception-nor-non- perception who is completely intent on Nirvāṇa (nibbāna).

65. “Sunakkhatta, I have given this simile in order to convey a meaning.

This is the meaning here: ‘Wound’ (vaṇa) is a term for the six internal bases (ajjhattikānaṃ āyatanānaṃ). ‘Poisonous humour’ (visadosa) is a term for ignorance (avijjā). ‘Arrow’ (salla) is a term for craving (taṇhā). ‘Probe’ (esanī) is a term for mindfulness (sati). ‘Knife’ (sattha) is a term for noble wisdom (ariyā paññā). Surgeon (bhisakko sallakatto) is a term for the Tathāgata, the Accomplished One (arahato), the Fully Enlightened One (sammāsambuddha).

“When, Sunakkhatta, a bhikkhu practises restraint in the six bases of contact (phassāyatanesu), and having understood that attachment (upadhi) is the root (mūla) of suffering (dukkha), is without attachment (nirupadhi), liberated (vimutto) by the destruction of attachment, it is not possible that he would direct his body (kāyaṃ upasaṃharissati) or arouse his mind (cittaṃ uppādessati) towards any object of attachment.

“Suppose, Sunakkhatta, there were a bronze cup of beverage (āpānīyakaṃso) possessing a good colour (vaṇṇasampanno), smell (gandhasampanno), and taste (rasasampanno), but it was mixed with poison (visena saṃsaṭṭho).

“And a man (puriso) came who wanted to live (jīvitukāmo), not to die (amaritukāmo), who wanted pleasure (sukhakāmo) and recoiled from pain (dukkhapaṭikūlo).

What do you think (taṃ kiṃ maññasi), Sunakkhatta, would that man drink (piveyya) that beverage cup (āpānīyakaṃsaṃ), knowing (jaññā) ‘If I drink (pivitvā) this, I will incur death (maraṇaṃ nigacchāmi) or deadly suffering (maraṇamattaṃ dukkha)?’” “No, venerable sir (bhante).”

“So too, Sunakkhatta, when a bhikkhu practises restraint in the six bases of contact (phassāyatanesu), and having understood that attachment (upadhi) is the root (mūla) of suffering (dukkha), is without attachment (nirupadhi), liberated (vimutto) by the destruction of attachment, it is not possible that he would direct his body (kāyaṃ upasaṃharissati) or arouse his mind (cittaṃ uppādessati) towards any object of attachment.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, [there were] a powerful snake (āsīviso) with terrifying poison (ghoraviso).

And a man (puriso) came who wanted to live (jīvitukāmo), not to die (amaritukāmo), who wanted pleasure (sukhakāmo) and recoiled from pain (dukkhapaṭikūlo).

What do you think (taṃ kiṃ maññasi), Sunakkhatta, would that man give (dajjā) that deadly poisonous snake (āsīvisassa ghoravisassa) his hand (hatthaṃ) or his thumb (aṅguṭṭhaṃ), knowing (jaññā) ‘If I am bitten (daṭṭho) by him, I will incur death (maraṇaṃ nigacchāmi) or deadly suffering (maraṇamattaṃ dukkha)?’” “No, venerable sir (bhante).”

“So too, Sunakkhatta, when a bhikkhu practises restraint in the six bases of contact (phassāyatanesu), and having understood that attachment (upadhi) is the root (mūla) of suffering (dukkha), is without attachment (nirupadhi), liberated (vimutto) by the destruction of attachment, it is not possible that he would direct his body (kāyaṃ upasaṃharissati) or arouse his mind (cittaṃ uppādessati) towards any object of attachment.”

The Blessed One (bhagavā) said this. Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis (sunakkhatto licchaviputto), was satisfied and delighted in the Blessed One’s words (bhagavato bhāsitaṃ abhinandīti).

The Sunakkhatta Sutta is finished, the fifth.
