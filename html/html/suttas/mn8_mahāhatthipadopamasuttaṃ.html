300. Evaṃ me sutaṃ – ekaṃ samayaṃ bhagavā sāvatthiyaṃ viharati jetavane anāthapiṇḍikassa ārāme. Tatra kho āyasmā sāriputto bhikkhū āmantesi – ‘‘āvuso bhikkhave’’ti. ‘‘Āvuso’’ti kho te bhikkhū āyasmato sāriputtassa paccassosuṃ. Āyasmā sāriputto etadavoca – ‘‘seyyathāpi, āvuso, yāni kānici jaṅgalānaṃ pāṇānaṃ padajātāni sabbāni tāni hatthipade samodhānaṃ gacchanti, hatthipadaṃ tesaṃ aggamakkhāyati yadidaṃ mahantattena; evameva kho, āvuso, ye keci kusalā dhammā sabbete catūsu ariyasaccesu saṅgahaṃ gacchanti. Katamesu catūsu? Dukkhe ariyasacce , dukkhasamudaye ariyasacce, dukkhanirodhe ariyasacce, dukkhanirodhagāminiyā paṭipadāya ariyasacce’’.
Thus have I heard:

On one occasion the Blessed One (bhagavā) was living near Sāvatthī in Jeta's Grove, Anāthapindika's Park (jetavane anāthapiṇḍikassa ārāme).

There the venerable Sāriputta addressed the monks, saying, "Friends, monks!"

"Yes, friend," the monks replied to the venerable Sāriputta.

The venerable Sāriputta said this: "Just as, friends, the footprints of all living beings that walk can be gathered into an elephant's footprint, and the elephant's footprint is declared the chief of them because of its great size; so too, all skillful qualities (kusalā dhammā) are gathered into the four noble truths (catūsu ariyasaccesu).

Which four? The noble truth of suffering (dukkhe ariyasacce), the noble truth of the origin of suffering (dukkhasamudaye ariyasacce), the noble truth of the cessation of suffering (dukkhanirodhe ariyasacce), and the noble truth of the path leading to the cessation of suffering (dukkhanirodhagāminiyā paṭipadāya ariyasacce)."
300.Evaṃme sutanti mahāhatthipadopamasuttaṃ. Tattha jaṅgalānanti pathavītalacārīnaṃ. Pāṇānanti sapādakapāṇānaṃ. Padajātānīti padāni. Samodhānaṃ gacchantīti odhānaṃ pakkhepaṃ gacchanti. Aggamakkhāyatīti seṭṭhaṃ akkhāyati. Yadidaṃ mahantattenāti mahantabhāvena aggaṃ akkhāyati, na guṇavasenāti attho. Ye keci kusalā dhammāti ye keci lokiyā vā lokuttarā vā kusalā dhammā. Saṅgahaṃ gacchantīti ettha catubbidho saṅgaho – sajātisaṅgaho, sañjātisaṅgaho, kiriyasaṅgaho, gaṇanasaṅgahoti. Tattha ‘‘sabbe khattiyā āgacchantu sabbe brāhmaṇā’’ti evaṃ samānajātivasena saṅgaho sajātisaṅgaho nāma. ‘‘Sabbe kosalakā sabbe māgadhakā’’ti evaṃ sañjātidesavasena saṅgaho sañjātisaṅgaho nāma. ‘‘Sabbe rathikā sabbe dhanuggahā’’ti evaṃ kiriyavasena saṅgaho kiriyasaṅgaho nāma. ‘‘Cakkhāyatanaṃ katamakkhandhagaṇanaṃ gacchatīti? Cakkhāyatanaṃ rūpakkhandhagaṇanaṃ gacchati. Hañci cakkhāyatanaṃ rūpakkhandhagaṇanaṃ gacchati, tena vata re vattabbe cakkhāyatanaṃ rūpakkhandhena saṅgahita’’nti (kathā. 471), ayaṃ gaṇanasaṅgaho nāma. Imasmimpi ṭhāne ayameva adhippeto. Nanu ca ‘‘catunnaṃ ariyasaccānaṃ kati kusalā kati akusalā kati abyākatāti pañhassa vissajjane samudayasaccaṃ akusalaṃ, maggasaccaṃ kusalaṃ, nirodhasaccaṃ abyākataṃ, dukkhasaccaṃ siyā kusalaṃ, siyā akusalaṃ, siyā abyākata’’nti (vibha. 216-217) āgatattā catubhūmakampi kusalaṃ diyaḍḍhameva saccaṃ bhajati. Atha kasmā mahāthero catūsu ariyasaccesu gaṇanaṃ gacchatīti āhāti? Saccānaṃ antogadhattā. Yathā hi ‘‘sādhikamidaṃ, bhikkhave, diyaḍḍhasikkhāpadasataṃ anvaddhamāsaṃ uddesaṃ āgacchati, yattha attakāmā kulaputtā sikkhanti. Tisso imā, bhikkhave, sikkhā adhisīlasikkhā adhicittasikkhā adhipaññāsikkhā’’ti (a. ni. 3.88) ettha sādhikamidaṃ diyaḍḍhasikkhāpadasataṃ ekā adhisīlasikkhāva hoti, taṃ sikkhantopi tisso sikkhā sikkhatīti dassito, sikkhānaṃ antogadhattā. Yathā ca ekassa hatthipadassa catūsu koṭṭhāsesu ekasmiṃ koṭṭhāse otiṇṇānipi dvīsu tīsu catūsu koṭṭhāsesu otiṇṇānipi siṅgālasasamigādīnaṃ pādāni hatthipade samodhānaṃ gatāneva honti. Hatthipadato amuccitvā tasseva antogadhattā. Evameva ekasmimpi dvīsupi tīsupi catūsupi saccesu gaṇanaṃ gatā dhammā catūsu saccesu gaṇanaṃ gatāva honti; saccānaṃ antogadhattāti diyaḍḍhasaccagaṇanaṃ gatepi kusaladhamme ‘‘sabbe te catūsu ariyasaccesu saṅgahaṃ gacchantī’’ti āha. ‘‘Dukkhe ariyasacce’’tiādīsu uddesapadesu ceva jātipi dukkhātiādīsu niddesapadesu ca yaṃ vattabbaṃ, taṃ visuddhimagge vuttameva. Kevalaṃ panettha desanānukkamova veditabbo.
300. Thus.

"Thus I have heard": the Mahāhatthipadopama Sūtra.

There, jaṅgalāna means those who walk on the surface of the earth.

Pāṇāna means those living beings with feet.

Padajātānī means footprints.

Samodhānaṃ gacchantī means they go to a place of meeting or combination.

Aggamakkhāyatī means it declares the best.

Yadidaṃ mahantattenā means it declares greatness through greatness, not through virtue. This is the meaning.

Ye keci kusalā dhammā means whatever wholesome things, whether mundane (lokiya) or supramundane (lokuttara), are wholesome things.

Saṅgahaṃ gacchantī means here there are four kinds of collection: collection of the same kind (sajātisaṅgaho), collection of the same origin (sañjātisaṅgaho), collection of the same activity (kiriyasaṅgaho), collection by reckoning (gaṇanasaṅgaho).

There, "May all the kṣatriyas come, may all the brāhmaṇas come": thus, collection by way of having the same birth is called sajātisaṅgaho.

"May all those from Kosala come, may all those from Magadha come": thus, collection by way of having the same place of origin is called sañjātisaṅgaho.

"May all the charioteers come, may all the archers come": thus, collection by way of having the same activity is called kiriyasaṅgaho.

"To which aggregate of elements does the eye-base belong? The eye-base belongs to the aggregate of the element of form.

If the eye-base belongs to the aggregate of the element of form, then should it be said that the eye-base is included in the aggregate of the element of form?"

471. This is called gaṇanasaṅgaho.

In this place also, this same is intended.

Now, concerning "How many of the four noble truths are wholesome, how many are unwholesome, how many are indeterminate?", in answer to the question, the truth of the origin (samudayasatya) is unwholesome, the truth of the path (mārgasatya) is wholesome, the truth of cessation (nirodhasatya) is indeterminate, the truth of suffering (duḥkhasatya) may be wholesome, may be unwholesome, may be indeterminate.

Because it was said, even one who is in the fourth stage of meditation cultivates only half of the truth that is wholesome.

Now, why does the great elder belong to the reckoning among the four noble truths? Because the truths are contained within each other.

Just as, "This collection of more than one hundred and fifty rules of training, monks, comes up for recitation every half-month, where sons of good families train themselves,"

"These three trainings, monks, are the training in higher morality (adhiśīla-śikṣā), the training in higher mind (adhicitta-śikṣā), the training in higher wisdom (adhiprajñā-śikṣā),"

Here, that collection of more than one hundred and fifty rules of training is only one training in higher morality; one who trains in that, too, is shown to train in the three trainings, because the trainings are contained within each other.

Just as the feet of the siṅgāla, the samiga, etc., even if they have stepped down in one, two, three, or four compartments of one elephant's footprint, have gone to meeting in the elephant's footprint itself.

Without being released from the elephant's footprint, because it is contained within that itself.

Just so, the phenomena that have gone to reckoning in one, two, three, or four truths have gone to reckoning in the four truths themselves; because the truths are contained within each other, even the wholesome phenomena that have gone to reckoning in the half-truth, "All of them go to collection in the four noble truths," he said.

Whatever has to be said in the descriptive passages such as "In the noble truth of suffering" etc., and in the definitive passages such as birth is suffering etc., that has already been said in the Visuddhimagga.

However, here only the order of exposition should be understood.

301. ‘‘Katamañcāvuso, dukkhaṃ ariyasaccaṃ? Jātipi dukkhā, jarāpi dukkhā, maraṇampi dukkhaṃ, sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsāpi dukkhā, yampicchaṃ na labhati tampi dukkhaṃ; saṃkhittena, pañcupādānakkhandhā dukkhā. Katame cāvuso, pañcupādānakkhandhā? Seyyathidaṃ – rūpupādānakkhandho, vedanupādānakkhandho, saññupādānakkhandho, saṅkhārupādānakkhandho, viññāṇupādānakkhandho. ‘‘Katamo cāvuso, rūpupādānakkhandho? Cattāri ca mahābhūtāni, catunnañca mahābhūtānaṃ upādāya rūpaṃ. ‘‘Katamā cāvuso, cattāro mahābhūtā? Pathavīdhātu, āpodhātu , tejodhātu, vāyodhātu.
301. “And what, friend, is the noble truth of suffering? Birth (jāti) is suffering, aging (jarā) is suffering, death (maraṇa) is suffering; sorrow (śoka), lamentation (parideva), pain (duḥkha), grief (daurmanasya), and despair (upāyāsa) are suffering; not to obtain what one wants is suffering; in brief, the five aggregates of clinging (pañcupādānakkhandhā) are suffering.

And what, friend, are the five aggregates of clinging (pañcupādānakkhandhā)? They are as follows: the form aggregate of clinging (rūpupādānakkhandha), the feeling aggregate of clinging (vedanupādānakkhandha), the perception aggregate of clinging (saññupādānakkhandha), the mental formations aggregate of clinging (saṅkhārupādānakkhandha), and the consciousness aggregate of clinging (viññāṇupādānakkhandha).

“And what, friend, is the form aggregate of clinging (rūpupādānakkhandha)? It is the four great elements (cattāri mahābhūtāni), and form (rūpa) derived from the four great elements (cattāri mahābhūtāni).

“And what, friend, are the four great elements (cattāro mahābhūtā)? They are the earth element (pathavīdhātu), the water element (āpodhātu), the fire element (tejodhātu), and the air element (vāyodhātu).

Tato yathā so vilīvakāro cattāro koṭṭhāse ṭhapetvā ekaṃ bhāgaṃ gahetvā pañcadhā phālesi, evaṃ thero cattāro arūpakkhandhe ṭhapetvā rūpakkhandhaṃ vibhajanto cattāri ca mahābhūtāni catunnañca mahābhūtānaṃ upādāya rūpanti pañcadhā akāsi. Just as a clever reed-worker, having obtained well-grown bamboo, cuts it into four parts, then puts aside three of those parts and, taking one, splits it into five, then putting aside four, taking one and splitting it, would make five strips; then putting aside four, taking one and splitting the belly part and the back part in two, putting aside the back part and taking the belly part, would make various kinds of reed-work from it such as boxes, seed-sprouts, sheaths, etc. One should not say that he will not use that back part, and the other four strips, and the other four parts, and the other three parts for work.

But it is not possible to use them with a single blow, he will use them successively.

Just so, this great elder also, like a reed-worker, having obtained well-grown bamboo, like the four parts, having begun this great discourse, established the matrix in terms of the Four Noble Truths.

Like the reed-worker putting aside three parts and taking one, making it into five, so the elder, putting aside three Noble Truths and taking one, the Truth of Suffering, dividing it, makes it into five in terms of the aggregates.

Then, just as that reed-worker, putting aside four parts, taking one part, splits it into five, so the elder, putting aside four immaterial aggregates, dividing the material aggregate, made five divisions, saying "the four great elements and form derived from the four great elements."

Then, just as that reed-worker, putting aside four strips, taking one, splits the belly part and the back part in two, so the elder, putting aside derived form and the three elements, dividing one, the earth element, showed it in two ways, internal and external.

Just as that reed-worker, putting aside the back part and taking the belly part, made various kinds of reed-work, so the elder, putting aside the external earth element, in order to show the internal earth element by dividing it into twenty aspects, said "What, friend, is the internal earth element?" and so on.

Just as the reed-worker will successively use the back part and the other four strips, and the other four parts, and the other three parts for work, it is not possible to use them with a single blow, so the elder also will successively show by dividing the external earth element and the other three elements, and derived form, and the other four immaterial aggregates, and the other three Noble Truths; it is not possible to show them with a single blow.

Moreover, this order should be understood by the simile of the king's sons -

It is said that there was a great king who had more than a thousand sons.

He placed their ornaments and equipment in four baskets and gave them to his eldest son: "Dear son, this is the ornaments and goods of your brothers; when the right time comes and they ask you, saying, 'Give us ornaments,' you should give them."

He replied, "Good, O king," and stored them in the inner chamber. Then on a festive day, the king's sons went to the king and said, "Give us ornaments, father, we will play at the festival."

"Dear sons, I have placed the ornaments in the hands of your eldest brother; have them brought and put them on."

They agreed, saying "Good," went to him, and said, "It is said that the ornaments and goods are in your hands; give them to us."

He opened the chamber, thinking, "I will do so," and bringing out four baskets, putting aside three and opening one, then bringing out five caskets, putting aside four and opening one, then when five boxes had been brought out, putting aside four and opening one, putting the lid aside, then he brought out and gave various kinds of ornaments, such as hand-ornaments, foot-ornaments, etc.

Although he does not divide and give from the other four boxes, and the other four caskets, and the other three baskets, he will show them successively; it is not possible to give them with a single blow.

There, the Blessed One should be regarded as the great king.

For it was said: "I am a king, O Sela, the Blessed One, a peerless king of the Dharma" (Sn 559).

The Venerable Sāriputta should be regarded as the eldest son, for it was said: "Monks, if one speaking rightly should say, 'A son of the Blessed One, his own son, born of his mouth, born of the Dharma, created by the Dharma, an heir of the Dharma, not an heir of material things,' one speaking rightly would say it of Sāriputta, 'a son of the Blessed One…no heir of material things'" (MN 3.97).

The Saṅgha of monks should be regarded as the more than a thousand king's sons.

For it was said:

"More than a thousand monks attend on the Sugata,

who teaches the stainless Dharma, Nibbāna, free from fear."

302. What, friend, is the earth element ( pathavīdhātu)? The earth element may be internal (ajjhattikā) or may be external (bāhirā).

What, friend, is the internal earth element (ajjhattikā pathavīdhātu)? Whatever internally, belonging to oneself, is solid (kakkhaḷaṃ), solidified (kharigataṃ), and clung-to (upādinnaṃ); that is, head-hairs (kesā), body-hairs (lomā), nails (nakhā), teeth (dantā), skin (taco), flesh (maṃsaṃ), sinews (nhāru), bones (aṭṭhi), bone-marrow (aṭṭhimiñjaṃ), kidneys (vakkaṃ), heart (hadayaṃ), liver (yakanaṃ), diaphragm (kilomakaṃ), spleen (pihakaṃ), lungs (papphāsaṃ), intestines (antaṃ), mesentery (antaguṇaṃ), contents of the stomach (udariyaṃ), feces (karīsaṃ), or whatever else internally, belonging to oneself, is solid, solidified, and clung-to.

This, friend, is called the internal earth element (ajjhattikā pathavīdhātu).

Now both the internal earth element (ajjhattikā pathavīdhātu) and the external earth element (bāhirā pathavīdhātu) are simply earth element (pathavīdhāturevesā).

And that should be seen as it actually is with proper wisdom (sammappaññāya) thus: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’

When one sees it thus as it actually is with proper wisdom (sammappaññāya), one becomes disenchanted with the earth element (pathavīdhātuyā nibbindati) and makes the mind dispassionate towards the earth element (pathavīdhātuyā cittaṃ virājeti).

Now there comes a time, friend, when the external water element (bāhirā āpodhātu) is disturbed (pakuppati).

And then the external earth element (bāhirā pathavīdhātu) vanishes (antarahitā).

When even this external earth element (bāhirāya pathavīdhātuyā), great (mahallikāya) as it is, is seen to be impermanent (aniccatā paññāyissati), subject to destruction (khayadhammatā paññāyissati), disappearance (vayadhammatā paññāyissati), and change (vipariṇāmadhammatā paññāyissati),

what of this body (kāyassa), which is clung to by craving (taṇhupādinnassa) and lasts but a while (mattaṭṭhakassa)? There can be no considering that as ‘I’ (ahaṃ) or ‘mine’ (mamaṃ) or ‘I am’ (asmi).

Now, if others abuse (akkosanti), revile (paribhāsanti), scold (rosenti), and harass (vihesenti) a bhikkhu, he understands thus: ‘This painful feeling (dukkha vedanā) born of ear-contact (sotasamphassajā) has arisen (uppannā) in me.’

That is dependent (paṭicca), not independent (apaṭicca).

Dependent on what? Dependent on contact (phassaṃ paṭicca).’

Then he sees that contact (phasso) is impermanent (anicco), that feeling (vedanā) is impermanent (anicca), that perception (saññā) is impermanent (anicca), that formations (saṅkhārā) are impermanent (aniccā), and that consciousness (viññāṇaṃ) is impermanent (aniccanti).

And his mind (cittaṃ), having made an element its objective support (dhātārammaṇameva), enters into (pakkhandati) that new objective support and acquires confidence (pasīdati), steadiness (santiṭṭhati), and resolution (adhimuccati).

Now, if others attack that bhikkhu in ways that are unwished for (aniṭṭhehi), undesired (akantehi), and disagreeable (amanāpehi), by contact with fists (pāṇisamphassena), clods (leḍḍusamphassena), sticks (daṇḍasamphassena), or knives (satthasamphassena),

he understands thus: ‘This body (kāyo) is of such a nature (tathābhūto) that contact with fists (pāṇisamphassāpi kamanti), clods (leḍḍusamphassāpi kamanti), sticks (daṇḍasamphassāpi kamanti), and knives (satthasamphassāpi kamanti) assail it.

But this has been said by the Blessed One (bhagavatā) in his “advice on the simile of the saw” (kakacūpamovāde): “Bhikkhus, even if bandits (corā) were to sever you savagely limb by limb (aṅgamaṅgāni okanteyyuṃ) with a two-handled saw (ubhatodaṇḍakena kakacena), he who gave rise to a mind (mano padūseyya) of hate towards them would not be carrying out my teaching (sāsanakaro).”

So tireless energy (vīriyaṃ) shall be aroused (āraddhaṃ) in me and unremitting (asallīnaṃ) mindfulness (sati) established (upaṭṭhitā), my body (kāyo) shall be tranquil (passaddho) and untroubled (asāraddho), my mind (cittaṃ) concentrated (samāhitaṃ) and unified (ekaggaṃ).

And now let contact with fists (pāṇisamphassāpi kamantu), clods (leḍḍusamphassāpi kamantu), sticks (daṇḍasamphassāpi kamantu), and knives (satthasamphassāpi kamantu) assail this body (imasmiṃ kāye); for this teaching (sāsanaṃ) of the Buddhas (buddhānaṃ) is being practised by me.’

When that bhikkhu thus recollects (anussarato) the Buddha (buddhaṃ), the Dhamma (dhammaṃ), and the Saṅgha (saṅghaṃ), if equanimity (upekkhā) supported by the wholesome (kusalanissitā) does not become established (na saṇṭhāti) in him,

then he arouses a sense of urgency (saṃvijjati saṃvegaṃ āpajjati): ‘It is a loss (alābhā) for me, it is no gain (lābhā) for me, it is bad (dulladdhaṃ) for me, it is no good (suladdhaṃ) for me, that when I thus recollect (anussarato) the Buddha (buddhaṃ), the Dhamma (dhammaṃ), and the Saṅgha (saṅghaṃ), equanimity (upekkhā) supported by the wholesome (kusalanissitā) does not become established (na saṇṭhāti).’

Just as when a daughter-in-law (suṇisā) sees her father-in-law (sasuraṃ disvā) she arouses a sense of urgency (saṃvijjati saṃvegaṃ āpajjati) to please him, so too, when that bhikkhu thus recollects (anussarato) the Buddha (buddhaṃ), the Dhamma (dhammaṃ), and the Saṅgha (saṅghaṃ), if equanimity (upekkhā) supported by the wholesome (kusalanissitā) does not become established (na saṇṭhāti), then he arouses a sense of urgency (saṃvijjati saṃvegaṃ āpajjati): ‘It is a loss (alābhā) for me, it is no gain (lābhā) for me, it is bad (dulladdhaṃ) for me, it is no good (suladdhaṃ) for me, that when I thus recollect (anussarato) the Buddha (buddhaṃ), the Dhamma (dhammaṃ), and the Saṅgha (saṅghaṃ), equanimity (upekkhā) supported by the wholesome (kusalanissitā) does not become established (na saṇṭhāti).’
But if, friend, when a bhikkhu recollects the Buddha (buddhaṃ anussarato), recollects the Dharma (dhammaṃ anussarato), and recollects the Sangha (saṅghaṃ anussarato), equanimity (upekkhā) supported by the wholesome (kusalanissitā) becomes established, then he is satisfied with it.

At that point too, friend, much has been done by that bhikkhu.

The second occasion is spoken of in connection with cultivated contemplation of jhāna, or with cultivated attention to the meditation subject, or with cultivated recitation of the Buddha's words, and so on, or having in mind something else prescribed.

On this occasion, "rūpa," four aggregates are obtained.
