223. Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was dwelling at Kosambi in Ghosita’s Park.

Now on that occasion the wanderer Sandaka was staying in the Pilakkha-tree Cave with a large assembly of wanderers, with as many as five hundred wanderers.

Then the venerable Ānanda, when it was evening, rose from meditation and addressed the monks, “Come, friends (āvuso), let us go to the Devakata Pool to see the cave.”

“Yes, friend (evamāvuso),” those monks replied to the venerable Ānanda.

Then the venerable Ānanda went to the Devakata Pool together with a number of monks.

Now on that occasion the wanderer Sandaka was seated with a large assembly of wanderers who were making an uproar, loudly and noisily talking many kinds of pointless talk, such as talk of kings, robbers, ministers, armies, dangers, battles, food, drink, clothing, beds, garlands, perfumes, relatives, vehicles, villages, towns, cities, countries, women, heroes, streets, wells, the dead, trifles, the origin of the world, the origin of the sea, or talk about profit and loss (itibhavābhavakathaṃ iti vā).

Then the wanderer Sandaka saw the venerable Ānanda coming in the distance.

Seeing him, he quieted his own assembly thus: “Sirs, be quiet; sirs, make no noise. Here comes the śramaṇa Ānanda, a disciple of the śramaṇa Gotama.

One of the śramaṇa Gotama’s disciples staying in Kosambi is this śramaṇa Ānanda.

These venerable ones like quiet; they are disciplined in quiet; they commend quiet. Perhaps if he finds our assembly a quiet one, he will think to join us.”

Then the wanderers became silent.

223. Thus have I heard: the Sandaka Sutta.

There, pilakkhaguhāya means that there was a pilakkha tree at the entrance to that cave; therefore, it came to be known as Pilakkha Cave.

paṭisallānā vuṭṭhito means having arisen from solitude.

devakatasobbho means a large water reservoir created by rainwater in three places.

In guhādassanāyā, guhā means a mud cave.

It was located in an elevated, water-free area. An underground passage was made on one side, removing soil and dust, and pillars were erected inside, with a roof made in the manner of a thatched house. The wanderers lived there.

It was full of water during the rainy season, and they lived there in the dry season.

Referring to that, he said, "to see the cave."

Indeed, it is proper to go to see a monastery, or to reflect on Anamataggiya, or even to go to see the ocean or a mountain.

unnādiniyā means making a loud noise.

Because it was high due to rising up in this way, and because it was spread out in the directions, it was a great loud noise; by that great loud noise (uccāsaddamahāsaddāya).

For those wanderers, there was no such thing as a cetiya practice, a bodhi practice, a teacher's or preceptor's practice, or wise attention, which should be done early in the morning.

Therefore, they sat in the young sun early in the morning, or gathered in the evening to talk comfortably, saying, "This one's hand is beautiful, that one's foot," and so on, starting with each other's hands and feet, or about the beauty of women, men, boys, and girls, or starting with some other object of sensual pleasure and the pleasure of existence, they started a conversation and gradually told various kinds of animal talk such as talk of kings.

That talk, being an obstacle to liberation and the paths to heaven and liberation, is called tiracchānakathā, "animal talk."

There, talk about kings (rājakathā) is talk about kings proceeding like this: "Mahāsammata, Mandhātu, Dhammāsoka had so much power," and so on.

This method applies to talk about thieves (corakathādīsu) and so on.

Among these, talk influenced by household life (gehassitakathā) is animal talk, proceeding thus: "So and so is a handsome, pleasing king."

But if it proceeds “So and so who was so powerful came to destruction,” it keeps within the meditation subject (kammaṭṭhānabhāve).

Even among thieves, talk influenced by household life is animal talk, such as, "Mūladeva was so powerful, Meghamāla was so powerful," considering their deeds, "Oh, they were heroes!"

And as regards battles, it is “animal talk” when it is instigated by satisfaction of sense-desires thus: “In the Bhārata battle, and so forth, so and so was killed thus, was wounded thus.”

but when it proceeds, “even they came to destruction,” then talk in each case conforms to a meditation subject.

Moreover it is wrong to talk about food and so forth according to satisfaction of sense-desires thus: “We chewed, we ate, we drank, we used what looked like this, smelt like this, tasted like this, was like this to touch.” But it is right to talk of it meaningfully thus: “Formerly we gave food, drink, clothing, beds, garlands and perfumes that looked like this and so forth to the virtuous; we made such an offering at the shrine.”

As regards relatives (ñātikathādīsupi) and so on, it is wrong to say, according to satisfaction of sense-desires, “our relatives are brave, capable” or “formerly we went about in vehicles like this.” But it should be said meaningfully thus: “even those relatives of ours have passed away” or “formerly we gave sandals like this to the Order.”

Villages (gāmakathāpi): it is wrong to talk of villages as good or bad to live in, or easy or hard to get alms in, or according to satisfaction of sense-desires thus: “The inhabitants of such and such a village are brave, capable”. But it is right to talk meaningfully thus: “They have faith, they have confidence” or “They have come to destruction and have passed away.”

The same method applies to talk of towns, cities, and districts (nigamanagarajanapadakathāsupi).

Talk of women (itthikathāpi) is wrong when instigated by satisfaction of sense-desires and is about appearance, figure, and so forth; it is right only if it proceeds thus: “they have faith, confidence” or “they have come to destruction.”

Talk of heroes (sūrakathāpi) is wrong if it is in accordance with satisfaction of sense-desires thus: “the soldier called Nandimitta was a hero”; it is right only if it proceeds thus: “He had faith” or “He has come to destruction.”

Talk of street inhabitants (visikhākathāpi) is wrong if it accords with satisfaction of sense-desires thus: “The people of such and such a street are easy to live with, brave, capable”; it is right only if it proceeds thus: “They have faith” or “They have come to destruction.”
Nigama, city, district talks and also women's talks and also hero's talks.
Visikhā talks and also talk of wells, that is, talk of places for getting water or talk of women water-carriers.
It is wrong when concerned with satisfaction of sense-desires thus: “She is pretty, she is clever at dancing and singing”; it is right only if it begins, “She has faith, has confidence.”
Talk of former dead relatives is talk of past relatives.
The definition here is the same as that for present relatives.

Talk of wells and talk of former dead relatives, talk of diversity, that is, talk that is free of former and latter, the remaining diverse things, meaningless talk.
Talk of the origin of the world is, this world was created by whom? It was created by so and so. “A crow is white because its bones are white.” “A crane is red because its blood is red,” and so on. This is Lokāyata sophistry and chatter.

Talk of diversity and talk of the origin of the world.
Talk of the origin of the sea is: Why is it called sea samudda? Because it was excavated by the god Sāgara, it is called sāgara. Because he marked it with the seal (muddā) of his hand saying, “The ocean sāgara has been excavated by me, it is called sea samudda.” And so on, meaningless talk of the origin of the sea.
Whether things are or are not is talk asserting that for some meaningless reason or other there is consequently existence; there is consequently non-existence (itibhavābhavakathā).
And here “existence” (bhava) is eternity and “non-existence” (abhava) is annihilation.
“Existence” (bhava) is growth, “non-existence” (abhava) is loss.
“Existence” (bhava) is sense-pleasure, “non-existence” (abhava) is self mortification.
So with this talk on whether things are or are not, "animal talk" is of thirty-two kinds.
He is seated speaking such animal talk.

Talk of the origin of the sea and talk of whether things are or are not.

Sandaka the wanderer saw...pe...was silent. There, "he trained (saṇṭhapesī)" means he taught, he hid their faults.

Just as a man, entering the midst of the company, arranges his lower garment for the purpose of concealing faults, arranges his outer robe, wipes a dusty place, so he trains him, "be quiet venerable sirs" for the purpose of concealing his faults, just as he was well placed, so he placed him, this is the meaning.

“Sandaka, when a bhikkhu is an arahaṃ whose āsavas are destroyed (khīṇāsavo), who has lived the holy life (vusitavā), done what had to be done (katakaraṇīyo), laid down the burden (ohitabhāro), reached the true goal (anuppattasadattho), destroyed the fetters of being (parikkhīṇabhavasaṃyojano), and is completely liberated through final knowledge (sammadaññā vimutto), he is incapable of transgression in five cases (pañcaṭṭhānāni ajjhācarituṃ).

A bhikkhu whose āsavas are destroyed (khīṇāsavo) is incapable of deliberately depriving a living being of life (pāṇaṃ jīvitā voropetuṃ); he is incapable of taking what is not given, that is, of stealing (adinnaṃ theyyasaṅkhātaṃ ādātuṃ); he is incapable of indulging in sexual intercourse (methunaṃ dhammaṃ paṭisevetuṃ); he is incapable of knowingly speaking falsehood (sampajānamusā bhāsituṃ); he is incapable of enjoying sensual pleasures by storing them up as he did formerly in lay life (sannidhikārakaṃ kāme paribhuñjituṃ, seyyathāpi pubbe agāriyabhūto).

Sandaka, when a bhikkhu is an arahaṃ whose āsavas are destroyed (khīṇāsavo), who has lived the holy life (vusitavā), done what had to be done (katakaraṇīyo), laid down the burden (ohitabhāro), reached the true goal (anuppattasadattho), destroyed the fetters of being (parikkhīṇabhavasaṃyojano), and is completely liberated through final knowledge (sammadaññā vimutto), he is incapable of transgression in these five cases (imāni pañcaṭṭhānāni ajjhācarituṃ).”
234.Sannidhikārakaṃ kāme paribhuñjitunti yathā pubbe gihibhūto sannidhiṃ katvā vatthukāme paribhuñjati, evaṃ tilataṇḍulasappinavanītādīni sannidhiṃ katvā idāni paribhuñjituṃ abhabboti attho. Nanu ca khīṇāsavassa vasanaṭṭhāne tilataṇḍulādayo paññāyantīti. No na paññāyanti, na panesa te attano atthāya ṭhapeti, aphāsukapabbajitādīnaṃ atthāya ṭhapeti. Anāgāmissa kathanti. Tassāpi pañca kāmaguṇā sabbasova pahīnā, dhammena pana laddhaṃ vicāretvā paribhuñjati.
"sannidhikārakaṃ kāme paribhuñjitu" means just as formerly, when one was a householder (gihibhūto), one made a store (sannidhiṃ katvā) and enjoyed (paribhuñjati) objects of desire (vatthukāme), in the same way, now it is not possible to enjoy storing up things like sesame seeds (tila), rice grains (taṇḍula), clarified butter (sappi), fresh butter (navanīta), and so on (ādīni). This is the meaning (attho).

Indeed (nanu ca), aren't sesame seeds (tila), rice grains (taṇḍula), and so on (ādayo) apparent (paññāyanti) in the dwelling place (vasanaṭṭhāne) of one whose āsavas are destroyed (khīṇāsavassa)?

No (no), they are not apparent (na paññāyanti). However (pana), he (esa) does not (na) store (ṭhapeti) them for his own (attano) sake (atthāya), but (ṭhapeti) for the sake (atthāya) of ascetics (pabbajita) and others (ādīnaṃ) who are uncomfortable (aphāsuka).

What about (kathanti) a non-returner (anāgāmissa)?

Even (api) for him (tassa), the five (pañca) sensual qualities (kāmaguṇā) are completely (sabbaso) abandoned (pahīnā); however (pana), having considered (vicāretvā) what is obtained (laddhaṃ) through the Dhamma (dhammena), he enjoys it (paribhuñjati).

235. ‘‘Yo pana so, bho ānanda, bhikkhu arahaṃ khīṇāsavo vusitavā katakaraṇīyo ohitabhāro anuppattasadattho parikkhīṇabhavasaṃyojano sammadaññā vimutto tassa carato ceva tiṭṭhato ca suttassa ca jāgarassa ca satataṃ samitaṃ ñāṇadassanaṃ paccupaṭṭhitaṃ – ‘khīṇā me āsavā’’’ti? ‘‘Tena hi, sandaka, upamaṃ te karissāmi; upamāyapidhekacce viññū purisā bhāsitassa atthaṃ ājānanti. Seyyathāpi, sandaka, purisassa hatthapādā chinnā; tassa carato ceva tiṭṭhato ca suttassa ca jāgarassa ca satataṃ samitaṃ (jānāti – ‘chinnā me hatthapādā’ti, udāhu paccavekkhamāno jānāti – ‘chinnā me hatthapādā’’’ti? ‘‘Na kho, bho ānanda, so puriso satataṃ samitaṃ jānāti – ‘chinnā me hatthapādā’ ti.) (chinnāva hatthapādā,) (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) Api ca kho pana naṃ paccavekkhamāno jānāti – ‘chinnā me hatthapādā’’’ti. ‘‘Evameva kho, sandaka, yo so bhikkhu arahaṃ khīṇāsavo vusitavā katakaraṇīyo ohitabhāro anuppattasadattho parikkhīṇabhavasaṃyojano sammadaññā vimutto tassa carato ceva tiṭṭhato ca suttassa ca jāgarassa ca satataṃ samitaṃ (ñāṇadassanaṃ na paccupaṭṭhitaṃ – ‘khīṇā me āsavā’ti;) (khīṇāva āsavā,) (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) api ca kho pana naṃ paccavekkhamāno jānāti – ‘khīṇā me āsavā’’’ti.
235. “But, Master Ānanda, when a bhikkhu is an arahaṃ with taints destroyed, one who has lived the holy life, done what was to be done, laid down the burden, attained his own welfare, completely destroyed the fetter of becoming, is liberated by perfect knowledge, is his knowledge and vision that his taints are destroyed (khīṇā me āsavā) continuously and uninterruptedly present to him whether he is walking or standing or sleeping or awake?”

“Then, Sandaka, I will make a comparison for you; for through a comparison, some intelligent men understand the meaning of what is spoken. Suppose, Sandaka, a man’s hands and feet were cut off. Would he know ‘My hands and feet are cut off’ continuously and uninterruptedly, whether he is walking or standing or sleeping or awake, or would he know ‘My hands and feet are cut off’ only when he reviews this fact?”

“No, Master Ānanda, that man would not know ‘My hands and feet are cut off’ continuously and uninterruptedly. But when he reviews this fact, he knows ‘My hands and feet are cut off.’”

“In the same way, Sandaka, when a bhikkhu is an arahaṃ with taints destroyed, one who has lived the holy life, done what was to be done, laid down the burden, attained his own welfare, completely destroyed the fetter of becoming, is liberated by perfect knowledge, is his knowledge and vision not continuously and uninterruptedly present to him whether he is walking or standing or sleeping or awake that ‘My taints are destroyed (khīṇā me āsavā)’? But when he reviews this fact, he knows ‘My taints are destroyed (khīṇā me āsavā).’”

236. ‘‘Kīvabahukā pana, bho ānanda, imasmiṃ dhammavinaye niyyātāro’’ti? ‘‘Na kho, sandaka, ekaṃyeva sataṃ na dve satāni na tīṇi satāni na cattāri satāni na pañca satāni, atha kho bhiyyova ye imasmiṃ dhammavinaye niyyātāro’’ti. ‘‘Acchariyaṃ, bho ānanda, abbhutaṃ, bho ānanda! Na ca nāma sadhammokkaṃsanā bhavissati, na paradhammavambhanā, āyatane ca dhammadesanā tāva bahukā ca niyyātāro paññāyissanti. Ime panājīvakā puttamatāya puttā attānañceva ukkaṃsenti, pare ca vambhenti tayo ceva niyyātāro paññapenti, seyyathidaṃ – nandaṃ vacchaṃ, kisaṃ saṃkiccaṃ, makkhaliṃ gosāla’’nti. Atha kho sandako paribbājako sakaṃ parisaṃ āmantesi – ‘‘carantu bhonto samaṇe gotame brahmacariyavāso. Na dāni sukaraṃ amhehi lābhasakkārasiloke pariccajitu’’nti. Iti hidaṃ sandako paribbājako sakaṃ parisaṃ uyyojesi bhagavati brahmacariyeti. Sandakasuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ chaṭṭhaṃ.
“How many are emancipated in this Dhamma and Discipline?”

“There are not only one hundred, Sandaka, or two hundred, three hundred, four hundred or five hundred, but far more emancipated ones than that in this Dhamma and Discipline.”

“It’s amazing, Ānanda! It’s astonishing, Ānanda! There will not be just praise of the true Dharma, nor disparagement of other doctrines. And in the locations where the Dharma is taught, many emancipated ones will be known.

But these Ājīvakas, those mothers’ dead sons, laud themselves and disparage others, and they recognize only three emancipated ones, namely, Nanda Vaccha, Kisa Sankicca, and Makkhali Gosala.”

Then the wanderer Sandaka addressed his own assembly: “Go, sirs. The holy life (brahmacariyavāso) is to be lived under the recluse Gotama.

It is not easy for us now to give up gain, honour (sakkāra), and renown.”

That is how the wanderer Sandaka exhorted his own assembly to live the holy life (brahmacariya) under the Blessed One (bhagavati).

The Sandaka Sutta, the sixth, is finished.
236.Puttamatāya puttāti so kira imaṃ dhammaṃ sutvā ājīvakā matā nāmāti saññī hutvā evamāha. Ayañhettha attho – ājīvakā matā nāma, tesaṃ mātā puttamatā hoti, iti ājīvakā puttamatāya puttā nāma honti. Samaṇe gotameti samaṇe gotame brahmacariyavāso atthi, aññattha natthīti dīpeti. Sesaṃ sabbattha uttānamevāti. Papañcasūdaniyā majjhimanikāyaṭṭhakathāya Sandakasuttavaṇṇanā niṭṭhitā.
236. "Because of the mother of sons ( puttamatāya puttā )": Having heard this doctrine, the Ājīvakas, considering themselves "dead mothers," spoke thus.

The meaning here is this: The Ājīvakas are "dead mothers," and their mothers are "mothers of sons." Thus, the Ājīvakas are called "sons because of the mother of sons."

"In the ascetic Gotama ( samaṇe gotame )": It indicates that the holy life ( brahmacariyavāso ) exists in the ascetic Gotama, but not elsewhere.

The rest is clear throughout.

The commentary on the Sandaka Sutta in the Papañcasūdanī, commentary on the Majjhimanikāya, is finished.
