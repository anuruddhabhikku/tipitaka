237. Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One (bhagavā) was dwelling at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove (veḷuvana), the Squirrels’ Sanctuary (kalandakanivāpa).

Now at that time, a number of well-known wanderers (paribbājakā) were staying at the Peacocks’ Sanctuary (moranivāpa), the wanderers’ park (paribbājakārāma), that is to say, Annabhāra, Varadhara, and the wanderer Sakuludāyin, as well as other well-known wanderers (paribbājakā).

Then the Blessed One (bhagavā), having dressed in the morning, taking his bowl (patta) and robe (cīvara), entered Rājagaha for alms (piṇḍa).

Then this thought occurred to the Blessed One (bhagavato): “It is still too early to walk for alms (piṇḍa) in Rājagaha.

Suppose I were to visit the wanderers' park (paribbājakārāma) at the Peacocks’ Sanctuary (moranivāpa), where the wanderer Sakuludāyin is?”

Then the Blessed One (bhagavā) went to the wanderers' park (paribbājakārāma) at the Peacocks’ Sanctuary (moranivāpa).

Now at that time the wanderer Sakuludāyin was seated with a large assembly of wanderers, who were making an uproar, loudly and noisily talking many kinds of pointless talk, such as talk of kings, talk of robbers, talk of ministers, talk of armies, talk of dangers, talk of battles, talk of food, talk of drink, talk of clothing, talk of beds, talk of garlands, talk of perfumes, talk of relatives, talk of vehicles, talk of villages, talk of towns, talk of cities, talk of countries, talk of women, talk of heroes, talk of streets, talk of wells, talk of past ancestors, talk of various things, tales of the world, tales of the sea, talk of what is and what is not.

The wanderer Sakuludāyin saw the Blessed One (bhagavantaṃ) coming in the distance.

Seeing him, he quieted his own assembly thus: “Sirs, be quiet; sirs, make no noise.

Here comes the recluse Gotama; that venerable one likes quiet and commends quiet.

Perhaps if he perceives our group as quiet, he will consider it worth his while to come our way.”

Then those wanderers became silent.

Then the Blessed One (bhagavā) went to the wanderer Sakuludāyin.

Then the wanderer Sakuludāyin said this to the Blessed One (bhagavantaṃ): “Come, venerable sir, Blessed One (bhagavā).

Welcome, venerable sir, to the Blessed One (bhagavato).

It is long since the Blessed One (bhagavā), venerable sir, found an opportunity to come here.

Be seated, venerable sir, Blessed One (bhagavā); this seat is prepared.”

The Blessed One (bhagavā) sat down on the prepared seat (āsane).

And the wanderer Sakuludāyin also took a low seat and sat down at one side.

237. Thus (evaṃ), “me suta” is the Mahāsakuludāyisuttaṃ.

There, "moranivāpe": in that place (tasmiṃ ṭhāne), they proclaimed (ghosetvā) safety (abhayaṃ) to the peacocks (morānaṃ) and gave (adaṃsu) food (bhojanaṃ).

Therefore (tasmā), that place (taṃ ṭhānaṃ) went (gataṃ) to the designation (saṅkhaṃ) "moranivāpo."

"annabhāro" is the name (nāmaṃ) of one (ekassa) wandering ascetic (paribbājakassa).

Likewise (tathā), "varadharo."

"aññe cā": not (na) only (kevalaṃ) these (ime) three (tayo), [but] other (aññepi) well-known (abhiññātā) wandering ascetics (paribbājakā) [were] many (bahū).

"appasaddassa vaṇṇavādī": here (idha), this (idaṃ) was said (vuttaṃ) without saying (avatvāva) "controlled by few sounds (appasaddavinītoti)."

Why (kasmā)? The Blessed One (bhagavā) was not (na hi) controlled (vinīto) by another (aññena).

238. Purimānī means "starting from yesterday, they are called purimāni; thereafter, purimatarāni."

Kutūhalasālāya means a kutūhalasālā is not a specific paccekasālā. But where various non-Buddhist ascetics and Brahmins engage in diverse discussions, that place is called a kutūhalasālā because it is the source of curiosity, [thinking] "What is this person saying?"

There is also the reading kotūhalasālā.

Lābhā means, those who can obtain the opportunity to see such ascetics and Brahmins, to ask questions, or to hear religious talks, for those Aṅga-Magadhans, this is called lābhā, meaning gain.

Saṅghino etc., means saṅgha, which is the term for a community of renunciates, exists for these people; therefore, they are saṅghino.

" Gaṇino " means they have a gaṇa, a group; therefore, they are gaṇino.

"Gaṇācariyā" means "teachers of the group," due to their teaching of conduct to that group.

"Ñātā" means "known," paññātā, "renowned," pākaṭā, "manifest."

Yasassino means "they have fame (yasas) arising from appropriate qualities (yathābhuccaguṇa) and inappropriate qualities (ayathābhūtaguṇa)."

Because for Pūraṇa and others, fame arises through the principle of "having few desires and being content; not even wearing clothes due to having few desires," and for the tathāgata, through appropriate qualities, like "itipi so bhagavā," "indeed, that Blessed One."

Titthakarā means "those who make gains (laddhikara)."

Sādhusammatā means "they are regarded thus: 'These are good, beautiful, and noble persons' (satpuruṣa)."

Bahujanassā means "of many people," both the unlearned, blind, childish common people and discerning, learned people.

There, non-Buddhists are regarded thus by the childish people, while the tathāgata is regarded thus by the learned people.

By this principle, the meaning of "Pūraṇo Kassapo Saṅghī," and so forth, should be understood.

Moreover, because the Blessed One, dividing the thirty-eight objects of mindfulness, made many places of pilgrimage for descending into nirvāṇa, therefore, it is fitting to say "titthakaro."

Saṅghino, saṅghino, gaṇino, gaṇācariyā. Ñātā, yasassino, titthakarā, sādhusammatā, bahujanassā, Pūraṇo Kassapo Saṅghī.

Why are all of these gathered there? For the purpose of protecting supporters and for the purpose of gain and honor.

For they think thus: "Our supporters might go to the samaṇa Gotama for refuge, and we will protect them. Seeing our supporters honoring the samaṇa Gotama, our supporters will also honor us."

Therefore, wherever the Blessed One goes, there all go.

“Thereupon some said this: ‘This Pūraṇa Kassapa is the head of an order, a group leader, the teacher of a group, known, famous, a founder of a school, regarded by many as a saint; yet he is not honoured, respected, revered, and venerated by his disciples, nor do his disciples live in dependence on him, honouring and respecting him.

Once Pūraṇa Kassapa was teaching the Dhamma to an assembly of several hundred followers.

Then a certain disciple of his made a noise thus: “Sirs, do not ask Pūraṇa Kassapa this question. He does not know that. We know that. Ask us that question. We will answer that for you, sirs.”

Once Pūraṇa Kassapa, waving his arms and wailing, did not get his way: “Be quiet, sirs, make no noise, sirs.
They are not asking you, sirs. They are asking us. We will answer them.”

Indeed, many of Pūraṇa Kassapa’s disciples left him after refuting his doctrine thus: “You do not understand this dharma-vinaya (Dhamma and Discipline). I understand this Dhamma and Discipline. How could you understand this Dhamma and Discipline? Your way is wrong. My way is right. I am consistent. You are inconsistent. What should have been said first, you said last. What should have been said last, you said first. What you had so carefully thought up has been turned inside out. Your doctrine is refuted. You are proved wrong. Go and learn better, or disentangle yourself if you can!”

Thus Pūraṇa Kassapa is not honoured, respected, revered, and venerated by his disciples, nor do his disciples live in dependence on him, honouring and respecting him.

Indeed, Pūraṇa Kassapa is scorned by the scorn shown to his Dhamma.’

Now, what is described by these right exertions (sammappadhānehi)? The preliminary practice (pubbabhāgapaṭipadā) of a disciple is described in the section connected with Kassapa (kassapasaṃyuttapariyāyena).

For it was said there:

"There are these four right exertions, friend. What four? Here, friend, a bhikkhu generates enthusiasm (ātappaṃ karoti) thinking, 'May evil, unwholesome states not arise that, arising, would lead to harm.' He generates enthusiasm thinking, 'May evil, unwholesome states that have arisen not be abandoned and lead to harm.' He generates enthusiasm thinking, 'May wholesome states that have not arisen not arise and lead to harm.' He generates enthusiasm thinking, 'May wholesome states that have arisen not cease and lead to harm'" (Saṃ. Ni. 2. 145).

Here, evil, unwholesome states (pāpakā akusalā) should be understood as greed and so forth. Wholesome states that have not arisen (anuppannā kusalā dhammā) are tranquility (samatha) and insight (vipassanā) as well as the path (maggo), and wholesome states that have arisen (uppannā kusalā) are tranquility and insight.

But there is no path (maggo) that, arising once and ceasing, would lead to harm. For it ceases only after giving the condition for the fruit.

“Sees material forms externally”: with the eye of jhāna one also sees material forms such as the blue kasiṇa, etc. externally. By this the four fine-material-sphere jhānas are shown for someone who arouses jhāna in the external kasiṇas which are internal and external.

“One not perceiving material forms internally”: This means that he does not arouse the fine-material-sphere jhānas on his own head hairs, etc. By this the fine-material-sphere jhānas are shown for someone who does the preparatory work externally and arouses jhāna only externally.

“One is released upon the idea of the beautiful”: by this, jhānas are shown in the very pure color kasiṇas such as blue, etc. In the meditative absorption itself there is no concern with the idea of the beautiful. But because one who dwells in meditation taking as his object a pure and beautiful kasiṇa can be described as “released upon the idea of the beautiful,” the teaching is expounded in those words.

In the Paṭisambhidāmagga, however, it is said: “'One is released upon the idea of the beautiful'—how is this an emancipation? Herein, a bhikkhu dwells pervading one direction, etc. with a mind endowed with loving-kindness; through the development of loving-kindness beings are not repulsive to him. He dwells pervading one direction with a mind endowed with compassion, with a mind endowed with joy, with a mind endowed with equanimity…through the development of equanimity beings are not repulsive to him. Thus, 'one is released upon the idea of the beautiful'—this is an emancipation'” (Paṭi. ma. 1.212).

Regarding all that should be stated in “complete transcendence of perception of material form,” etc., that is stated in the Visuddhimagga.

“This is the eighth emancipation”: the eighth emancipation is the highest emancipation, since it is completely purified and liberated from the four aggregates ( skandha).

What do they master? Both the opponent-phenomena (paccanīkadhammepi) and the objects (ārammaṇānipi).

For they overcome opponent-phenomena by way of opposition, [and they are] objects for the knowledge of an individual, which is supreme.

In "ajjhattaṃ rūpasaññī " etc., one is called "ajjhattaṃ rūpasaññī" by way of the inner-form preparatory practice (parikammavasena).

And practicing the blue preparatory practice (nīlaparikammaṃ karonto) he makes [it] in the hair or in the bile or in the pupil of the eye, practicing the yellow preparatory practice (pītaparikammaṃ karonto) he makes [it] in the fat or in the skin or in the palms of the hands and soles of the feet or in the yellow place of the eyes, practicing the red preparatory practice (lohitaparikammaṃ karonto) he makes [it] in the flesh or in the blood or in the tongue or in the red place of the eyes, practicing the white preparatory practice (odātaparikammaṃ karonto) he makes [it] in the bones or in the teeth or in the nails or in the white place of the eyes.

That, however, is not very blue, very yellow, very red, very white, it is only not very pure.

"Eko bahiddhā rūpāni passati" means: Whose preparatory practice (parikammaṃ) has arisen internally (ajjhattaṃ), but the sign (nimittaṃ) is external (bahiddhā), thus, by way of the application (appanāya) of the internal preparatory practice (ajjhattaṃ parikammassa) and external [sign] (bahiddhā ca), he is called "ajjhattaṃ rūpasaññī eko bahiddhā rūpāni passati."

"Parittānī" means undeveloped.

"Suvaṇṇadubbaṇṇānī" means: Let them be beautiful (suvaṇṇāni vā hontu) or ugly (dubbaṇṇāni vā), this sphere of mastery (idamabhibhāyatanaṃ) should be known to be spoken of only by way of the limited.

There, manomaya means produced by the mind.

Sabbaṅgapaccaṅgi means complete with all its limbs and secondary limbs.

Ahīnindriya means unimpaired faculties through the power of [its] configuration.

For if the possessor of supernormal power is white, then the form he creates will also be white.

If his earlobes are unpierced, the form will also have earlobes which are unpierced. Thus it is similar to him in all respects.

Muñjamhā Īsika and so on, even in the simile, that is said only to show the similarity of condition.

For the reed īsika inside is similar to the muñja.

The sword is similar to its scabbard, since they put a round sword into a round scabbard and a flat sword into a flat scabbard.

Karaṇḍā means this is also a name for the snake's slough ( ahikañcuka), not for a vilīvakaraṇḍaka.

The snake’s slough ( ahikañcuka) is similar to the snake.

Though in the text it is said, “Suppose a man were to pull a snake out from its slough ( karaṇḍā),” as if he were to pull it out with his hand, we should understand that he extracts it mentally.

For this snake, abiding in its own species, relying on a log or a tree, by its strength, known as the effort of dragging its body out from the skin, feeling disgust towards the old skin as if it were devouring its body, abandons its slough by itself alone, through these four means, no one can pull it out by other means.

Therefore, it should be understood that this is said in reference to the mental extraction of the snake.

Just as when someone examines the mark on his face (tattha yathā tassa mukhanimittaṃ paccavekkhato), the defect in the face becomes evident (mukhadoso pākaṭo hoti), so too (evaṃ) it should be understood (veditabbaṃ) that when a bhikkhu sits having directed his mind towards the knowledge of encompassing the minds of others (cetopariyañāṇāya cittaṃ abhinīharitvā nisinnassa bhikkhuno), the sixteen kinds of minds of others become evident (paresam soḷasavidhaṃ cittaṃ pākaṭaṃ hoti iti). Everything that has to be said about past dwellings and comparisons (pubbenivāsaupamādīsu yaṃ vattabbaṃ), all that (taṃ sabbaṃ) has already been said in Mahāassapura (mahāassapure vuttameva).

257. ‘‘Puna caparaṃ, udāyi, akkhātā mayā sāvakānaṃ paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipannā me sāvakā anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussaranti, seyyathidaṃ – ekampi jātiṃ dvepi jātiyo tissopi jātiyo catassopi jātiyo pañcapi jātiyo dasapi jātiyo vīsampi jātiyo tiṃsampi jātiyo cattālīsampi jātiyo paññāsampi jātiyo jātisatampi jātisahassampi jātisatasahassampi, anekepi saṃvaṭṭakappe anekepi vivaṭṭakappe anekepi saṃvaṭṭavivaṭṭakappe – ‘amutrāsiṃ evaṃnāmo evaṃgotto evaṃvaṇṇo evamāhāro evaṃsukhadukkhappaṭisaṃvedī evamāyupariyanto, so tato cuto amutra udapādiṃ; tatrāpāsiṃ evaṃnāmo evaṃgotto evaṃvaṇṇo evamāhāro evaṃsukhadukkhappaṭisaṃvedī evamāyupariyanto, so tato cuto idhūpapanno’ti. Iti sākāraṃ sauddesaṃ anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussarati. Seyyathāpi, udāyi, puriso sakamhā gāmā aññaṃ gāmaṃ gaccheyya, tamhāpi gāmā aññaṃ gāmaṃ gaccheyya; so tamhā gāmā sakaṃyeva gāmaṃ paccāgaccheyya; tassa evamassa – ‘ahaṃ kho sakamhā gāmā aññaṃ gāmaṃ agacchiṃ, tatra evaṃ aṭṭhāsiṃ evaṃ nisīdiṃ evaṃ abhāsiṃ evaṃ tuṇhī ahosiṃ; tamhāpi gāmā amuṃ gāmaṃ agacchiṃ, tatrāpi evaṃ aṭṭhāsiṃ evaṃ nisīdiṃ evaṃ abhāsiṃ evaṃ tuṇhī ahosiṃ, somhi tamhā gāmā sakaṃyeva gāmaṃ paccāgato’ti. Evameva kho, udāyi, akkhātā mayā sāvakānaṃ paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipannā me sāvakā anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussaranti, seyyathidaṃ – ekampi jātiṃ…pe… iti sākāraṃ sauddesaṃ anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussaranti. Tatra ca pana me sāvakā bahū abhiññāvosānapāramippattā viharanti.
257. “Again, Udayin, I have proclaimed to my disciples the way (paṭipadā) whereby, having practiced, my disciples recollect their manifold past lives (anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussaranti), that is, one birth (ekampi jātiṃ), two births (dvepi jātiyo), three births (tissopi jātiyo), four births (catassopi jātiyo), five births (pañcapi jātiyo), ten births (dasapi jātiyo), twenty births (vīsampi jātiyo), thirty births (tiṃsampi jātiyo), forty births (cattālīsampi jātiyo), fifty births (paññāsampi jātiyo), a hundred births (jātisatampi), a thousand births (jātisahassampi), a hundred thousand births (jātisatasahassampi), many aeons of world-contraction (anekepi saṃvaṭṭakappe), many aeons of world-expansion (anekepi vivaṭṭakappe), many aeons of world-contraction and expansion (anekepi saṃvaṭṭavivaṭṭakappe): ‘There I was so named (evaṃnāmo), of such a clan (evaṃgotto), with such an appearance (evaṃvaṇṇo), such was my food (evamāhāro), such my experience of pleasure and pain (evaṃsukhadukkhappaṭisaṃvedī), such my life-term (evamāyupariyanto). Passing away from there (so tato cuto), I arose there (amutra udapādiṃ); there I was so named (evaṃnāmo), of such a clan (evaṃgotto), with such an appearance (evaṃvaṇṇo), such was my food (evamāhāro), such my experience of pleasure and pain (evaṃsukhadukkhappaṭisaṃvedī), such my life-term (evamāyupariyanto). Passing away from there, I was reborn here (idhūpapanno).’

Thus he recollects his numerous past lives (anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussarati) in their modes and their details (sākāraṃ sauddesaṃ).

Just as, Udayin, a man might go from his own village (sakamhā gāmā) to another village (aññaṃ gāmaṃ), and from that village (tamhāpi gāmā) to another village (aññaṃ gāmaṃ); and then from that village (tamhā gāmā) he might return to his own village (sakaṃyeva gāmaṃ paccāgaccheyya); he might think: ‘I went from my own village (sakamhā gāmā) to that other village (aññaṃ gāmaṃ), and there I stood (aṭṭhāsiṃ) in such a way, sat (nisīdiṃ) in such a way, spoke (abhāsiṃ) in such a way, kept silent (tuṇhī ahosiṃ) in such a way; and from that village (tamhāpi gāmā) I went to that other village (amuṃ gāmaṃ), and there too I stood (aṭṭhāsiṃ) in such a way, sat (nisīdiṃ) in such a way, spoke (abhāsiṃ) in such a way, kept silent (tuṇhī ahosiṃ) in such a way, and from that village (tamhā gāmā) I have just returned to my own village (sakaṃyeva gāmaṃ paccāgato.’

So too, Udayin, I have proclaimed to my disciples the way (paṭipadā) whereby, having practiced, my disciples recollect their manifold past lives (anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussaranti), that is, one birth (ekampi jātiṃ)… and so on… Thus he recollects his numerous past lives (anekavihitaṃ pubbenivāsaṃ anussaranti) in their modes and their details (sākāraṃ sauddesaṃ).

And thereby many disciples of mine abide having reached the consummation and perfection of direct knowledge (abhiññāvosānapāramippattā viharanti).

258. ‘‘Puna caparaṃ, udāyi, akkhātā mayā sāvakānaṃ paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipannā me sāvakā dibbena cakkhunā visuddhena atikkantamānusakena satte passanti cavamāne upapajjamāne hīne paṇīte suvaṇṇe dubbaṇṇe sugate duggate yathākammūpage satte pajānanti – ‘ime vata bhonto sattā kāyaduccaritena samannāgatā vacīduccaritena samannāgatā manoduccaritena samannāgatā ariyānaṃ upavādakā micchādiṭṭhikā micchādiṭṭhikammasamādānā, te kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā apāyaṃ duggatiṃ vinipātaṃ nirayaṃ upapannā; ime vā pana bhonto sattā kāyasucaritena samannāgatā vacīsucaritena samannāgatā manosucaritena samannāgatā ariyānaṃ anupavādakā sammādiṭṭhikā sammādiṭṭhikammasamādānā, te kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā sugatiṃ saggaṃ lokaṃ upapannā’ti. Iti dibbena cakkhunā visuddhena atikkantamānusakena satte passanti cavamāne upapajjamāne hīne paṇīte suvaṇṇe dubbaṇṇe sugate duggate yathākammūpage satte pajānanti. Seyyathāpi, udāyi, dve agārā sadvārā sannadvārā (ka.). Tatra cakkhumā puriso majjhe ṭhito passeyya manusse gehaṃ pavisantepi nikkhamantepi anucaṅkamantepi anuvicarantepi; evameva kho, udāyi, akkhātā mayā sāvakānaṃ paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipannā me sāvakā dibbena cakkhunā visuddhena atikkantamānusakena satte passanti cavamāne upapajjamāne hīne paṇīte suvaṇṇe dubbaṇṇe sugate duggate yathākammūpage satte pajānanti…pe… tatra ca pa me sāvakā bahū abhiññāvosānapāramippattā viharanti.
35. Again, Udayin, I have proclaimed to my disciples the way whereby with the divine eye (dibbena cakkhunā), which is purified and surpasses the human, they see beings passing away and reappearing, inferior and superior, fair and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate. They understand how beings pass on according to their actions thus: ‘These worthy beings who were ill-conducted in body (kāyaduccaritena), speech, and mind (manoduccaritena), revilers of noble ones (ariyānaṃ upavādakā), wrong in their views (micchādiṭṭhikā), giving effect to wrong view in their actions (micchādiṭṭhikammasamādānā), on the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (paraṃ maraṇā), have reappeared in a state of deprivation (apāyaṃ), in a bad destination (duggatiṃ), in the nether world (vinipātaṃ), in hell (nirayaṃ); whereas these worthy beings who were well-conducted in body (kāyasucaritena), speech, and mind (manosucaritena), not revilers of noble ones (ariyānaṃ anupavādakā), right in their views (sammādiṭṭhikā), giving effect to right view in their actions (sammādiṭṭhikammasamādānā), on the dissolution of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (paraṃ maraṇā), have reappeared in a good destination (sugatiṃ), in the heavenly world (saggaṃ), in the world (lokaṃ)’. Thus, with the divine eye (dibbena cakkhunā) which is purified and surpasses the human, they see beings passing away and reappearing, inferior and superior, fair and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate, and they understand how beings pass on according to their actions (yathākammūpage satte pajānanti).

Just as, Udayin, if there were two houses with doors. There a man with good sight, standing in the middle, might see people entering the house, leaving, walking back and forth, and wandering about; just so, Udayin, I have proclaimed to my disciples the way whereby with the divine eye which is purified and surpasses the human, they see beings passing away and reappearing, inferior and superior, fair and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate, and they understand how beings pass on according to their actions … and thereby many disciples of mine abide having reached the consummation and perfection of direct knowledge (abhiññāvosānapāramippattā viharanti).

Seyyathāpi, udāyi, pabbatasaṅkhepe udakarahado accho vippasanno anāvilo, tattha cakkhumā puriso tīre ṭhito passeyya sippisambukampi sippikasambukampi (syā. kaṃ. ka.) sakkharakaṭhalampi macchagumbampi carantampi tiṭṭhantampi. Tassa evamassa – ‘ayaṃ kho udakarahado accho vippasanno anāvilo, tatrime sippisambukāpi sakkharakaṭhalāpi macchagumbāpi carantipi tiṭṭhantipī’ti. Evameva kho, udāyi, akkhātā mayā sāvakānaṃ paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipannā me sāvakā āsavānaṃ khayā anāsavaṃ cetovimuttiṃ paññāvimuttiṃ diṭṭheva dhamme sayaṃ abhiññā sacchikatvā upasampajja viharanti. Tatra ca pana me sāvakā bahū abhiññāvosānapāramippattā viharanti. Ayaṃ kho, udāyi, pañcamo dhammo yena mama sāvakā sakkaronti garuṃ karonti mānenti pūjenti, sakkatvā garuṃ katvā upanissāya viharanti. ‘‘Ime kho, udāyi, pañca dhammā yehi mamaṃ sāvakā sakkaronti garuṃ karonti mānenti pūjenti, sakkatvā garuṃ katvā upanissāya viharantī’’ti. Idamavoca bhagavā. Attamano sakuludāyī paribbājako bhagavato bhāsitaṃ abhinandīti. Mahāsakuludāyisuttaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ sattamaṃ.
259. Furthermore, Udayin, I have explained to my disciples the practice, whereby my disciples, through the destruction of the taints, having realized here and now, by their own direct knowledge, the taintless deliverance of mind, the deliverance by wisdom, abide in it, having attained it.

Just as, Udayin, in a mountain recess there is a lake of water, clear, limpid, and undisturbed, where a man with good eyesight standing on the bank could see oyster-shells, gravel and pebbles, and shoals of fish moving about and keeping still.

He might think: ‘There is this lake, clear, limpid, and undisturbed, and there are these oyster-shells, gravel, and pebbles, and also these shoals of fish swimming about and resting.’

Even so, Udayin, I have explained to my disciples the practice, whereby my disciples, through the destruction of the āsavas, having realized here and now, by their own direct knowledge, the taintless deliverance of mind ( cetovimutti), the deliverance by wisdom ( paññāvimutti), abide in it, having attained it.

And thereby many disciples of mine abide having reached the consummation and perfection of direct knowledge ( abhiññāvosānapāramippattā).

This, Udayin, is the fifth quality because of which my disciples honor, respect, revere, and venerate me, and live in dependence on me, honoring and respecting me.

“These, Udayin, are the five qualities because of which my disciples honor, respect, revere, and venerate me, and live in dependence on me.”

This is what the Buddha said.

The wanderer Sakuludayin was satisfied and delighted in the Blessed One’s words.

The Great Sakuludayi Sutra, the seventh, is finished.
259.Ayaṃ kho udāyi pañcamo dhammoti ekūnavīsati pabbāni paṭipadāvasena ekaṃ dhammaṃ katvā pañcamo dhammoti vutto. Yathā hi aṭṭhakanāgarasutte (ma. ni. 2.17 ādayo) ekādasa pabbāni pucchāvasena ekadhammo kato, evamidha ekūnavīsati pabbāni paṭipadāvasena eko dhammo katoti veditabbāni. Imesu ca pana ekūnavīsatiyā pabbesu paṭipāṭiyā aṭṭhasu koṭṭhāsesu vipassanāñāṇe ca āsavakkhayañāṇe ca arahattavasena abhiññāvosānapāramippattatā veditabbā, sesesu ciṇṇavasibhāvavasena. Sesaṃ sabbattha uttānamevāti. Papañcasūdaniyā majjhimanikāyaṭṭhakathāya Mahāsakuludāyisuttavaṇṇanā niṭṭhitā.
This, Udāyi, is the fifth dhamma—that is, because the nineteen topics, by way of practice, make one dhamma, it is said to be the fifth dhamma.

Just as in the Aṭṭhakanāgara Sutta (M.
N.
2.
17 ff.) eleven topics, by way of questioning, make one dhamma, so here it should be understood that the nineteen topics, by way of practice, make one dhamma.

And in these nineteen topics, in the first eight sections, through the knowledge of insight (vipassanāñāṇa) and the knowledge of the destruction of the contaminants (āsavakkhayañāṇa), the perfection of the ending in direct knowledge (abhiññāvosāna-pāramippattatā) should be understood as being in terms of being an Arhat, and in the remaining ones, as being in terms of the cultivation of mastery (ciṇṇavasibhāva).

The rest is clear throughout.

is finished.
