Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was living at Vesālī in the grove outside the city to the west.

Now on that occasion Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, had recently left this Dhamma and Discipline.

He was making this statement before the Vesālī assembly: “The recluse Gotama does not have any superhuman states, any distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones. The recluse Gotama teaches a Dhamma merely hammered out by reasoning, following his own line of inquiry as it occurs to him, and when he teaches the Dhamma to anyone, it leads him when he practises it to the complete destruction of suffering.”

Then, when it was morning, the venerable Sāriputta dressed, and taking his bowl and outer robe, went into Vesālī for alms.

Then he heard Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, making this statement before the Vesālī assembly: “The recluse Gotama does not have any superhuman states, any distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones. The recluse Gotama teaches a Dhamma merely hammered out by reasoning, following his own line of inquiry as it occurs to him, and when he teaches the Dhamma to anyone, it leads him when he practises it to the complete destruction of suffering.”

Then, having wandered for alms in Vesālī and had returned from his almsround, after his meal he went to the Blessed One, and after paying homage to him, he sat down at one side.

Seated at one side, the venerable Sāriputta said to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, Sunakkhatta, son of the Licchavis, has recently left this Dhamma and Discipline. He is making this statement before the Vesālī assembly: ‘The recluse Gotama does not have any superhuman states, any distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones. The recluse Gotama teaches a Dhamma merely hammered out by reasoning, following his own line of inquiry as it occurs to him, and when he teaches the Dhamma to anyone, it leads him when he practises it to the complete destruction of suffering.’”

146. Thus have I heard: the Mahāsīhanāda Sutta.

There, vesāli means in a city of such a name. It is said that because it was constantly expanding, it came to be called "Vesālī."

Here is the sequential story: It seems that a child was conceived in the womb of the chief queen of a king of Benares (Bārāṇasī). Knowing this, she informed the king. The king gave her the "child-protection" ceremony (gabbhaparihāraṃ). As she was being well cared for, at the time of the child's maturity, she entered the maternity home. For virtuous women, the rising from the womb occurs at dawn, and she was one of those. Therefore, at dawn, she gave birth to a piece of flesh resembling a lump of lac or hibiscus flowers (alattakapaṭalabandhujīvakapupphasadisaṃ maṃsapesiṃ). Then, thinking, "Other goddesses give birth to sons like golden statues (suvaṇṇabimbasadise putte vijāyanti), but the chief queen gives birth to a piece of flesh—there might be criticism of me in front of the king," out of fear of that criticism, she put that piece of flesh in a vessel, covered it, sealed it with the royal seal, and cast it into the current of the Ganges River (Gaṅgā). As soon as it was discarded by humans, deities arranged for its protection. They inscribed on a gold plate in vermilion (jātihiṅgulakena) the words "Offspring of the chief queen of the King of Benares (Bārāṇasī)" and attached it to the vessel. Then that vessel floated along the Ganges River current, undisturbed by fear of waves and other dangers.

At that time, a certain ascetic (tāpaso), relying on a cowherd village, was living on the bank of the Ganges. That morning, having gone down to the Ganges, he saw that vessel coming and picked it up, regarding it as discarded rags (paṃsukūlasaññāya). Then, seeing the inscribed plate and the royal seal, he unwrapped it and saw that piece of flesh. Having seen it, he thought, "It could be a fetus, as it does not have a foul, putrid odor." He took it to his hermitage and placed it in a clean place. Then, after half a month, there were two pieces of flesh. The ascetic, seeing this, placed them in an even better place. Then, after another half-month, on each piece of flesh, five swellings appeared for the hands, feet, and head. Then, after another half-month, one piece of flesh became a boy like a golden statue (suvaṇṇabimbasadiso dārako), and the other became a girl.

Affection for the children arose in that ascetic. Milk arose from his thumb. From then on, he did not obtain milk-rice. So, after eating his meal, he poured milk into the mouths of the children. Whatever entered their stomachs was visible as if it were inside a crystal vessel. Thus, they were without skin (nicchavī). Others said, "Their skin adhered to each other as if stitched together." Thus, they were known as Licchavi (Licchavī) because of their lack of skin or because their skins adhered together.

The ascetic, while raising the children, would enter a village named Ussūra for alms and return late in the day. Knowing of his activity, the cowherds said, "Venerable sir, raising children is an impediment for those who have gone forth. Give us the children; we will raise them. You do your own work." The ascetic agreed. The cowherds, on the second day, leveled the road, scattered it with flowers, raised flags and banners, and came to the hermitage playing musical instruments. The ascetic said, "These children are of great merit. Raise them with diligence. And when they are grown, arrange a marriage between them. After pleasing the king with the five products of the cow, obtain a portion of land, have a city built there, and anoint the boy as king." Having said this, he gave them the children.
They agreed, and so they brought the children away and looked after them.

As the children grew up, they played with the cowherds’ children, and in the course of quarrels they cuffed and kicked them.

They cried.

and when asked by their parents ‘ What are you crying for? ‘, they said ‘These orphans the ascetic used to nurse are hitting us too much ‘.

Then their parents said ‘ These children are bullying and hurting the other children, they must not be kept company with, they must be kept away from ’.

From that time on, that region was called Vajjī, with a measurement of one hundred yojanas.

Then the cowherds made a present to the king and took over that place.

after which they had a town built there. The boy, who was now sixteen years old, they anointed king.

and they married him to the girl. Then they made an agreement that no girls were to be brought in from outside and that no girls were to be given out from there.

From their first cohabitation two children were born, a daughter (dhītā) and a son (putto).

Sixteen times there were two born in this way.

Then, as those children gradually grew, being unable to secure the prosperity of parks, gardens, dwelling places, sites and retinue, they encircled the city three times at intervals of one gāvuta.

Because it was repeatedly made wide (visālīkata), the name Vesālī (vesālī) arose.

Therefore it was said, "In a city named Vesālī (vesālī)."

147. The Blessed One said: “Sariputta, the misguided man Sunakkhatta is angry, and his words are spoken out of anger. Sariputta, the misguided man Sunakkhatta, intending to speak dispraise, speaks only praise of the Tathāgata. For it is praise of the Tathāgata to say of him: ‘When he teaches the Dhamma to anyone, it leads him when he practises it to the complete destruction of suffering.’

“Sariputta, this misguided man Sunakkhatta will never infer of me according to Dhamma: ‘That Blessed One is an arhat, a sammāsambuddha (fully enlightened), perfect in true knowledge and conduct (vijjācaraṇa-sampanno), a sugata (sublime), a knower of worlds (lokavidū), an anuttara (incomparable) leader of persons to be tamed (purisadammasārathi), a teacher of gods and humans (satthā devamanussānam), a Buddha (enlightened), a Bhagavā (blessed).’

“And he will never infer of me according to Dhamma: ‘That Blessed One enjoys the various kinds of supernormal power (anekavihitaṃ iddhividhaṃ paccanubhoti): having been one, he becomes many; having been many, he becomes one; he appears and vanishes; he goes unhindered through a wall, through an enclosure, through a mountain as though through space; he dives in and out of the earth as though it were water; he walks on water without sinking as though it were earth; seated cross-legged, he travels in space like a bird; with his hand he touches and strokes the moon and sun so powerful and mighty; he wields power with his body even as far as the Brahma world.’

“And he will never infer of me according to Dhamma: ‘With the divine ear element (dibbāya sotadhātuyā), which is purified and surpasses the human, that Blessed One hears both kinds of sounds, the heavenly and the human, those that are far as well as near.’

“And he will never infer of me according to Dhamma: ‘That Blessed One encompasses with his own mind (cetasā ceto paricca pajānāti) the minds of other beings, other persons. He understands a mind affected by lust (sarāgaṃ) as affected by lust (sarāgaṃ cittaṃ), and a mind unaffected by lust (vītarāgaṃ) as unaffected by lust (vītarāgaṃ cittaṃ); he understands a mind affected by hate (sadosaṃ) as affected by hate (sadosaṃ cittaṃ), and a mind unaffected by hate (vītadosaṃ) as unaffected by hate (vītadosaṃ cittaṃ); he understands a mind affected by delusion (samohaṃ) as affected by delusion (samohaṃ cittaṃ), and a mind unaffected by delusion (vītamohaṃ) as unaffected by delusion (vītamohaṃ cittaṃ); he understands a contracted mind (saṃkhittaṃ) as contracted (saṃkhittaṃ cittaṃ), and a distracted mind (vikkhittaṃ) as distracted (vikkhittaṃ cittaṃ); he understands an exalted mind (mahaggataṃ) as exalted (mahaggataṃ cittaṃ), and an unexalted mind (amahaggataṃ) as unexalted (amahaggataṃ cittaṃ); he understands a surpassable mind (sauttaraṃ) as surpassable (sauttaraṃ cittaṃ), and an unsurpassed mind (anuttaraṃ) as unsurpassed (anuttaraṃ cittaṃ); he understands a concentrated mind (samāhitaṃ) as concentrated (samāhitaṃ cittaṃ), and an unconcentrated mind (asamāhitaṃ) as unconcentrated (asamāhitaṃ cittaṃ); he understands a liberated mind (vimuttaṃ) as liberated (vimuttaṃ cittaṃ), and an unliberated mind (avimuttaṃ) as unliberated (avimuttaṃ cittaṃ).’”

These are the ten elephant lineages.

The body-power of ten men is [equal to] that of one kālāvaka elephant.

“These, Sāriputta”: Sāriputta, previously, I spoke of “these ten powers of a Tathāgata.” I am referring to these.

Here, a rival might say: The ten powers of knowledge (dasa-bala-ñāṇa) are not separate; this is just a division of the knowledge of omniscience (sabbaññutañ-ñāṇa).

But it should not be seen that way. The ten powers of knowledge are one thing, the knowledge of omniscience is another. The ten powers of knowledge know only their own particular functions. The knowledge of omniscience knows that, and also what remains besides.

Among the ten powers of knowledge, the first knows only what is possible and impossible. The second knows only the result of past actions. The third knows only the divisions of actions. The fourth knows only the causes for the diversity of elements. The fifth knows only the inclinations and resolutions of beings. The sixth knows only the sharpness and dullness of the faculties. The seventh knows only the defilements etc. associated with absorption etc. The eighth knows only the continuity of past aggregates. The ninth knows only the passing away and rebirth of beings. The tenth knows only the divisions of truths.

But the knowledge of omniscience knows what can be known by these, and also knows beyond that. But it does not do all of their functions. For it cannot attain absorption after being absorption; it cannot transform after being psychic power; it cannot destroy defilements after being the path.

Moreover, the rival should be asked: “Is this ten-powered knowledge discursive or non-discursive, with or without initial application and sustained application of mind, belonging to the desire realm, form realm, or formless realm, mundane or supramundane?” If they know, they will say that seven knowledges in order are discursive. The two after that are non-discursive. They will say that knowledge of the destruction of the corruptions may be discursive, or just non-discursive, or completely non-discursive. Likewise, they will say that seven in order belong to the desire realm, the two after that belong to the form realm, and at the end one is supramundane. But they will say that the knowledge of omniscience is just discursive, belonging to the desire realm, and is mundane.

153. “These five destinations, Sāriputta, are such that one must go to them in accordance with wholesome and unwholesome actions.” Thus, destinations (gati).

Moreover, destination-destination (gatigati), arising-destination (nibbattigati), disposition-destination (ajjhāsayagati), flourishing-destination (vibhavagati), and accomplishment-destination (nipphattigati) are names for various destinations.

There, “Having passed away, I will go to that destination” (A. Ni. 4.184), and, “Whose destination is not known by gods, spirits, or humans” (Dha. Pa. 420), this is called destination-destination (gatigati).

“I do not know the destination or non-destination of these virtuous monks” (Ma. Ni. 1.508), this is called arising-destination (nibbattigati).

“Even I, Brahma, understand the destination and know the splendor” (Ma. Ni. 1.503), this is called disposition-destination (ajjhāsayagati).

“Flourishing is the destination of phenomena, extinguishment (nirvāṇa) is the destination of an Arhat” (Pari. 339), this is called flourishing-destination (vibhavagati).

“There are only two destinations, no others” (Dī. Ni. 1.258; 2.34), this is called accomplishment-destination (nipphattigati).

Among these, destination-destination (gatigati) is intended here.

In nirayo etc., niraya [hell] is so called because it is without delight (niratiatthena) and without pleasure (nirassādaṭṭhena).

Because they go horizontally (tiriyaṃ añchitāti), they are called animals (tiracchānā).

Their womb is the animal womb (tiracchānayoni).

The sphere of those who have attained the state of the departed is called the sphere of the departed (pettivisayo).

Because of the mind's elation, they are called humans (manussā).

Because they sport with the five sense qualities and their own powers, they are called gods (devā).

In nirayañcāhaṃ, sāriputtā etc., niraya [hell] means the aggregates together with space.

In tiracchānayoniṃ cātiādīsu, the same method applies.

Maggaṃ paṭipada ["path and practice"] shows the very action that is the cause of turning towards the destination that was spoken of in both ways.

Yathā ca paṭipanno ["And as he has practiced"], having made both the path by which and the practice by which one has practiced into one, he indicates it.

In apāya etc., apāya [downfall] is so called because it is devoid (apeta) of this (ayā), whether reckoned as increase (vaḍḍhisaṅkhātā) or reckoned as happiness (sukhasaṅkhātā).

Duggati [bad destination] means that suffering is the destination and refuge (dukkhassa gati paṭisaraṇanti).
Because those who do evil fall down here, hence vinipāta (downfall, ruin).

"And I know nirvāṇa": this is said to show that I know not only the course of existence, but also nirvāṇa as the escape from the course of existence.

Here, "mārga (path) and pratipadā (practice)" both refer to the Noble Path (āryamārga).

Description of the section on the destruction of defilements

In the section on the destruction of defilements, instead of "with the divine eye," it is said, "I see him." Why is that? Because there is no fixed rule. Indeed, he will see this person with the divine eye, he will know with the knowledge of the minds of others, he will know with the knowledge of omniscience.
The exclusively pleasant sensation, although verbally similar to the happiness of the realm of the gods, differs in meaning.

The happiness of the realm of the gods is not exclusively pleasant, because of the existence of the burning of passion, and so on.

Nirvāṇa happiness, however, is exclusively pleasant in every way because of the calming of all burning.

Even in an example, it is said, "As exclusively pleasant in a palace."

That is not exclusively pleasant because the burning of the path has not subsided, because of hunger, and because of being overwhelmed by thirst.

But in a forest grove, having descended into a pond, because the dirt and grime have been washed away, because the fatigue of the path has subsided, and because hunger and thirst have been removed by eating lotus roots and drinking sweet water, having changed into a bathing cloth, having put on smooth fine linen, having made a pillow of a bundle of rice chaff, having squeezed the bathing cloth and placed it on the heart, and because [one is] lying down fanned by a gentle breeze, it is exclusively pleasant in every way.

Exclusively pleasant sensation:

Just so, here is a comparison with an example: the noble path should be seen as like a pond.

The preliminary practice is like the path to the pond.

The person who is endowed with the practice is like one who has ascended the path.

The Blessed One with the divine eye is like the man with sight.

Nirvāṇa is like the forest grove.

Just as that man, having seen [another] ascend the path, knows, "Having gone by this path, this one will bathe in the pond and sit or lie down at the root of a tree in a delightful forest grove," just so the Blessed One, just by completing the practice, just by distinguishing name and form, just by analyzing the conditions, just by engaging in insight based on characteristics, knows, "Having completed this practice, having destroyed all the influxes, having attained the fruit attainment, called 'untainted liberation of mind and liberation by wisdom', this one will dwell.

Just as that man later sees that man, having bathed in that pond, having entered the forest grove, sitting or lying down, experiencing an exclusively pleasant sensation, just so the Blessed One later sees that person, having completed the practice, having cultivated the path, having realized the fruit, having reached the best resting place of cessation, directing the fruit attainment toward Nirvāṇa, experiencing an exclusively pleasant sensation.

Just so.

It seems that Sunakkhatta had the view that purification comes from arduous practices.

Then, the Blessed One, to show that "I, dwelling in a single existence, have done an arduous thing endowed with four factors; there is no one like me called an arduous practitioner; if there is purification through arduous practice, I myself would be the pure one," began this teaching.

Then, the Blessed One, showing that "This one is pleased with arduous practice, and I, dwelling in this single existence, have done an arduous thing endowed with four factors; even though one is pleased with arduous practices, he should be pleased with me, but that pleasure of his is not in me," began this teaching.
