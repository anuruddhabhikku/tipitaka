148. Evaṃ me sutaṃ – ekaṃ samayaṃ bhagavā aṅguttarāpesu viharati āpaṇaṃ nāma aṅguttarāpānaṃ nigamo. Atha kho bhagavā pubbaṇhasamayaṃ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya āpaṇaṃ piṇḍāya pāvisi. Āpaṇe piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṃ piṇḍapātapaṭikkanto yenaññataro vanasaṇḍo tenupasaṅkami divāvihārāya. Taṃ vanasaṇḍaṃ ajjhogāhetvā aññatarasmiṃ rukkhamūle divāvihāraṃ nisīdi. Āyasmāpi kho udāyī pubbaṇhasamayaṃ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya āpaṇaṃ piṇḍāya pāvisi. Āpaṇe piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṃ piṇḍapātapaṭikkanto yena so vanasaṇḍo tenupasaṅkami divāvihārāya. Taṃ vanasaṇḍaṃ ajjhogāhetvā aññatarasmiṃ rukkhamūle divāvihāraṃ nisīdi. Atha kho āyasmato udāyissa rahogatassa paṭisallīnassa evaṃ cetaso parivitakko udapādi – ‘‘bahūnaṃ bahunnaṃ (sī. syā. kaṃ. pī.) evamīdise aviññāṇakappakaraṇe vata no bhagavā dukkhadhammānaṃ apahattā, bahūnaṃ vata no bhagavā sukhadhammānaṃ upahattā; bahūnaṃ vata no bhagavā akusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ apahattā, bahūnaṃ vata no bhagavā kusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ upahattā’’ti. Atha kho āyasmā udāyī sāyanhasamayaṃ paṭisallānā vuṭṭhito yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavantaṃ abhivādetvā ekamantaṃ nisīdi .
148. Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One was living in the country of the Aṅguttarāpas at a town of theirs named Āpaṇa, a market town of the Aṅguttarāpas.

Then, when it was morning, the Blessed One dressed, and taking his bowl and outer robe, went into Āpaṇa for alms.

When he had wandered for alms in Āpaṇa and had returned from his almsround, after his meal he went to a certain grove (vanasaṇḍa) for the day’s abiding (divāvihāra).

Having entered that grove (vanasaṇḍa), he sat down at the root of a tree for the day’s abiding (divāvihāraṃ nisīdi).

The venerable Udayin also, when it was morning, dressed, and taking his bowl and outer robe, went into Āpaṇa for alms.

When he had wandered for alms in Āpaṇa and had returned from his almsround, after his meal he went to that same grove (vanasaṇḍa) for the day’s abiding (divāvihāra).

Having entered that grove (vanasaṇḍa), he sat down at the root of a tree for the day’s abiding (divāvihāraṃ nisīdi).

Then, while the venerable Udayin was alone in meditation, the following thought arose in his mind: “How many painful states ( dukkha-dhammā ) has the Blessed One rid us of! How many pleasant states ( sukha-dhammā ) has the Blessed One brought us! How many unwholesome states ( akusala-dhammā ) has the Blessed One rid us of! How many wholesome states ( kusala-dhammā ) has the Blessed One brought us!”

Then, when it was evening, the venerable Udayin rose from meditation, went to the Blessed One, and after paying homage to him, he sat down at one side.
148.Evaṃme sutanti laṭukikopamasuttaṃ. Tattha yena so vanasaṇḍoti ayampi mahāudāyitthero bhagavatā saddhiṃyeva piṇḍāya pavisitvā saddhiṃ paṭikkami. Tasmā yena so bhagavatā upasaṅkamanto vanasaṇḍo tenupasaṅkamīti veditabbo. Apahattāti apahārako. Upahattāti upahārako. Paṭisallānā vuṭṭhitoti phalasamāpattito vuṭṭhito.

Thus, "me suta" is the Laṭukikopamasutta.

There, "yena so vanasaṇḍo" means that the great Udāyitthera, having entered for alms with the Blessed One, then departed with him.

Therefore, "yena so bhagavatā upasaṅkamanto vanasaṇḍo tenupasaṅkamīti veditabbo" should be understood.

"apahattā" means apahārako.

"upahattā" means upahārako.

"paṭisallānā vuṭṭhito" means phalasamāpattito vuṭṭhito.

Then the venerable Udayin, being alone in seclusion, this thought arose in his mind: "Indeed, the Blessed One has rid us of many painful states; indeed, the Blessed One has brought us many pleasant states; indeed, the Blessed One has rid us of many unwholesome states; indeed, the Blessed One has brought us many wholesome states."

Venerable sir, formerly we used to eat in the evening, in the morning, and during the day outside the proper time.

Then there was an occasion when the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying: 'Come, monks, abandon this eating at the wrong time of day.'

Venerable sir, I was upset and sad, thinking: 'Even though faithful householders give us good food, both hard and soft, at the wrong time of day, the Blessed One tells us to abandon it, the Well-Gone One tells us to relinquish it.'

Then we, venerable sir, seeing love, respect, shame, and fear of wrongdoing in the Blessed One, abandoned that eating at the wrong time of day.

Then we, venerable sir, eat only in the evening and in the morning.

Then there was an occasion when the Blessed One addressed the monks, saying: 'Come, monks, abandon this eating at the wrong time of night.'

Venerable sir, I was upset and sad, thinking: 'Even the more delicious of these two meals, the Blessed One tells us to abandon, the Well-Gone One tells us to relinquish.’

Once, venerable sir, a certain man, having obtained a soup during the day, said thus: 'Come, put this aside, and this evening we will all enjoy it together.'

Whatever cooking there is, venerable sir, is mostly done at night, and little by day.

Then we, venerable sir, seeing love, respect, shame, and fear of wrongdoing in the Blessed One, abandoned that eating at the wrong time of night.

Once, venerable sir, monks wandering for alms in the pitch-black darkness of the night enter a charnel ground, fall into a cesspool, climb into a thorn bush, fall over a sleeping cow, meet with young ruffians who have already committed crimes and those planning to do so, and women entice them to unrighteousness.

Once, venerable sir, I was wandering for alms in the pitch-black darkness of the night.

Then a certain woman, washing a pot by the flash of lightning, saw me.

Having seen me, frightened, she exclaimed: 'Is this an earth-bound spirit or a demon?' When this was said, I, venerable sir, said to that woman: 'Sister, I am not a demon, I am a monk standing for alms.'

'It is a bhikkhu whose father is dead, a bhikkhu whose mother is dead!' Better for you, monk, that your belly be cut open with a sharp butcher's knife than to wander for alms in the pitch-black darkness of the night for the sake of your belly!'

Venerable sir, when I recollect that, it occurs to me: 'Indeed, the Blessed One has rid us of many painful states; indeed, the Blessed One has brought us many pleasant states; indeed, the Blessed One has rid us of many unwholesome states; indeed, the Blessed One has brought us many wholesome states.'

150. "Just so (evamevāti)" [means:] just so, without seeing any benefit, for no reason at all.

"Thus they said (evamāhaṃsūti)" he says, criticizing.

There, "they said (āhaṃsūti)" means they say.

"What then of this (kiṃ panimassā)" means what should be said about this trifling cause? Surely one should be like one who does not see or hear.

"Of very little (oramattakassā)" means of a very small amount.

"He over-mortifies himself (adhisallikhatevāyaṃ)" means this renunciant mortifies himself excessively, like someone grinding fresh butter, like someone sawing a lotus stalk with a saw; he makes excessive effort.

"Desiring training (sikkhākāmā)" means desiring training, like Sāriputta, Moggallāna, and so on; and they establish no confidence in them. For it occurs to them, "If these were to say, 'This is very little, let the Blessed One accept it,' would the Teacher not accept it? But without speaking thus, surrounding the Blessed One and sitting down, they obtain even greater enthusiasm, [saying,] 'Thus, Blessed One, good, Blessed One, teach, Blessed One.'" Therefore, they establish no confidence in them.

"Of those (tesaṃ)" [means:] of some of those foolish men.

"That (taṃ)" [means:] that trifling thing to be abandoned.

pavivekasukha means pleasure secluded from the multitude and also from the afflictions (gaṇatopi kilesatopi pavivittasukhaṃ).

upasamasukha means pleasure for the stilling of attachment and the like (rāgādivūpasamatthāya sukhaṃ).

sambodhasukha means pleasure for the sake of the arising of enlightenment known as the path (maggasaṅkhātassa sambodhassa nibbattanatthāya sukhaṃ).

na bhāyitabba means one should not be afraid of the acquisition of this pleasure and also of its result; rather, this should be cultivated (bhāvetabbamevetaṃ).

Here, Udayin, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states, a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the first jhāna… With the stilling of applied and sustained thought, he enters upon and abides in the second jhāna… With the fading away as well of rapture… he enters upon and abides in the third jhāna…With the abandoning of pleasure and pain… he enters upon and abides in the fourth jhāna.

This is called the bliss of renunciation (nekkhammasukhaṃ), the bliss of seclusion (pavivekasukhaṃ), the bliss of peace (upasamasukhaṃ), the bliss of enlightenment (sambodhasukhaṃ). I say of this kind of pleasure that it should be pursued (āsevitabbaṃ), that it should be developed (bhāvetabbaṃ), that it should be cultivated (bahulīkātabbaṃ), and that it should not be feared (na bhāyitabbaṃ etassa sukhassā’ti vadāmi).

Here, Udayin, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states, a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the first jhāna; Now this, I say, belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ).

And what belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ) therein? The applied thought (vitakka) and sustained thought (vicāra) that have not ceased therein, that is what belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ).

Here, Udayin, with the stilling of applied and sustained thought, a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the second jhāna… Now this, I say, also belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ).

And what belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ) therein? The rapture and pleasure (pītisukhaṃ) that have not ceased therein, that is what belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ).

Here, Udayin, with the fading away as well of rapture…a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the third jhāna…Now this, I say, also belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ).

And what belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ) therein? The equanimity and pleasure (upekkhāsukhaṃ) that have not ceased therein, that is what belongs to the perturbable (iñjitasmiṃ).

Here, Udayin, with the abandoning of pleasure and pain… a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the fourth jhāna… Now this, I say, belongs to the imperturbable (aniñjitasmiṃ).

Here, Udayin, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states, a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the first jhāna… That, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, with the stilling of applied and sustained thought, a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the second jhāna… That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, with the fading away as well of rapture…a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the third jhāna…That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, with the abandoning of pleasure and pain…a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the fourth jhāna… That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, by completely surmounting all perceptions of form (rūpasaññānaṃ), with the disappearance of perceptions of resistance (paṭighasaññānaṃ), and not attending to perceptions of diversity (nānattasaññānaṃ), aware that ‘there is infinite space,’ a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the base of infinite space (ākāsānañcāyatanaṃ). That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, by completely surmounting the base of infinite space (ākāsānañcāyatanaṃ), aware that ‘there is infinite consciousness,’ a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the base of infinite consciousness (viññāṇañcāyatanaṃ). That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, by completely surmounting the base of infinite consciousness (viññāṇañcāyatanaṃ), aware that ‘there is nothing,’ a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanaṃ). That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, by completely surmounting the base of nothingness (ākiñcaññāyatanaṃ), a bhikkhu enters upon and abides in the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṃ). That surmounts it (samatikkama). But that too, I say, is not enough (anala). Abandon it (pajahatha), I say; surmount it (samatikkamatha), I say.

And what surmounts it (samatikkama)? Here, Udayin, by completely surmounting the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṃ), a bhikkhu enters and abides in the cessation of perception and feeling (saññāvedayitanirodhaṃ). That surmounts it (samatikkama). Thus I speak of the abandoning even of the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatanassapi pahānaṃ vadāmi).

Do you see, Udayin, any fetter (saṃyojanaṃ), small or great, of whose abandoning I do not speak?” “No, venerable sir.”

Thus spoke the Lord. The venerable Udayin was satisfied and delighted in the Blessed One’s words.
