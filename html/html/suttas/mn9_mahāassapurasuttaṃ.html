415. THUS HAVE I HEARD. On one occasion the Blessed One was living in the Angan country at a town of the Aṅgas named Assapura.

There the Fortunate One addressed the monks, saying: “Bhikkhus.”

“Venerable sir,” those bhikkhus replied.

The Blessed One said this:

“Monks, the people recognize you as ascetics ( śramaṇa). And when you are asked, ‘What are you? ‘, you claim that you are ascetics. Since that is what you are designated and what you claim to be: ‘We will undertake and practise those things that make one an ascetic (śramaṇa), that make one a brāhmaṇa, so that our designations may be true and our claims genuine. and so that the services of those whose robes (cīvara), almsfood (piṇḍapāta), lodging (senāsana), and medicinal requisites (gilānappaccayabhesajjaparikkhāra) we use shall bring them great fruit and benefit, and so that our going forth (pravrajyā) shall not be in vain but fruitful and fertile.’

That is how you should train, monks.

Thus I have heard: the Mahāassapurasutta.
There, aṅgesū means that the Aṅgas are the names of country-dwelling princes; their dwelling place, even a single district, is called "Aṅga" by common parlance; in that Aṅga district.
Assapuraṃ nāma aṅgānaṃ nigamo means assapura which is a designation received by the name of a city; a single market town of the Aṅga district, that, having made [it] a pasture village, he dwells in—[this is] the meaning.
Bhagavā etadavocā means this statement: "Ascetics recognize you monks as ascetics."

Why did he say thus?
In that market town, it is said that people, faithful, serene, Buddha-revering, Dharma-revering, Saṅgha-revering, even a newly ordained śrāmaṇera, having made [him] like a senior monk of a hundred years, praise [him]; having seen the community of monks entering for alms in the forenoon, even those going to the field taking seeds, ploughs, etc., even those entering the forest taking axes, etc., having put down those implements, having swept the community of monks’ seating place, a hall or pavilion or the foot of a tree, having laid out seats, having prepared non-dusty water, having seated the community of monks, having given rice gruel, hard food, etc., having dismissed the community of monks who had finished their meal duties, then, having taken those implements, going to a field or a forest, they do their own work; even at the place of work, there is no other talk of theirs by name.
The four who are standing on the path, the four who are standing in the fruit—these eight persons are called the noble Saṅgha; they praise the qualities of the community of monks, "Possessed of such morality, such conduct, such a practice, modest, amiable, with excellent qualities.”
Having come from the work place, even having sat down at the door of the house having eaten the evening meal, even having entered their own sleeping-house, they tell of the virtues of the community of monks.
The Blessed One, having seen that respect of those people, having engaged the community of monks in the collection of alms, spoke this.

Ye dhammā samaṇakaraṇā ca brāhmaṇakaraṇā cā means the qualities that, having undertaken and fulfilled, make [one] both an ascetic who has calmed evil and a brāhmaṇa who has banished evil—this is the meaning.
“These three, monks, are the ascetic's qualities that make one an ascetic.
Which three? Undertaking the training in higher morality, undertaking the training in higher mind, undertaking the training in higher wisdom” (A.

And those also are certainly qualities that make one an ascetic.
Here, however, the teaching is extensively distributed in terms of shame, regard for blame, and so forth.
Evaṃ no ayaṃ amhāka means that here, no is merely a particle.
Evaṃ ayaṃ amhāka is the meaning.
Mahapphalā mahānisaṃsā means both are one and the same in meaning.
Avañjhā means not barren.
Saphalā means this is the meaning of that itself.
For that which has no fruit, that is called barren.
Saudrayā means with good increase, this is a synonym for fruitfulness.
Evañhi vo, bhikkhave, sikkhitabba means, monks, thus should you train.
Thus, the Blessed One, with this much, in this place, told the qualities of the qualities beginning with shame and regard for blame.
Why? For the purpose of cutting off the path of speech.
If, indeed, some recently ordained, foolish monk should say thus: "The Blessed One says, 'One should live undertaking the qualities beginning with shame and regard for blame'—what now is the benefit in undertaking those qualities and living?"—for the purpose of cutting off his path of speech.
And this is the benefit: these qualities, having undertaken and fulfilled, make [one] both an ascetic who has calmed evil and a brāhmaṇa who has banished evil, they generate the gain of the four requisites, they bring about the great fruitfulness of the givers of requisites, they make ordination not barren, fruitful, with good increase—thus he spoke the qualities.

416. Hirottappena means "that which one is ashamed of, one is ashamed; that which one should be afraid of, one is afraid" (Dha. Sa. 1331), thus, it is expanded as shame hiri and dread ottappa. Moreover, here, hiri arises from within, and ottappa arises from external factors. Hiri [is] having self as authority, ottappa [is] having the world as authority. Hiri is established in the nature of modesty, ottappa is established in the nature of fear, the detailed discussion is stated in its entirety in the Visuddhimagga. Moreover, these two qualities are called lokapāladharmā (qualities that protect the world) because they protect the world. As it is said: "Monks, these two bright qualities protect the world. Which two? Hiri and ottappa."

Ni. 2.9). These same are called devadhammā (godlike qualities) in the Jātaka. As it is said:

417. "Parisuddho no kāyasamācāro" means here, bodily conduct (kāyasamācāro) is twofold: pure (parisuddho) and impure (aparisuddho).

A monk (bhikkhu) who kills a living being (pāṇaṃ hanati), takes what is not given (adinnaṃ ādiyati), engages in misconduct in regard to sensual pleasures (kāmesu micchā carati), his bodily conduct is called impure (aparisuddho nāma); but this is prevented by the power of the path of action (kammapathavaseneva vārito).

Furthermore, whoever strikes or harasses another with the hand (pāṇinā vā), a clod of earth (leḍḍunā vā), a stick (daṇḍena vā), or a knife (satthena vā), his bodily conduct is called impure (aparisuddho nāma); and this too is rejected by the rule of training (sikkhāpadabaddheneva paṭikkhitto).

In this sutta, without speaking of both of these, the highest eradication (paramasallekho) is spoken of.

A monk (bhikkhu) who, by way of preventing crows (kākānaṃ nivāraṇavasena) from those drinking water (pānīyaṃ pivantānaṃ) in a water pot (pānīyaghaṭe vā), or those eating food (bhattaṃ bhuñjantānaṃ) in a bowl (patte vā), throws up his hand (hatthaṃ vā), a stick (daṇḍaṃ vā), or a clod of earth (leḍḍuṃ vā), his bodily conduct is impure (aparisuddho).

The opposite is called pure (parisuddho nāma).

"Sīhaseyya" here [means]: there are four postures: the posture of one enjoying sensual pleasures (kāmabhogiseyyā), the posture of a ghost (petaseyyā), the lion posture (sīhaseyyā), and the posture of the Tathāgata (tathāgataseyyā).

Therein, "kāmabhogiseyyā" [is thus]: "Monks, for the most part, beings enjoying sensual pleasures lie down on their left side." (A.Ni. 4.246) For among them, there is no one lying down on their right side for the most part.

"Petaseyyā" [is thus]: "Monks, for the most part, ghosts lie on their back." (A.Ni. 4.246) For ghosts, being deficient in flesh and blood and matted with a collection of bones, are not able to lie down on one side; they lie down only on their back.

"Sīhaseyyā" [is thus]: "Monks, for the most part, the lion, the king of beasts, lies down on its right side without inserting its tail between its thighs." (A.Ni. 4.246)

For the lion, the king of beasts, being of outstanding energy, places its two forefeet in one place, its hind feet in one place, inserts its tail between its thighs, considers the location of its forefeet, hind feet and tail, places its head on top of the two forefeet, and lies down. Even having lain down during the day, when waking up, it wakes up without being startled. But having raised its head, it considers the location of its forefeet. If any place is standing having abandoned [its place], thinking "This is not befitting of your birth, nor of your bravery," it becomes displeased and lies down right there, and does not depart for pasture. But when [they are] standing without having abandoned [their place], [thinking] "This is befitting of your birth and of your bravery," delighted and pleased, it rises, yawns the lion's yawn, shakes its mane, roars the lion's roar three times, and departs for pasture.

The posture of the fourth jhāna, however, is called "tathāgataseyyā." Here, the lion posture has come. For this is said to be the supreme posture, owing to [its] being a posture of outstanding energy.

"Pāde pāda" means the left foot on the right foot.

"Accādhāyā" means having placed [it] excessively, having slightly exceeded [the other foot]. For, when the ankle is bumping against the ankle, or the knee against the knee, pain frequently arises, the mind is not one-pointed, the posture is uncomfortable. However, just as it does not bump, so when having placed [it] having exceeded [the other foot], pain does not arise, the mind is one-pointed, the posture is comfortable; therefore, he said thus.

Furthermore (aparaṃ), monks, when a monk, with the stilling of applied and sustained thought (vitakkavicārānaṃ vūpasamā), attains inner tranquility (ajjhattaṃ sampasādanaṃ), oneness of mind (cetaso ekodibhāvaṃ), enters and dwells in the second jhāna (dutiyaṃ jhānaṃ), which is without applied thought (avitakkaṃ), without sustained thought (avicāraṃ), and has rapture and pleasure born of concentration (samādhijaṃ pītisukhaṃ).

He drenches (abhisandeti), steeps (parisandeti), saturates (paripūreti), and suffuses (parippharati) this very body (kāyaṃ) with the rapture and happiness born of concentration (samādhijena pītisukhena), so that there is no part of his entire body (sabbāvato kāyassa) which is not suffused by this rapture and happiness (samādhijena pītisukhena apphuṭaṃ hoti).

Just as (seyyathāpi), monks, there were a lake (udakarahado) fed by a spring (ubbhiddodako).

It would have no inflow (āyamukhaṃ) of water (udakassa) from the east (puratthimāya disāya), west (pacchimāya disāya), north (uttarāya disāya), or south (dakkhiṇāya disāya), and the deva (devo) would not from time to time (kālena kālaṃ) send moderate showers (sammādhāraṃ anuppaveccheyya).

Then a current of cool water (sītā vāridhārā), welling up (ubbhijjitvā) from that very lake (tamhāva udakarahadā), would drench (abhisandeyya), steep (parisandeyya), saturate (paripūreyya) and suffuse (paripphareyya) that very lake (tameva udakarahadaṃ) with cool water (sītena vārinā), so that there would be no part of that entire lake (sabbāvato udakarahadassa) which is not suffused with the cool water (sītena vārinā apphuṭaṃ assa).

Even so (evameva kho), monks, a monk (bhikkhu) drenches (abhisandeti), steeps (parisandeti), saturates (paripūreti), and suffuses (parippharati) this very body (imameva kāyaṃ) with the rapture and happiness born of concentration (samādhijena pītisukhena), so that there is no part of his entire body (sabbāvato kāyassa) which is not suffused by this rapture and happiness (samādhijena pītisukhena apphuṭaṃ hoti).

Āyamukha means āgamanamagga.

Devo is megha.

Kālenakāla means at various times, either every half month or every ten days; this is the meaning.

Dhāra means rain (vuṭṭhiṃ).

Nānuppaveccheyyā means he should not allow to enter, he should not cause to rain; this is the meaning.

Sītā vāridhārā ubbhijjitvā means the cold water, having burst forth, fills that water-lake.

Water that rises from below stirs up the water as it bursts forth again and again.

Water entering from the four directions stirs up the water with old leaves, grass, wood, sticks, and so on.

Rainwater stirs up the water with streams, falling drops, and bubbles.

But rather, having settled down, like water arising as if created by psychic power, it pervades this region; there is no [place] that it does not pervade.

Therefore, it is not called aphuṭokāsa.

There, the material body is like the lake, the bliss of the second dhyāna is like the water.

The rest should be understood in the same way as previously.

430. In the simile of the happiness of the fourth jhāna, "with a pure and cleansed mind" ( parisuddhena cetasā pariyodātenā), parisuddha should be understood in the sense of being free from defilements (nirupakkilesaṭṭhena), and pariyodāta in the sense of being radiant (pabhassaraṭṭhena).

"With a clean cloth" (odātena vatthenā) was said for the sake of suffusion by heat (utupharaṇatthaṃ). For there is no suffusion by heat with a soiled cloth (kiliṭṭhavatthena); with a clean cloth that has just been washed, the suffusion by heat is strong (taṅkhaṇadhotaparisuddhena utupharaṇaṃ balavaṃ hoti). For in this simile, the material body (karajakāyo) is like the cloth (vatthaṃ viya), and the happiness of the fourth jhāna (catutthajjhānasukhaṃ) is like the suffusion by heat (utupharaṇaṃ viya).

Therefore, just as when a man has bathed well (sunhātassa purisassa) and is sitting covered from the head down by a clean cloth (parisuddhaṃ vatthaṃ sasīsaṃ pārupitvā nisinnassa), the heat (utu) from his body suffuses the entire cloth (sabbameva vatthaṃ pharati), and there is no part of the cloth that is not suffused (na koci vatthassa aphuṭokāso hoti), similarly, there is no part of the bhikkhu's material body that is not suffused by the happiness of the fourth jhāna (evaṃ catutthajjhānasukhena bhikkhuno karajakāyassa na koci okāso aphuṭo hoti). Thus, the meaning should be understood here.

Or, the mind of the fourth jhāna itself is like the cloth (vatthaṃ viya), and the matter (rūpaṃ) arisen from it is like the suffusion by heat (utupharaṇaṃ viya). Just as even without the body touching the clean cloth anywhere (kathaci odātavatthe kāyaṃ apphusantepi), the body is entirely suffused by the heat arisen from it (taṃsamuṭṭhānena utunā sabbatthakameva kāyo phuṭṭho hoti), similarly, the bhikkhu's material body is entirely suffused by the subtle matter (sukhumarūpena) arisen from the fourth jhāna (evaṃ catutthajjhānasamuṭṭhitena sukhumarūpena sabbatthakameva bhikkhuno karajakāyo phuṭo hoti). Thus, the meaning should be understood here.

433. “When his mind is thus concentrated (samāhite), purified (parisuddhe), cleansed (pariyodāte), unblemished (anaṅgaṇe), rid of defilement (vigatūpakkilese), malleable (mudubhūte), wieldy (kammaniye), steady (ṭhite), and attained to imperturbability (āneñjappatte), he directs his mind to the knowledge of the destruction of the taints (āsavānaṃ khayañāṇāya cittaṃ abhininnāmeti).

He understands as it really is: ‘This is suffering (idaṃ dukkha)’; ‘This is the origin of suffering (ayaṃ dukkhasamudayo)’; ‘This is the cessation of suffering (ayaṃ dukkhanirodho)’; ‘This is the way leading to the cessation of suffering (ayaṃ dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā)’.

He understands as it really is: ‘These are the taints (ime āsavā)’; ‘This is the origin of the taints (ayaṃ āsavasamudayo)’; ‘This is the cessation of the taints (ayaṃ āsavanirodho)’; ‘This is the way leading to the cessation of the taints (ayaṃ āsavanirodhagāminī paṭipadā)’.

When he knows and sees thus, his mind is liberated from the taint of sensual desire (kāmāsavāpi cittaṃ vimuccati), from the taint of being (bhavāsavāpi cittaṃ vimuccati), and from the taint of ignorance (avijjāsavāpi cittaṃ vimuccati).

When it is liberated, there comes the knowledge: ‘It’s liberated (vimuttasmiṃ vimuttamiti ñāṇaṃ hoti).’ He understands: ‘Birth is destroyed (khīṇā jāti), the spiritual life has been lived (vusitaṃ brahmacariyaṃ), what had to be done has been done (kataṃ karaṇīyaṃ), there is no more coming to any state of being (nāparaṃ itthattāyā)’.

“Just as if there were a lake in a mountain recess (pabbatasaṅkhepe udakarahado), clear (acchho), limpid (vippasanno), and undisturbed (anāvilo), so that a man with good sight (cakkhumā puriso) standing on the bank (tīre ṭhito) could see shells (sippisambukampi), gravel and pebbles (sakkharakathalampi), and shoals of fish (macchagumbampi), moving about (carantampi) and keeping still (tiṭṭhantampi).

He would think to himself: ‘This is a lake (ayaṃ kho udakarahado) with clear water (acchho), limpid (vippasanno) and unsullied (anāvilo). There are these shells (sippisambukāpi), these gravel and pebbles (sakkharakathalāpi), these shoals of fish (macchagumbāpi) moving about (carantipi) and keeping still (tiṭṭhantipīti).’

So too, a bhikkhu understands as it actually is: ‘This is suffering (idaṃ dukkha)’…pe…He understands: ‘Birth is destroyed (nāparaṃ itthattāyāti pajānāti).’

Macchānaṃ gumbā ghaṭāti macchagumbaṃ. Tiṭṭhantampi carantampīti ettha sakkharakathalaṃ tiṭṭhatiyeva, itarāni carantipi tiṭṭhantipi. Yathā pana antarantarā ṭhitāsupi nisinnāsupi vijjamānāsupi, ‘‘etā gāvo carantī’’ti carantiyo upādāya itarāpi carantīti vuccanti. Evaṃ tiṭṭhantameva sakkharakathalaṃ upādāya itarampi dvayaṃ tiṭṭhantanti vuttaṃ. Itarañca dvayaṃ carantaṃ upādāya sakkharakathalampi carantanti vuttaṃ. Tattha cakkhumato purisassa tīre ṭhatvā passato sippisambukādīnaṃ vibhūtakālo viya āsavānaṃ khayāya cittaṃ nīharitvā nisinnassa bhikkhuno catunnaṃ saccānaṃ vibhūtakālo daṭṭhabbo.
433. Regarding the simile of the knowledge of the destruction of the āsavas, pabbatasaṅkhepe means on the summit of the mountain.

anāvilo means nikkaddamo (free from mud).

sippiyo ca sambukā ca means sippisambukaṃ (shells and snails).

sakkharā ca kathalā ca means sakkharakathalaṃ (gravel and potsherds).

macchānaṃ gumbā ghaṭāti means macchagumbaṃ (a cluster of fish).

tiṭṭhantampi carantampī means here, sakkharakathalaṃ only stands, the others both move and stand.

Just as, however, even though some are standing, some are sitting, and some are moving, taking those which are moving, the others also are said to be moving.

Likewise, taking just the standing sakkharakathalaṃ, the other pair also is said to be standing.

And taking the moving pair, the sakkharakathalaṃ is also said to be moving.

There, just as the time when shells and snails etc. become clear to a man with eyes standing on the bank, so the time when the four truths become clear to a bhikkhu sitting having drawn his mind to the destruction of the āsavas should be seen.

434. “Thus, monks, a bhikkhu is called a recluse (śramaṇa), a Brahmin (brāhmaṇa), one who has been washed (nhātaka), one who has attained to knowledge (vedagū), a holy scholar (sottiyo), a noble one (āriya), and an arahant (arahaṃ).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu a recluse (śramaṇa)? He has quieted down evil unwholesome states (akusalā dhammā) that defile (saṃkilesikā), bring renewal of being (ponobbhavikā), give trouble (sadarā), ripen in suffering (dukkhavipākā), and lead to future birth, ageing, and death (jātijarāmaraṇiyā). That is how, monks, a bhikkhu is a recluse (śramaṇa).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu a Brahmin (brāhmaṇa)? He has banished evil unwholesome states that defile, bring renewal of being, give trouble, ripen in suffering, and lead to future birth, ageing, and death. That is how, monks, a bhikkhu is a Brahmin (brāhmaṇa).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu one who has been washed (nhātaka)? He has washed off evil unwholesome states that defile, bring renewal of being, give trouble, ripen in suffering, and lead to future birth, ageing, and death. That is how, monks, a bhikkhu is one who has been washed (nhātaka).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu one who has attained to knowledge (vedagū)? He has known evil unwholesome states that defile, bring renewal of being, give trouble, ripen in suffering, and lead to future birth, ageing, and death. That is how, monks, a bhikkhu is one who has attained to knowledge (vedagū).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu a holy scholar (sottiyo)? The evil unwholesome states that defile, bring renewal of being, give trouble, ripen in suffering, and lead to future birth, ageing, and death, have streamed away from him. That is how a bhikkhu is a holy scholar (sottiyo).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu a noble one (āriya)? Evil unwholesome states that defile, bring renewal of being, give trouble, ripen in suffering, and lead to future birth, ageing, and death, are far away from him. That is how a bhikkhu is a noble one (āriya).

“And how, monks, is a bhikkhu an arahant (arahaṃ)? Evil unwholesome states that defile, bring renewal of being, give trouble, ripen in suffering, and lead to future birth, ageing, and death, are far away from him. That is how a bhikkhu is an arahant (arahaṃ).”

The Blessed One said this. Gratified, the monks delighted in the Blessed One’s words.

The Mahāassapura Sutta, the ninth, is finished.

Tattha evaṃ kho, bhikkhave, bhikkhu samaṇohotītiādīsu, bhikkhave, evaṃ bhikkhu samitapāpattā samaṇo hoti. Bāhitapāpattā brāhmaṇo hoti. Nhātakilesattā nhātako hoti, dhotakilesattāti attho. Catumaggañāṇasaṅkhātehi vedehi akusaladhammānaṃ gatattā vedagū hoti, viditattāti attho. Teneva viditāssa hontītiādimāha. Kilesānaṃ sutattā sottiyo hoti, nissutattā apahatattāti attho. Kilesānaṃ ārakattā ariyo hoti, hatattāti attho. Tehi ārakattā arahaṃ hoti, dūrībhūtattāti attho. Sesaṃ sabattha pākaṭamevāti. Papañcasūdaniyā majjhimanikāyaṭṭhakathāya Mahāassapurasuttavaṇṇanā niṭṭhitā.
Now, while taking up the name of the khīṇāsava (one who has destroyed the āsavas), with seven aspects, with the liṅga, and with the qualities, he says, “This is called, O monks, a bhikkhu, a samaṇa,” etc.

There, in “It is in this way, O monks, that a bhikkhu is a samaṇa,” etc., O monks, in this way a bhikkhu, because he has calmed evil (samita-pāpattā), is a samaṇa (ascetic). Because he has warded off evil (bāhita-pāpattā), he is a brāhmaṇa (Brahmin). Because he has washed away defilements (nhāta-kilesattā), he is a nhātako (one who has bathed), that is, the meaning is, because he has cleansed away defilements. Because he has gone to the Vedas (vedagū)—in the sense of the four paths' knowledge—gone to unwholesome things, he is a vedagū, that is, the meaning is, because he has known it. Just because of that, he says, “They are known to him,” etc. Because he has heard the defilements (kilesānaṃ sutattā), he is a sottiyo (one who has heard), that is, the meaning is, because he has completely heard, because he has warded off. Because he is far from the defilements (kilesānaṃ ārakattā), he is an ariya (noble one), that is, the meaning is, because he has destroyed. Because he is far from them (tehi ārakattā), he is an arahaṃ (worthy one), that is, the meaning is, because he has become distant. The rest is clear everywhere.
