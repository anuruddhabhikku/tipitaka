Thus have I heard. On one occasion the Blessed One (bhagavā) was staying at Sāvatthī in Jeta's Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Park (Jetavane Anāthapiṇḍikassa ārāme).

There the Blessed One (bhagavā) addressed the monks (bhikkhū), saying: "Monks (bhikkhavo)!"

"Venerable sir (bhadante)," those monks (bhikkhū) replied to the Blessed One (bhagavato).

The Blessed One (bhagavā) said this:

"Suppose, monks (bhikkhave), there were two houses with doors sandhidvārā (ka.), and a man with good sight standing in the middle could see people entering and leaving, walking back and forth, and wandering about. In the same way, monks (bhikkhave), with the divine eye (dibbena cakkhunā) that is purified and surpasses the human, I see beings passing away and being reborn, inferior and superior, beautiful and ugly, fortunate and unfortunate. I understand beings passing on according to their actions: ‘These worthy beings (sattā) were endowed with good conduct of body (kāyasucaritena), good conduct of speech (vacīsucaritena), and good conduct of mind (manosucaritena); they were not revilers of the noble ones (ariyānaṃ anupavādakā), held right view (sammādiṭṭhikā), and undertook actions governed by right view (sammādiṭṭhikammasamādānā). With the breakup of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (paraṃ maraṇā), they have reappeared in a good destination (sugatiṃ), a heavenly world (saggaṃ lokaṃ).'

"Or these worthy beings (sattā) were endowed with good conduct of body (kāyasucaritena), good conduct of speech (vacīsucaritena), and good conduct of mind (manosucaritena); they were not revilers of the noble ones (ariyānaṃ anupavādakā), held right view (sammādiṭṭhikā), and undertook actions governed by right view (sammādiṭṭhikammasamādānā). With the breakup of the body (kāyassa bhedā), after death (paraṃ maraṇā), they have reappeared among human beings (manussesu upapannā)."

262. "Having been born in hell": In some instances, the Blessed One brings his teaching to a close with the heavenly realm, beginning with hell, and in other instances, he brings his teaching to a close with hell, beginning with the heavenly realm.

If he wishes to expound on the prosperity of the heavenly realm, he speaks in brief about the suffering of hell, the suffering of the animal realm, the suffering of the realm of the departed spirits, and the prosperity of the human realm, but he expounds at length only on the prosperity of the heavenly realm.

If he wishes to expound on the suffering of hell, he speaks in brief about prosperity in the heavenly and human realms, and the suffering in the animal realm and the realm of departed spirits, but he expounds at length only on the suffering of hell.

So, in this sutta, he wishes to expound on the suffering of hell, therefore, beginning with the heavenly realm, he brings his teaching to a close with hell.

After speaking in brief about prosperity in the heavenly and human realms, and the sufferings in the animal realm and the realm of departed spirits, he speaks at length about the suffering of hell, [saying,] "Then, bhikkhus, the hell-wardens," and so on.

There, some theras say, "There are no so-called 'hell-wardens'; karma itself, like a machine, is the agent that causes [suffering]."

Their [view] is refuted in the Abhidhamma (in the Kathā-vatthu, 866) with the line, "Are there hell-wardens in hell? Yes. And are there judicial agents?"

Just as in the human realm there are agents who do actions, so too in hell there are hell-wardens.

"Yamassa rañño": Yama, the king, is a king of departed spirits who is in a celestial mansion. At one time he experiences the prosperity of a divine palace, divine wish-fulfilling trees, divine parks, divine theatrical performances, and so on; at another time, he [experiences] the result of karma. He is a righteous king.

And he is not alone; but at the four gates there are four people.

"Nāddasa": He says this in reference to the absence of any divine messenger sent to his presence.

Then Yama, knowing that "This one does not understand the meaning of what has been said," wishing to make him understand, says, "Ambho," and so on.

"Nirayapālā yamassa rañño nāddasa ambho":

"Jātidhammo": "Jāti-sabhāvo, subject to birth, not freed from birth, 'birth' exists within me."

And so the same method [applies] in the following [phrases,] "jarādhammo," and so on.

Why is this hell called Avīci (Avici hell)?

The name vīci means interval (antaraṃ), and there is no interval there, whether of fire flames (aggijālānaṃ), or of beings (sattānaṃ), or of suffering (dukkha).

Therefore, it is called Avīci (Avici hell).

Indeed, flames arise from its eastern wall (puratthimabhittito jālā uṭṭhitā), continuously spreading (saṃsibbamānā) and, after going a hundred yojanas (yojanasataṃ gantvā), piercing the western wall (pacchimabhittiṃ vinivijjhitvā), they go a hundred yojanas beyond (parato yojanasataṃ gacchati).

The same method (nayo) applies to the other directions as well (sesadisāsupi eseva nayo).

In the middle of these six flame-masses (imesaṃ channaṃ jālānaṃ majjhe) Devadatta is reborn (nibbatto devadatto), and his individual existence measures a hundred yojanas (tassa yojanasatappamāṇo attabhāvo). His two feet, up to the ankles (dve pādā yāva gopphakā), are immersed in the iron ground (lohapathaviṃ paviṭṭhā), his two hands, up to the wrists (dve hatthā yāva maṇibandhā), are immersed in the iron walls (lohabhittiyo paviṭṭhā), his head, up to the eyebrows (sīsaṃ yāva bhamukaṭṭhito), is immersed in the iron roof (lohachadane paviṭṭhaṃ), and an iron stake going downwards (adhobhāgena ekaṃ lohasūlaṃ) having entered, pierces through his body (kāyaṃ vinivijjhantaṃ), is immersed in the roof (chadane paviṭṭhaṃ). A stake emerging from the eastern wall (pācīnabhittito nikkhantasūlaṃ) pierces through his heart (hadayaṃ vinivijjhitvā) and is immersed in the western wall (pacchimabhittiṃ paviṭṭhaṃ), a stake emerging from the northern wall (uttarabhittito nikkhantasūlaṃ) pierces through his ribs (phāsukā vinivijjhitvā) and is immersed in the southern wall (dakkhiṇabhittiṃ paviṭṭhaṃ).

Because of offending the unmoving Tathāgata (niccale tathāgatamhi aparaddhattā), having become unmoving, he suffers (niccalova hutvā paccatīti). Due to the similarity of the karma (kammasarikkhatāya), he is born thus (ediso jāto).

Thus, because of the continuous nature of the flames (evaṃ jālānaṃ nirantaratāya), it is called Avīci (Avici hell).

Within, in that place measuring a hundred yojanas (abbhantare panassa yojanasatike ṭhāne), beings are continuous, like being pressed in a bamboo tube filled with flour (nāḷiyaṃ koṭṭetvā pūritapiṭṭhaṃ viya sattā nirantarā). It should not be said, "In this place there is a being (imasmiṃ ṭhāne satto atthi), in this place there is not (imasmiṃ natthī)." There is no end to those going, standing, sitting, or lying down (gacchantānaṃ ṭhitānaṃ nisinnānaṃ nipannānaṃ anto natthi); whether going, standing, sitting, or lying down (gacchante vā ṭhite vā nisinne vā nipanne vā), they do not obstruct each other (aññamaññaṃ na bādhanti).

Thus, because of the continuous nature of the beings (evaṃ sattānaṃ nirantaratāya), it is Avīci (Avici hell).

At the body door (kāyadvāre) six minds accompanied by equanimity arise (cha upekkhāsahagatāni cittāni uppajjanti), and one accompanied by suffering (ekaṃ dukkhasahagataṃ).

Even so, just as, having placed six drops of honey on the tip of the tongue (yathā jivhagge cha madhubindūni ṭhapetvā), if an iron drop is placed on one of them (ekasmiṃ tambalohabindumhi ṭhapite), due to the power of intense burning (anudahanabalavatāya), only that one is known (tadeva paññāyati), the others are nominal (itarāni abbohārikāni honti), so too, due to the power of intense burning (evaṃ anudahanabalavatāya), only suffering is continuous there (dukkham evettha nirantaraṃ), the others are nominal (itarāni abbohārikānīti).

Thus, because of the continuous nature of suffering (evaṃ dukkhassa nirantaratāya), it is Avīci (Avici hell).
