THUS HAVE I HEARD. On one occasion the venerable Mahā Moggallāna was living in the Bhagga country at Susumāragira in the Bhesakaḷā Grove, the Deer Park. There the venerable Mahā Moggallāna addressed the bhikkhus thus: “Friends, bhikkhus.” “Yes, friend,” those bhikkhus replied to the venerable Mahā Moggallāna. The venerable Mahā Moggallāna said this:

“Friends, though a bhikkhu makes this invitation: ‘Let the venerable ones admonish me, I am open to admonishment by the venerable ones,’ yet if he is difficult to admonish, possessing qualities that make him difficult to admonish, impatient, and not receiving instruction respectfully, then his companions in the holy life do not consider him worthy of being spoken to, do not consider him worthy of being instructed, and do not consider him a person in whom confidence should be placed.

“And what, friends, are the qualities that make one difficult to admonish? Here, friends, a bhikkhu is one of evil desires, overcome by evil desires. This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.

“Again, friends, a bhikkhu lauds himself and disparages others (attukkaṃsako hoti paravambhī). This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.

“Again, friends, a bhikkhu is angry and overcome by anger (kodhano hoti kodhābhibhūto). This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.

“Again, friends, a bhikkhu is angry and harbors resentment because of anger (kodhahetu upanāhī). This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.

“Again, friends, a bhikkhu is angry and stubborn because of anger (kodhahetu abhisaṅgī). This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.

“Again, friends, a bhikkhu is angry, and utters words bordering on anger (kodhasāmantā vācaṃ nicchāretā). This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.

“Again, friends, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he counter-reproves the reprover. This, friends, is a quality that makes a bhikkhu difficult to admonish.
Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he denigrates the reprover. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he counter-reproves the reprover. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he prevaricates, leads the talk aside, and shows anger, hate, and bitterness. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he is not pleased with giving explanations. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is contemptuous and domineering. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is envious and avaricious. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is fraudulent and deceitful. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu is obstinate and arrogant. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish.

Again, a bhikkhu adheres to his own views, tenaciously clinging to them and relinquishing them with difficulty. This is a quality that makes him difficult to admonish. These, friends, are called the qualities that make him difficult to admonish (dovacassakaraṇā dhammā).

“Friends, though a bhikkhu does not ask thus: ‘Let the venerable ones admonish me; I need to be admonished by the venerable ones,’ yet if he is easy to admonish (suvaco), possesses qualities that make him easy to admonish (sovacassakaraṇā dhammā), if he is patient and takes instruction rightly,’ then his companions in the holy life think that he should be admonished and instructed, and they think of him as a person to be trusted.

“And what, friends, are the qualities that make one easy to admonish? Here, a bhikkhu has no evil wishes (na pāpiccho hoti), and is not dominated by evil wishes. The fact that a bhikkhu has no evil wishes and is not dominated by evil wishes – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu does not laud himself (anattukkaṃsako hoti), nor disparage others (aparavambhī). The fact that a bhikkhu does not laud himself, nor disparage others – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is not angry (na kodhano hoti), nor allows anger to overcome him (na kodhābhibhūto). The fact that a bhikkhu is not angry, nor allows anger to overcome him – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is not angry, or revengeful because of anger (na kodhahetu upanāhī). The fact that a bhikkhu is not angry, or revengeful because of anger – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is not angry, or stubborn because of anger (na kodhahetu abhisaṅgī). The fact that a bhikkhu is not angry, or stubborn because of anger – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is not angry, and he does not utter words bordering on anger (na kodhasāmantā vācaṃ nicchāretā). The fact that a bhikkhu is not angry, and he does not utter words bordering on anger – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not resist the reprover (codakaṃ nappaṭippharati). The fact that a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not resist the reprover– this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not denigrate the reprover (codakaṃ na apasādeti). The fact that a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not denigrate the reprover – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not counter-reprove the reprover (codakassa na paccāropeti). The fact that a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not counter-reprove the reprover – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not prevaricate, lead the talk aside, and show anger, hate, and bitterness (na aññenaññaṃ paṭicarati, na bahiddhā kathaṃ apanāmeti, na kopañca dosañca appaccayañca pātukaroti). The fact that a bhikkhu is reproved, and he does not prevaricate, lead the talk aside, and show anger, hate, and bitterness – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is reproved, and he accounts for his conduct (apadāne sampāyati). The fact that a bhikkhu is reproved, and he accounts for his conduct – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is not contemptuous and domineering (amakkhī hoti apaḷāsī). The fact that a bhikkhu is not contemptuous and domineering – this is a quality that makes him easy to admonish.

“Again, a bhikkhu is without envy and is unselfish (anissukī hoti amaccharī).
Again, friends, a bhikṣu is not fraudulent or deceitful. This is a quality, friends, that makes a bhikṣu easy to admonish.

Again, friends, a bhikṣu is not obstinate or arrogant. This is a quality, friends, that makes a bhikṣu easy to admonish.

Again, friends, a bhikṣu does not adhere to his own views or hold on to them tenaciously, and he relinquishes them easily; this is a quality, friends, that makes him easy to admonish.

These, friends, are called the qualities that make him easy to admonish.

184. "Furthermore, a monk should review himself thus: 'Am I one with evil wishes, dominated by evil wishes?' If, when he reviews himself, he knows: 'I am one with evil wishes, dominated by evil wishes,' then he should make an effort to abandon those evil unwholesome states. But if, when he reviews himself, he knows: 'I have no evil wishes, I am not dominated by evil wishes,’ then he can abide happy and glad, training day and night in wholesome states.

"Again, a monk should review himself thus: 'Am I one who praises myself and disparages others?' If, when he reviews himself, he knows: 'I am one who praises myself and disparages others,' then he should make an effort to abandon those evil unwholesome states. But if, when he reviews himself, he knows: 'I do not praise myself, nor do I disparage others,' then he can abide happy and glad, training day and night in wholesome states.

If, when he reviews himself, he knows: 'I am irascible and overcome by anger ( krodha ),' then he should make an effort to abandon those evil unwholesome states. But if, when he reviews himself, he knows: 'I am not irascible and not overcome by anger ( krodha ),' then he can abide happy and glad, training day and night in wholesome states.

184. Paccavekkhitabba means that which should be reviewed. Ahorattānusikkhinā means one who trains day and night, one who trains in wholesome qualities both night and day, meaning that one should only generate joy and gladness.

Acche vā udakapatte means in a clear water bowl. Mukhanimitta means the reflection of the face. Raja means adventitious dust. Aṅgaṇa means a birthmark, a mole, or a pimple there. Sabbepime pāpake akusale dhamme pahīne means by this he described the abandonment of all. How? Because for one generating reflection, thinking, "These unwholesome qualities are not suitable for one gone forth," paṭisaṅkhānappahānaṃ (abandonment by reflection) is described. For one making moral discipline the foundation, beginning the preliminary work on the kasiṇa, and generating the eight attainments, vikkhambhanappahānaṃ (abandonment by suppression) is described. For one making attainment the foundation and developing insight, tadaṅgappahānaṃ (momentary abandonment) is described. For one developing insight and cultivating the path, samucchedappahānaṃ (abandonment by cutting off) is described. When the fruition comes, paṭippassaddhippahānaṃ (abandonment by tranquility) [is described], when nirvāṇa comes, nissaraṇappahāna (abandonment by escape) [is described]. Thus, in this sūtra, the abandonment of all is described.

Acche vā udakapatte, mukhanimitta, raja, aṅgaṇa, sabbepime pāpake akusale dhamme pahīne, paṭisaṅkhānappahānaṃ, vikkhambhanappahānaṃ, tadaṅgappahānaṃ, samucchedappahānaṃ, paṭippassaddhippahānaṃ, nissaraṇappahāna.

This sūtra is called the bhikkhupātimokkhaṃ, so the elders say. This should be reviewed three times a day. In the morning, having entered one's dwelling and sat down, one should review, "Are these many defilements present in me, or are they not?" If one sees that they are present, one should strive for their abandonment. If one does not see them, one should be glad, thinking, "I have gone forth well." Having done the meal duty, having sat down in one's night dwelling or day dwelling, one should review. In the evening, having sat down in one's dwelling, one should review. If one is unable to [do it] three times, one should review [it] two times. If one is unable to [do it] two times, one should necessarily review [it] one time; it is not proper not to review, so they say.

The rest is clear throughout.
